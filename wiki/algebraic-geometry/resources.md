# Resources

## The one book the exam is drawn from

The exercises an oral examiner reaches for are [@Har10a]'s, and the chapter and section numbers they quote are that book's. The text itself is famously hard to learn from: the definitions arrive in their final generality with no worked motivation, and a reader who has not already seen the object being defined has nothing to hold onto.
The standard use of it is therefore to read someone else first and come back here for the problems.

Read II.1--II.8, III.1--III.5 with III.9, and IV.1--IV.3, plus the statement of Serre duality in III.6. Chapter I is the classical theory in a compressed form and is better learned elsewhere.
Chapters IV and V are where the machinery is finally spent, and an examiner who has read your file knows it, so reaching them matters more than completing the exercises in order.

## The first pass

[@Gat21] is the notes to read before the first chapter of anything else.
Every construction arrives with the example that motivated it, the proofs are complete, and the whole of the classical theory and the scheme-theoretic rewrite fit into one document.
A student who has taken the course and needs the subject back in a fortnight should read these and nothing else.

[@Vak25] is the book to open when a definition in [@Har10a] refuses to make sense.
It says out loud what the terse version assumes: why the sheaf axiom is stated for covers rather than pairs, why $\Proj$ needs a graded ring, what the affine communication lemma buys and how it removes most of the "check this on an affine cover" drudgery from Chapter II. It is long enough that reading it cover to cover is a year's project, so use it by section, against whatever is currently opaque.

[@Mil08a] is the short alternative, and it is the right choice when the exam is a week away and the gap is classical: dimension, tangent spaces, smoothness, and the finiteness theorems, without the scheme apparatus.

## Before the schemes

Orals in this subject begin with a variety far more often than with a scheme, and the classical picture is what a follow-up question probes.
[@Har10b] is a catalogue of the concrete objects an examiner asks you to produce: rational normal curves, Veronese and Segre embeddings, determinantal varieties, secant and tangent varieties, Grassmannians.
It proves little and shows a great deal, which is exactly the balance wanted when the question is "give me an example of".

[@Sha13] is the systematic version of the same material, with real proofs, and it is the book to use if the classical theory was never properly learned rather than merely forgotten.

[@Mum94] is the shortest honest account of why schemes look the way they do.
It is worth two evenings for the geometry of $\Spec$ alone --- generic points, the picture of $\Spec \ZZ[x]$, and what a nilpotent is doing to the space.
[@EH00] extends that picture, and its long sequence of worked $\Spec$ and $\Proj$ computations answers the "what does this scheme actually look like" question that a diagram-chasing treatment leaves open.

## The commutative algebra underneath

Most of what looks like a gap in algebraic geometry is a gap in commutative algebra, and an examiner will find it by asking why a hypothesis is needed.
[@AM18] is the whole of what is assumed, in a form small enough to reread: localization, integral dependence, the Nullstellensatz, dimension theory, completion.
Work its exercises rather than reading it.

[@Eis95] is the reference for everything [@AM18] omits and [@Har10a] silently uses --- regular sequences, depth, Cohen--Macaulay and regular rings, flatness, the Koszul complex.
Consult it by index; it is not a book to read through.

## By topic

For toric varieties, [@Ful93] is still the fastest route from a fan to an answer, and it is the reason the toric chapter here is worth its space: it makes divisors, cohomology, and resolution of singularities into finite combinatorics.

For divisors and positivity, [@Laz04] is where the definitions of ample, very ample, nef and big are separated carefully enough to survive a follow-up question, and where the standard counterexamples separating them live.

For birational geometry --- minimal models, the cone theorem, singularities of pairs --- [@KM98] is the standard reference, and the relevant part for an exam is Chapters 1 and 2.

The coherent-sheaf formalism that Chapter III uses was set up in [@Ser55], and reading its first sections is a quick way to see the cohomology as something constructed for a purpose rather than imposed.

## Problems and solutions

[@Har07] is the closest thing to the actual exam: a transcribed list of questions asked in past orals, with the follow-ups attached and the examiner named.
Read it early rather than late.
It is the best available evidence for what "define a scheme" is actually the opening of, and its questions are the shape the cards here are built for.

[@Vak07] is the best problem source outside [@Har10a] itself, because the problems are graded by difficulty and chosen to build practical facility rather than to test the reader.
[@Bor12] assigns a curated subset of [@Har10a]'s exercises in the order a course actually uses them, which solves the "which exercises" problem, and posts written solutions to the first several.
[@PA12] is a complete written solution set for a scheme-theory course, useful when a computation with $\Spec$ or a Čech complex will not come out and no argument in the literature is written at that level of detail.

Circulating solution manuals for [@Har10a] are of very uneven quality and several are no longer reachable.
Use them to check an answer you have already produced, never to read a solution for the first time --- an oral asks you to construct an argument aloud, and a source that hands you one trains the wrong thing.
