# Topology

A toric variety is a union of tori indexed by the cones of its fan, and that decomposition computes its topology: the fundamental group is the lattice modulo the sublattice the cones span, the Euler characteristic is the number of maximal cones, the Betti numbers are binomial alternating sums of the cone counts, and over the complex numbers the whole space is the total space of a torus bundle over a polytope.
This page is the reason toric geometry is useful as a test laboratory: every topological invariant that is hard for a general variety is a finite combinatorial sum here.

## The orbit decomposition

[[PR-O8V3I]]

The orbit of a cone $\sigma$ is the torus of dimension $n-\dim\sigma$ obtained by forgetting the coordinates in $\sigma$, and its closure is the toric variety of the star of $\sigma$ ([[D-FULSTAR]]).
So $X_\Sigma$ is the disjoint union of its orbits, and the closure of an orbit is the union of the orbits of all cones containing it; the topology of the variety is organised by the incidence structure of the fan and nothing else.

## The fundamental group

[[PR-TORMOR]]

Over $\CC$, $\pi_1(X_\Sigma)$ is the lattice $N$ modulo the sublattice generated by the lattice points on the cones of $\Sigma$, so the fan decides simple connectedness: a fan whose cones span $N$ gives a simply connected variety, and one that does not gives a quotient of a torus.
That is the same statement as the torus $T$ being $\pi_1$-generated by the loops that extend across the boundary cones, and it recovers the familiar cases: $\PP^n$ is simply connected because its rays generate $N$, while the torus itself and the affine quadric cone are not.
Completeness is the other lattice condition in the same card, and it has a valuative reading: every one-parameter subgroup has a limit at $0$ exactly when the fan covers $N_\RR$.

## Euler characteristic

Over $\CC$ the Euler characteristic is the number of maximal cones, because every orbit of positive dimension is a torus and contributes zero, while a fixed point contributes one.
For $\PP^n$ that is $n+1$, for $\PP^1\times\PP^1$ it is $4$, and for the Hirzebruch surface $\FF_a$ it is again $4$ for every $a$ — which is the first hint that different $a$ give different varieties with the same cohomology of the underlying space ([[FE-TORHIRZ]]).

## Betti numbers

[[T-FULBETTI]]

The odd Betti numbers of a smooth complete toric variety vanish and the even ones are determined by the numbers of cones of each dimension, so the Poincaré polynomial is a fan measurement.
That is the combinatorial form of the hard Lefschetz and Hodge–Riemann statements that hold for smooth projective varieties in general: here they follow from the fan, which is why toric varieties serve as the place where a general theorem is checked first.

[[T-TORCOHOM]]

The Stanley–Reisner presentation turns the same counts into a ring: $H^*(X_\Sigma,\ZZ)$ has the faces of the fan as generators and the linear relations of the rays as relations, and the Betti number formula above is the Hilbert series of that presentation.
So intersection theory on a toric variety is the algebra of the fan, and the Chow ring and the cohomology ring coincide in the smooth case.

## The moment map

[[D-TORMOMENT]]

Over $\CC$ the projective toric variety $X_P$ of a lattice polytope $P$ is the total space of a torus bundle over $P$: the compact torus $T_c\cong(S^1)^n$ acts, the moment map has image $P$, and the fibre over a point of the relative interior of a face is the compact torus of the corresponding orbit.
So the topology of $X_P$ is that of a torus bundle over a convex body: the fibre over a point of the relative interior of a face is the compact torus of the corresponding orbit, and the orbit stratification of $X_P$ is the face stratification of $P$ — which is why a projective toric variety is the same data as a lattice polytope.

## What the topology does not see

Two remarks keep the combinatorial invariants in proportion.
First, the Euler characteristic and the Betti numbers are blind to the lattice embedding except through the cone counts, so $\FF_0$ and $\FF_1$ have the same Betti numbers and are not isomorphic; the finer invariant is the intersection form, which sees $a$ ([[FE-TORHIRZ]]).
Second, the fundamental group computation is a statement over $\CC$ with its classical topology; over a field of characteristic $p$ the corresponding étale fundamental group is prime to $p$ in the same sense, and the classical computation pins down only that part.
