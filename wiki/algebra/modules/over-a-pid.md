# Modules over a PID

## Definitions

Before using the structure theorem, separate the notions it will decompose.  Submodules
and module maps have the same one-step closure tests as their group analogues.  Simple
modules have no nontrivial submodules, indecomposable modules cannot be split as a
nontrivial direct sum, cyclic modules are generated by one element, and Noetherianity
is the finiteness condition on ascending chains of submodules.  These are different
properties; the PID theorem tells you how they interact for finitely generated modules.

## The structure theorem

For a finitely generated module over a PID, the classification separates the free part
from the torsion part and then decomposes the torsion prime by prime.  Invariant factors
and elementary divisors are two encodings of the same decomposition.  The proof strategy
to remember is therefore: isolate torsion, split off the free quotient, take primary
components, and only then choose the normal form appropriate to the question.

:::{.remark title="Free plus torsion"}
Every finitely generated module decomposes as $M \cong F_M \oplus M_t$ with $F_M$ free and $M_t$ torsion, and moreover $F_M \cong M/M_t$.

$M/M_t$ is torsion-free: if $r(m + M_t) = M_t$ then $rm$ is torsion, so $r'(rm) = 0$ for some $r'$, making $m$ torsion and $m + M_t$ the zero coset.

$F_M \cong M/M_t$: the sequence $0\to M_t\to M \to F\to 0$ gives $F \cong M/M_t$, and it splits because $F$ is free hence projective.

:::

## Ideals, and when they are free

[[PR-ASW5L]]

:::{.proof}
$\implies$:
Suppose $I$ is free with basis $B = \ts{\vector m_j}_{j\in J}$, and suppose $\abs B \geq 2$, so there are distinct $\vector m_1, \vector m_2 \in B$.
Since $R$ is commutative,
\[
\vector m_1 \vector m_2 - \vector m_2 \vector m_1 = \vector 0
.\]
which is a nontrivial dependence with coefficients $\alpha_1 = -m_2$ and $\alpha_2 = m_1$, both nonzero.
This contradicts $B$ being a basis, so $\abs B = 1$ and $I = \gens{\vector m}$ is principal.

$\impliedby$:
Suppose $I = \gens{\vector m}$ with $\vector m \neq 0$.
Every $x\in I$ is $\alpha\vector m$, and $\alpha \vector m = 0$ with $R$ a domain and $m \neq 0$ forces $\alpha = 0$.
So $\ts{\vector m}$ is independent and is a basis.

:::

## Algebraic properties

Tensor and Hom become especially concrete over a field: in finite dimensions, duals
turn tensor products into Hom spaces and the trace pairing identifies the corresponding
duals.  Over a general ring these identities require stronger finiteness/projectivity
hypotheses, so do not import vector-space formulas blindly.  Flatness is the property
that controls the basic exactness question here: tensoring with $M$ preserves injections
exactly when $M$ is flat.
