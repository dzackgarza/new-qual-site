# Quotients, products, and automorphisms

## The isomorphism theorems

The three statements below are the whole of the quotient calculus this chapter uses: a homomorphism is read through the quotient by its kernel, and a subgroup statement is carried through a quotient by the correspondence theorem.

## Direct products

A direct product decomposition is exhibited by subgroups rather than by a map: normal subgroups that together generate $G$ and whose intersections with the subgroup generated by the others are trivial multiply to $G$ by an isomorphism.
The recognition theorem turns that hypothesis into the isomorphism, so classifying a group up to isomorphism becomes the search for normal subgroups of prescribed orders and intersections.

## Semidirect products

With one normal factor only, the same construction produces a semidirect product whose multiplication is twisted by the conjugation map $H\to\Aut(N)$, and two twisting maps give isomorphic groups when they differ by an automorphism of $H$.
A normal Sylow subgroup $N$ of $G$ with a complement $H$ is the case this arises in: $N\intersect H=1$ and $NH=G$, so the recognition theorem applies to the pair.

## Automorphism groups

An automorphism of a cyclic group is multiplication by a unit of $\ZZ/n$, so the automorphism group is a unit group, and the twisting maps $H\to\Aut(N)$ of the semidirect products are counted there.

## Finitely generated abelian groups

A finitely generated abelian group is a free abelian group times finitely many finite cyclic groups, the finite part is canonically the direct sum of its $p$-primary parts, and the two standard decompositions differ only in the grouping of those cyclic factors.
The invariant factors are ordered by divisibility and the elementary divisors are the prime powers; the number of abelian groups of order $\prod_i p_i^{e_i}$ is the product of the partition numbers $P(e_i)$.
