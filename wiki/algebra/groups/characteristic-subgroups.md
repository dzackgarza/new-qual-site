# Characteristic subgroups, normal closures, and cores

Normality is not transitive: if $H \normal G$ and $N \normal H$, it does not follow that $N \normal G$. Characteristic subgroups fix this — they are preserved by *all* automorphisms, not just inner ones, and they compose correctly with normal subgroups.

## Characteristic subgroups

[[D-53LTN]]

:::{.remark title="Characteristic isn't equivalent to normalcy"}
Characteristic subgroups are normal, because $\psi_g(\wait) \da g(\wait)g\inv$ is an (inner) automorphism of $G$.
Not every normal subgroup is characteristic: take $G \da H_1 \cross H_2$ and $\psi(x, y) = (y, x)$.

:::

[[PR-NESS4]]

[[PR-IOMVN]]

:::{.proof}
$A \ch B \normal C \implies A\normal C$:

- $A\ch B$ iff $A$ is fixed by every $\psi\in \Aut(B)$., WTS $cAc\inv = A$ for all $c\in C$.
- Since $B\normal C$, the automorphism $\psi(\wait) \da c(\wait)c\inv$ descends to an element of $\Aut(B)$.
- Then $\psi(A) = A$ since $A\ch B$, so $cAc\inv = A$ and $A\normal C$.

:::

[[PR-GJLQP]]

:::{.proof}
Let $\psi \in \Aut(H)$ and $x=\psi(y)\in \psi(Z(H))$ so $y\in Z(H)$, then for arbitrary $h\in H$,
\[
\psi(y)h 
&= \psi(y) (\psi \circ \psi\inv)(h) \\
&= \psi( y \cdot \psi\inv(h) ) \\
&= \psi( \psi\inv(h) \cdot y ) && \text{since } \psi\inv(h)\in H, \, y\in Z(H) \\
&= h\psi(y)
.\]

:::

## Normal closures and cores

[[D-BPRD3]]

[[D-QMVEB]]

[[T-4XKGD]]

The **normal closure** of $H$ in $G$ is the smallest normal subgroup of $G$ containing $H$ — it is the intersection of all normal subgroups of $G$ that contain $H$, or equivalently the subgroup generated by all conjugates $gHg\inv$.

The **normal core** of $H$ in $G$ is the largest normal subgroup of $G$ contained in $H$ — it is the intersection of all conjugates $gHg\inv$. The core is trivial precisely when $G$ acts faithfully on the cosets $G/H$.

Frattini's argument connects these: if $K \normal G$ and $P$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $K$, then $G = K \cdot N_G(P)$. The proof conjugates $P$ by an arbitrary $g \in G$, uses that $gPg\inv$ is also a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $K$ (since $K \normal G$), and applies Sylow's theorem to get $gPg\inv = kPk\inv$ for some $k \in K$.
