# Gröbner bases and varieties

Throughout, $k$ is a field, $R = k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, and $J\subseteq R$ is an ideal.
Recall that a [[D-GRBNR|Gröbner basis]] of $J$ is a finite generating set whose initial monomials generate the initial ideal of $J$, for a fixed term order on the monomials. The division algorithm divides a polynomial by a finite set of polynomials with respect to the same order.

## Buchberger's algorithm

Fix a monomial order, such as the lexicographic order or the graded reverse lexicographic order, and a finite generating set $G$ of $J$.
For each pair $f_i,f_j$ of elements of $G$, form the $S$-polynomial $S(f_i,f_j)$ and reduce it by division against $G$, adjoining a nonzero remainder to $G$; stop when every $S$-polynomial of a pair has remainder zero.

By Buchberger's criterion, a finite set $G\subseteq J$ that generates $J$ is a Gröbner basis of $J$ if and only if every $S$-polynomial $S(f_i,f_j)$ of a pair of its elements reduces to zero on division by $G$ ([[P-APA17G|Buchberger criterion, with the monomial basis of a lex Gröbner ideal in four variables]]).

The algorithm terminates because the ideals generated by the leading terms of the successive sets form an ascending chain of monomial ideals, and by [[T-DICKSN|Dickson's lemma]] every monomial ideal in $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is finitely generated; the generators of the union of the chain therefore occur already at some stage, which stabilizes there.

## Applications of Gröbner bases

Let $G$ be a Gröbner basis of $J$.

Ideal membership: $f\in J$ if and only if the remainder of $f$ on division by $G$ is zero, since the remainder is the normal form of $f$ modulo the ideal of leading terms.

Elimination: for the lexicographic order with $x_1>\cdots>x_n$, the set $G\intersect k[x_{i+1},\ldots,x_n]$ is a Gröbner basis of the elimination ideal $J\intersect k[x_{i+1},\ldots,x_n]$, which solves polynomial systems by back-substitution and computes implicitizations.

Dimension and degree: for any monomial order, the monomials not in $\operatorname{LT}(J)$ form a $k$-basis of both $R/J$ and $R/\operatorname{LT}(J)$; for a graded order the two rings therefore have the same Hilbert function when $J$ is homogeneous, and the same affine Hilbert function in general, hence the same Krull dimension and the same degree.
For $k$ algebraically closed, $\dim V(J) = \dim R/J$, and the degree of $R/J$ equals $\deg V(J)$ when $J$ is radical.

Equality of ideals: for a fixed monomial order, each ideal has a unique reduced Gröbner basis, so two ideals are equal if and only if their reduced Gröbner bases are equal.

::: {.example}
For $J = (x^2)\subseteq k[x,y]$, $V(J)$ is the line $x = 0$, of degree $1$, while $R/J$ has degree $2$: the monomials $y^d$ and $xy^{d-1}$ span its degree-$d$ part. The radical $\sqrt J = (x)$ has degree $1$.
:::

## Ideals and varieties

Let $k$ be algebraically closed.
By the Nullstellensatz, $I(V(J)) = \sqrt J$ ([[T-JRTS2|Hilbert's Nullstellensatz]]).
By the Rabinowitsch trick, $f\in\sqrt J$ if and only if $1\in J + \generators{1-yf}\subseteq R[y]$, which converts the radical membership question into the ideal membership test above.
In particular $V(J) = \emptyset$ if and only if $1\in J$, if and only if the reduced Gröbner basis of $J$ is $\theset 1$: if $V(J)$ is empty then $I(V(J)) = R$, so $1\in\sqrt J$, and the Rabinowitsch criterion gives $1\in J$.
