# $L^1$

$L^1$ is the first space where the integral becomes a norm: functions are identified almost everywhere and $\|f\|_1=\int |f|$.
On an arbitrary measure space, absolute continuity of the integral says that an integrable function contributes little on sets of sufficiently small measure.
For Lebesgue $L^1(\RR^d)$ there is also a spatial tail estimate: the $L^1$ mass outside a sufficiently large ball is small.

## Basic consequences of integrability

Zero $L^1$ norm means zero almost everywhere, and integrals add over disjoint sets.
An $L^1$ function is finite almost everywhere, and its integral is absolutely continuous with respect to the underlying measure.
For Lebesgue measure on $\RR^d$, translations preserve the integral and truncating outside a sufficiently large ball changes the $L^1$ norm only slightly.
These are the estimates behind approximation arguments.

For Lebesgue $L^1(\RR^d)$, translation is continuous: $\|\tau_hf-f\|_1\to0$ as $h\to0$.
This is the lemma that later makes convolution with an approximate identity converge and explains why compactly supported or smooth approximants can recover a general $L^1$ function.

## Non-integrable functions, and what fails

Pointwise, almost-everywhere, uniform, and $L^1$ convergence are genuinely different.
The standard spike, moving-box, and spreading-box examples below are worth memorizing because each isolates a missing hypothesis: mass can concentrate, escape to infinity, or spread out while one of the other convergence modes still holds.
