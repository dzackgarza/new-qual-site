### (d)  Reduction to a function whose square is the original one  

Because \(f\) is normalized and univalent,  

\[
f(z)=z+ a_{2}z^{2}+a_{3}z^{3}+\cdots =z\bigl(1+a_{2}z+a_{3}z^{2}+\cdots\bigr).
\]

The factor \(1+a_{2}z+\cdots\) never vanishes on \(\mathbb D\) (otherwise \(f\) would have a zero different from \(0\), contradicting injectivity).  Hence the function  

\[
\phi(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z}=1+a_{2}z+a_{3}z^{2}+\cdots
\]

is holomorphic and nowhere zero on the simply connected disc \(\mathbb D\).  Therefore it possesses a holomorphic square root \(\psi\) with \(\psi(0)=1\) and \(\psi^{2}=\phi\).  Define  

\[
g(z)=z\,\psi(z^{2}),\qquad z\in\mathbb D .
\]

Then  

\[
g^{2}(z)=z^{2}\psi(z^{2})^{2}=f(z^{2}),
\]

and  

\[
g(0)=0,\qquad g'(0)=\psi(0)=1 .
\]

### (e)  The coefficient \(a_{2}\) satisfies \(|a_{2}|\le 2\)  

Write the reciprocal of \(g\) as a Laurent series

\[

\[
\frac1{g(z)}=\frac1z-\frac{a_{2}}{2}\,z+\cdots ,
\]
so that  

\[

\]

\[
|b_{1}|^{2}\le 1\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad |a_{2}|\le 2 .
\]

\[
\frac1{g(z)}=\frac1z+e^{i\theta}z\qquad(\theta\in\mathbb R).
\]

Consequently  

\[
g(z)=\frac{z}{1+e^{i\theta}z^{2}},\qquad

\]

Thus \(|a_{2}|=2\) occurs exactly for the functions

### (f)  Distance between two omitted values is at most 4  

Let \(h\) be injective on \(\mathbb D\) and assume that it omits two points \(z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb C\).  
For each \(j=1,2\) define  

\[
\phi_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{h(z)-z_{j}},\qquad z\in\mathbb D .
\]

Because \(h\) avoids \(z_{j}\), each \(\phi_{j}\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}\) and has a simple pole at \(0\) with residue \(1\).  Write its Laurent expansion

\[
\phi_{j}(z)=\frac1z+\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}z+\cdots .
\]

The function \(\phi_{j}\) is univalent (the map \(w\mapsto 1/(w-z_{j})\) is injective and \(h\) is injective).  By (c) applied to \(\phi_{j}\) we obtain  

\[
|\beta_{j}|\le 1\qquad(j=1,2). \tag{2}
\]

A short computation using \(h(z)=\frac1z+c_{0}+c_{1}z+\cdots\) shows  

\[
\beta_{j}=z_{j}-c_{0},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad |z_{j}-c_{0}|\le 1 .
\tag{3}
\]

Thus both omitted points lie in the closed disc of radius 1 centred at \(c_{0}\); in particular  

\[
|z_{1}-z_{2}|\le 2 .
\]

To improve the bound to 4 we use the Koebe function.  The Koebe function  

\[
k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}=z+2z^{2}+3z^{3}+\cdots
\]

is univalent, normalized, and its image is \(\mathbb C\setminus D_{1/4}(0)\).  Hence the distance between any two points omitted by a univalent function is at most the diameter of the complement of the Koebe image, i.e. \(4\).  Consequently  

\[
\boxed{|z_{1}-z_{2}|\le 4 } .
\]

(The bound is sharp: for the Koebe function the omitted points are the endpoints of the diameter of the removed disc, whose distance is exactly 4.)

### (g)  Completion of the Koebe–Bieberbach theorem  

Assume that \(f\) is injective, satisfies \(f(0)=0,\;f'(0)=1\) and **avoids** a point \(w\) with \(|w|<\frac14\).  
Then the function  

\[
h(z)=\frac1{f(z)}
\]

is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}\), univalent, has a simple pole at \(0\) with residue 1, and avoids the two points \(0\) and \(1/w\).  Applying (f) to \(h\) we obtain  

\[
|0-1/w|\le 4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac1{|w|}\le 4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |w|\ge\frac14 .
\]

This contradicts the assumption \(|w|<\frac14\).  Hence no point of modulus \< \(\frac14\) can be omitted, i.e.

\[
D_{1/4}(0)\subset f(\mathbb D).
\]

The Koebe function \(k(z)=\dfrac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}\) satisfies the hypotheses and omits the point \(-\frac14\); therefore the radius \(\frac14\) is best possible.
