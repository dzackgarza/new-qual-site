## Model Solution

Let \(\mathbb D=\{z:|z|<1\}\) and \(\mathbb D^*=\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}\).

## (a)

Take

\[
f_n(z)=\frac zn.
\]

Then \(f_n\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\), \(f_n(0)=0\), and

\[
f_n(\mathbb D)=D_{1/n}(0).
\]

Hence \(1/n\notin f_n(\mathbb D)\), because \(D_{1/n}(0)\) is the open disk of radius \(1/n\). Also

\[
f_n'(0)=\frac1n\to 0.
\]

If a universal \(r>0\) existed, then choosing \(n\) with \(1/n<r\) would give

\[
1/n\in D_r(0)
\]

but \(1/n\notin f_n(\mathbb D)\), contradiction. Thus no such \(r\) exists without further normalization.

## (b)

Now impose \(f'(0)=1\). For \(\varepsilon>0\), define

\[
f_\varepsilon(z)=\varepsilon\left(e^{z/\varepsilon}-1\right).
\]

Then \(f_\varepsilon\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\),

\[
f_\varepsilon(0)=0,
\]

and

\[
f_\varepsilon'(z)=e^{z/\varepsilon},
\qquad
f_\varepsilon'(0)=1.
\]

But \(f_\varepsilon(\mathbb D)\) omits the value \(-\varepsilon\), because

\[
f_\varepsilon(z)=-\varepsilon
\]

would imply

\[
e^{z/\varepsilon}=0,
\]

which is impossible.

Given any \(r>0\), choose \(0<\varepsilon<r\). Then \(-\varepsilon\in D_r(0)\), but \(-\varepsilon\notin f_\varepsilon(\mathbb D)\). Therefore even the normalization \(f(0)=0\), \(f'(0)=1\) is insufficient without injectivity.

## (d)

Let

\[
f(z)=z+a_2z^2+a_3z^3+\cdots
\]

be holomorphic and injective on \(\mathbb D\), with \(f(0)=0\) and \(f'(0)=1\).

Because \(f\) is injective and \(f(0)=0\), we have

\[
f(z)\neq 0\qquad (z\neq 0).
\]

Thus

\[
\frac{f(z)}z
\]

is holomorphic and nowhere zero on \(\mathbb D\). Since \(\mathbb D\) is simply connected, there exists a holomorphic function \(\psi\) on \(\mathbb D\) such that

\[
\psi(z)^2=\frac{f(z)}z,
\qquad
\psi(0)=1.
\]

Define

\[
g(z)=z\psi(z^2).
\]

Then

\[
g(z)^2
=
z^2\psi(z^2)^2
=
z^2\frac{f(z^2)}{z^2}
=
f(z^2).
\]

Also,

\[
g(0)=0,
\]

and

\[
g'(0)=\psi(0)=1.
\]

It remains to check injectivity. Suppose

\[
g(z_1)=g(z_2).
\]

Squaring gives

\[
f(z_1^2)=f(z_2^2).
\]

Since \(f\) is injective,

\[
z_1^2=z_2^2.
\]

Thus \(z_2=\pm z_1\). If \(z_2=z_1\), we are done. If \(z_2=-z_1\), then because \(g\) is odd,

\[
g(z_2)=g(-z_1)=-g(z_1).
\]

But \(g(z_2)=g(z_1)\), so \(g(z_1)=0\). Since

\[
g(z)=z\psi(z^2)
\]

and \(\psi\) is nowhere zero, this forces \(z_1=0\), hence \(z_2=0\). Therefore \(g\) is injective.

Thus \(g\) satisfies the same normalization:

\[
g(0)=0,\qquad g'(0)=1,
\]

and

\[
\boxed{g(z)^2=f(z^2).}
\]

## (e)

Let \(f\) be as above. From part (d), there is an odd univalent function

\[
g(z)=z\psi(z^2)
\]

such that

\[
g(z)^2=f(z^2).
\]

Write

\[
g(z)=z+Bz^3+\cdots.
\]

Then

\[
g(z)^2=z^2+2Bz^4+\cdots.
\]

On the other hand,

\[
f(z^2)=z^2+a_2z^4+\cdots.
\]

Therefore

\[
2B=a_2,
\qquad
B=\frac{a_2}{2}.
\]

Now consider

\[
H(z)=\frac1{g(z)}.
\]

Since \(g\) is injective and has its only zero at \(0\), \(H\) is injective on \(\mathbb D^*\) and has a simple pole at \(0\). Its expansion is

H(z)=\frac1z+c_0+c_1z+\cdots.

Because \(g\) is odd, \(H\) is odd, so \(c_0=0\). Also,

\[
H(z)
=
\frac1{z+Bz^3+\cdots}
=
\frac1z\left(1+Bz^2+\cdots\right)^{-1}
=
\frac1z-Bz+\cdots.
\]

Thus

\[
c_1=-B=-\frac{a_2}{2}.

By part (c),

\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\le 1.
\]

In particular,

\[
|c_1|^2\le 1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\left|\frac{a_2}{2}\right|\le 1,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{|a_2|\le 2.}

Now suppose equality holds, i.e.

\[
|a_2|=2.
\]

Then \(|c_1|=1\). Since

\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\le 1,
\]

and the \(n=1\) term already equals \(1\), we must have

\[
c_n=0\qquad(n\ge 2).
\]

Thus

\[
H(z)=\frac1z+c_1z.
\]

\[
\frac1{g(z)}=\frac1z+c_1z

\frac{1+c_1z^2}{z}.

g(z)=\frac{z}{1+c_1z^2}.

f(z)=\frac{z}{(1+c_1z)^2}.

\[
\boxed{

for some \(\theta\in\mathbb R\).

\frac{z_1}{(1-az_1)^2}

Therefore equality holds if and only if

\[
\boxed{
f(z)=\frac{z}{(1-e^{i\theta}z)^2}.
}

## (f)

Let

\[
h(z)=\frac1z+c_0+c_1z+c_2z^2+\cdots
\]

be injective on \(\mathbb D^*\), and suppose \(h\) omits two finite values \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\).

For \(j=1,2\), define

\[
F_j(z)=\frac1{h(z)-\alpha_j}.
\]

Because \(h\) has a simple pole at \(0\), \(F_j\) extends holomorphically to \(0\). Moreover,

\[
h(z)-\alpha_j
=
\frac1z+(c_0-\alpha_j)+c_1z+\cdots
=
\frac1z\left(1+(c_0-\alpha_j)z+c_1z^2+\cdots\right).
\]

Therefore

\[
F_j(z)
=
z\left(1+(c_0-\alpha_j)z+c_1z^2+\cdots\right)^{-1}.
\]

Thus

\[
F_j(z)
=
z+(\alpha_j-c_0)z^2+\cdots.
\]

So

\[
F_j(0)=0,
\qquad
F_j'(0)=1.
\]

Also, \(F_j\) is injective on \(\mathbb D\). Indeed, \(F_j(z)=0\) only at \(z=0\), and if \(z_1,z_2\neq 0\) and \(F_j(z_1)=F_j(z_2)\), then

\[
h(z_1)=h(z_2),
\]

so \(z_1=z_2\).

Thus each \(F_j\) is a normalized univalent function on \(\mathbb D\). By part (e), its second coefficient has modulus at most \(2\). Hence

\[
|\alpha_j-c_0|\le 2
\]

for \(j=1,2\). Therefore

\[
|\alpha_1-\alpha_2|
\le
|\alpha_1-c_0|+|\alpha_2-c_0|
\le
2+2
=
4.
\]

So

\[
\boxed{|\alpha_1-\alpha_2|\le 4.}
\]

## (g)

Let \(f\) be holomorphic and injective on \(\mathbb D\), with

\[
f(0)=0,
\qquad
f'(0)=1.
\]

We prove that

\[
D_{1/4}(0)\subset f(\mathbb D).
\]

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that some \(w\) with

\[
0<|w|<\frac14
\]

is omitted by \(f\). Define

\[
h(z)=\frac1{f(z)}
\]

on \(\mathbb D^*\). Since \(f\) is injective and \(f(0)=0\), we have \(f(z)\neq 0\) for \(z\neq 0\). Thus \(h\) is holomorphic and injective on \(\mathbb D^*\). Its expansion is

\[
h(z)=\frac1z+\cdots.
\]

Also, \(h\) omits \(0\), because \(1/f(z)\neq 0\). Since \(f\) omits \(w\), \(h\) also omits \(1/w\). Therefore \(h\) is injective on \(\mathbb D^*\) and omits the two values

\[
0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac1w.
\]

By part (f),

\[
\left|0-\frac1w\right|\le 4.
\]

Thus

\[
\frac1{|w|}\le 4,
\]

so

\[
|w|\ge \frac14,
\]

contradicting \(|w|<1/4\). Therefore no such omitted \(w\) exists, and hence

\[
\boxed{D_{1/4}(0)\subset f(\mathbb D).}
\]

Finally, the constant \(1/4\) is sharp. Consider the Koebe function

\[
k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}.
\]

It is normalized and univalent on \(\mathbb D\). But

\[
k(z)=-\frac14
\]

would imply

\[
\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}=-\frac14,
\]

so

\[
4z=-(1-z)^2,
\]

hence

\[
(z+1)^2=0.
\]

Thus \(z=-1\), which is not in \(\mathbb D\). Therefore

\[
-\frac14\notin k(\mathbb D).
\]

So no disk of radius larger than \(1/4\) can be guaranteed to lie in \(f(\mathbb D)\) for every normalized univalent \(f\). Hence the best possible universal radius is

\[
\boxed{\frac14}.
\]
