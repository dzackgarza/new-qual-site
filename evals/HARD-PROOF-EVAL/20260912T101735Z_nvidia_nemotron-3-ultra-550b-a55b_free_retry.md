## Model Solution

**Solution to the Koebe–Bieberbach radius theorem**

We work in the unit disc \(\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}\).  
A function \(f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}\) is *normalized univalent* if it is holomorphic, injective, and satisfies \(f(0)=0,\;f'(0)=1\).  
The theorem states that for every such \(f\) one has \(D_{1/4}(0)\subset f(\mathbb{D})\), and the constant \(1/4\) is best possible.

---

### (a) No uniform radius without further restrictions

For \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) define \(f_n(z)=\frac{z}{n}\).  
Then \(f_n(0)=0\) and \(f_n\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb{D}\).  
The image is \(f_n(\mathbb{D})=D_{1/n}(0)\), which does **not** contain the point \(1/n\) (it contains only points of modulus \(<1/n\)).  
Moreover \(f_n'(0)=\frac{1}{n}\to 0\).

Given any \(r>0\), choose \(n>1/r\). Then \(1/n\in D_r(0)\) but \(1/n\notin f_n(\mathbb{D})\); hence \(D_r(0)\not\subset f_n(\mathbb{D})\).  
Thus no single \(r>0\) works for all holomorphic \(f\) with \(f(0)=0\).

---

### (b) The condition \(f'(0)=1\) is still insufficient

For \(\varepsilon>0\) set \(f_\varepsilon(z)=\varepsilon(e^{z/\varepsilon}-1)\).  
Then \(f_\varepsilon(0)=0\) and \(f_\varepsilon'(z)=e^{z/\varepsilon}\), so \(f_\varepsilon'(0)=1\).  
Because \(e^{z/\varepsilon}\neq 0\) for all \(z\), we have \(f_\varepsilon(z)\neq -\varepsilon\); i.e. \(-\varepsilon\notin f_\varepsilon(\mathbb{D})\).

If a uniform \(r>0\) existed, we could take \(\varepsilon<r\). Then \(-\varepsilon\in D_r(0)\) but \(-\varepsilon\notin f_\varepsilon(\mathbb{D})\), a contradiction.  
Hence even with \(f'(0)=1\) no universal \(r\) exists.

### (c) Area theorem for functions with a simple pole

Let \(h(z)=\frac{1}{z}+c_0+c_1z+c_2z^2+\cdots\) be analytic and injective on the punctured disc \(0<|z|<1\).  
For \(0<\rho<1\) consider the image \(\Omega_\rho=h(\mathbb{D}_\rho\setminus\{0\})\). Its complement \(K_\rho=\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega_\rho\) is a compact set (because \(h(z)\to\infty\) as \(z\to 0\)). The boundary of \(\Omega_\rho\) is the simple closed curve \(\Gamma_\rho=h(\{z:|z|=\rho\})\).

We compute the area of \(K_\rho\) using the line integral formula.  
Parametrise \(\Gamma_\rho\) by \(z=\rho e^{i\theta},\;0\le\theta\le 2\pi\). Because the dominant term \(1/z\) maps the circle clockwise, \(\Gamma_\rho\) is negatively oriented. The area of the interior of a negatively oriented curve is \(-\frac12\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Gamma_\rho}\bar w\,dw\). Hence

\[
\operatorname{Area}(K_\rho)=-\frac12\operatorname{Im}\int_0^{2\pi}\overline{h(\rho e^{i\theta})}\,h'(\rho e^{i\theta})\,i\rho e^{i\theta}\,d\theta
=-\frac12\operatorname{Im}\oint_{|z|=\rho}\overline{h(z)}\,h'(z)\,dz.
\]

On \(|z|=\rho\) we have \(\bar z=\rho^2/z\), so

\[
\overline{h(z)}=\frac{z}{\rho^2}+\sum_{n=0}^\infty \bar c_n\rho^{2n}z^{-n},\qquad
h'(z)=-\frac{1}{z^2}+\sum_{m=1}^\infty m c_m z^{m-1}.
\]

The product \(\overline{h(z)}h'(z)\) is a Laurent series in \(z\). The integral picks out the coefficient of \(z^{-1}\):

\[
\oint_{|z|=\rho}\overline{h(z)}h'(z)\,dz=2\pi i\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\rho^2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}\Bigr).
\]

Therefore

\[
\operatorname{Area}(K_\rho)=-\frac12\operatorname{Im}\Bigl[2\pi i\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\rho^2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}\Bigr)\Bigr]
=\pi\Bigl(\frac{1}{\rho^2}-\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}\Bigr).
\]

Area is non‑negative, so \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}\le 1/\rho^2\) for every \(\rho<1\).  
Letting \(\rho\to 1^-\) (monotone convergence) yields

\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\le 1.
\]

### (d) Construction of the “square root” function \(g\)

Let \(f(z)=z+a_2z^2+a_3z^3+\cdots\) be normalized univalent.  
Because \(f\) is injective and \(f(0)=0\), we have \(f(z)\neq 0\) for \(z\neq 0\). Hence \(f(z)/z\) is holomorphic and nowhere zero on \(\mathbb{D}\). Since \(\mathbb{D}\) is simply connected, there exists a holomorphic function \(\psi\) on \(\mathbb{D}\) with \(\psi(0)=1\) and \(\psi(z)^2=f(z)/z\).

Define \(g(z)=z\psi(z^2)\). Then

\[
g(z)^2=z^2\psi(z^2)^2=z^2\frac{f(z^2)}{z^2}=f(z^2).
\]

Clearly \(g(0)=0\) and \(g'(0)=\psi(0)=1\).  
To see that \(g\) is injective, suppose \(g(z_1)=g(z_2)\). Then \(f(z_1^2)=f(z_2^2)\), and injectivity of \(f\) gives \(z_1^2=z_2^2\), i.e. \(z_1=\pm z_2\). If \(z_1=-z_2\), then \(g(z_1)=g(-z_2)=-z_2\psi(z_2^2)=-g(z_2)\). Together with \(g(z_1)=g(z_2)\) this forces \(g(z_2)=0\), hence \(z_2=0\) (because \(g'(0)=1\) implies \(0\) is a simple zero). Thus \(z_1=z_2\) in all cases, and \(g\) is injective.
