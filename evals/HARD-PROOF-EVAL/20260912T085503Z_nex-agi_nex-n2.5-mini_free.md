## Model Solution

Let \(\mathbb D=\{z:|z|<1\}\). A “normalized univalent” function means holomorphic and injective on \(\mathbb D\), with \(f(0)=0\) and \(f'(0)=1\).

---

## (a) No uniform radius without further restrictions

Take

\[
f_n(z)=\frac{z}{n}.
\]

Then \(f_n\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\), \(f_n(0)=0\), and

\[
f_n(\mathbb D)=D_{1/n}(0).
\]

Hence

\[
\frac1n\notin f_n(\mathbb D).
\]

But \(1/n\to 0\). Therefore, given any proposed radius \(r>0\), choose \(n\) so large that \(1/n<r\). Then \(1/n\in D_r(0)\), but \(1/n\notin f_n(\mathbb D)\). Thus

\[
D_r(0)\not\subseteq f_n(\mathbb D).
\]

So no such universal \(r\) exists.

Also,

\[
f_n'(0)=\frac1n\to 0,
\]

which shows the obstruction is that the derivatives at \(0\) can shrink to \(0\).

---

## (b) Even with \(f'(0)=1\), no uniform radius exists

For \(\epsilon>0\), define

\[
f_\epsilon(z)=\epsilon\left(e^{z/\epsilon}-1\right).
\]

Then \(f_\epsilon\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\), and

\[
f_\epsilon(0)=\epsilon(1-1)=0.
\]

Also,

\[
f_\epsilon'(z)=e^{z/\epsilon},
\]

so

\[
f_\epsilon'(0)=1.
\]

However,

\[
f_\epsilon(z)=-\epsilon
\]

would imply

\[
\epsilon(e^{z/\epsilon}-1)=-\epsilon,
\]

hence

\[
e^{z/\epsilon}=0,
\]

which is impossible. Thus

\[
-\epsilon\notin f_\epsilon(\mathbb D).
\]

Given any \(r>0\), choose \(0<\epsilon<r\). Then \(-\epsilon\in D_r(0)\), but \(-\epsilon\notin f_\epsilon(\mathbb D)\). Therefore no universal \(r>0\) works even under the normalization \(f'(0)=1\).

## (c) Area theorem estimate

Let

\[
h(z)=\frac1z+c_0+c_1z+c_2z^2+\cdots
\]

be injective on \(\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}\). Fix \(0<\rho<1\). We will compute the area of the complement of \(h(\mathbb D_\rho\setminus\{0\})\).

Let

\[
\gamma_\rho(t)=h(\rho e^{it}),\qquad 0\le t\le 2\pi.
\]

Since \(h\) is injective on the punctured disk, \(\gamma_\rho\) is a simple closed curve. Also, because \(h(z)\to\infty\) as \(z\to 0\), the image \(h(\mathbb D_\rho\setminus\{0\})\) is the unbounded component of \(\mathbb C\setminus \gamma_\rho\). Thus its complement is the bounded component enclosed by \(\gamma_\rho\).

Write

\[
S_\rho=\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}.
\]

We compute the signed area enclosed by \(\gamma_\rho\). Since the leading term of \(\gamma_\rho(t)\) is \(\rho^{-1}e^{-it}\), the curve is traversed clockwise. Hence the area \(A_\rho\) of the bounded component is

\[
A_\rho=-\frac1{2i}\int_{\gamma_\rho}\overline{w}\,dw.
\]

Now

\[
\gamma_\rho(t)=\rho^{-1}e^{-it}+c_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n\rho^n e^{int},
\]

so

\[
\gamma_\rho'(t)
=
-i\rho^{-1}e^{-it}
+
i\sum_{n=1}^\infty n c_n\rho^n e^{int}.
\]

Using orthogonality of exponentials,

\[
\int_0^{2\pi}\overline{\gamma_\rho(t)}\gamma_\rho'(t)\,dt
=
2\pi i\left(S_\rho-\rho^{-2}\right).
\]

Therefore

\[
A_\rho
=
-\frac1{2i}\cdot 2\pi i\left(S_\rho-\rho^{-2}\right)
=
\pi\left(\rho^{-2}-S_\rho\right).
\]

Since area is nonnegative,

\[
\pi\left(\rho^{-2}-\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}\right)\ge 0.
\]

Thus

\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\rho^{2n}\le \rho^{-2}.
\]

Letting \(\rho\to 1^-\), monotone convergence gives

\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty n|c_n|^2\le 1.
\]

This proves the desired estimate.

## (d) Construction of \(g\) with \(g^2(z)=f(z^2)\)

Let

\[
f(z)=z+a_2z^2+a_3z^3+\cdots
\]

be normalized and univalent. Define

\[
F(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z}.
\]

Since \(f(0)=0\) and \(f'(0)=1\), \(F\) extends holomorphically to \(0\) with

\[

\]

Also, \(F\) has no zeros on \(\mathbb D\). Indeed, if \(F(z_0)=0\) for \(z_0\neq 0\), then \(f(z_0)=0=f(0)\), contradicting injectivity of \(f\).

Because \(\mathbb D\) is simply connected and \(F\) is nowhere zero, there exists a holomorphic function \(\psi\) on \(\mathbb D\) such that

\[
\psi(z)^2=F(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z},

with the normalization

\[
\psi(0)=1.

\[

\]

Then \(g\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb D\), and

\[
g(0)=0.

Moreover,

\[
g'(0)=\psi(0)=1.

Also,

\[
g(z)^2
=
z^2\psi(z^2)^2
=
z^2\frac{f(z^2)}{z^2}
=
f(z^2).
\]

It remains to show that \(g\) is injective. Since

\[
g(z)=z\psi(z^2),

we have

\[

\[
f(z_1^2)=g(z_1)^2=g(z_2)^2=f(z_2^2).

## (g) Completion of the Koebe-Bieberbach radius theorem

Let \(f\) be normalized and univalent on \(\mathbb D\). We prove that

\[
D_{1/4}(0)\subseteq f(\mathbb D).
\]

Suppose, for contradiction, that some \(w\) with \(|w|<1/4\) is omitted by \(f\). Since \(f(0)=0\), we have \(w\neq 0\).

Consider

\[
h(z)=\frac1{f(z)}.
\]

Then \(h\) is holomorphic and injective on \(\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}\), and near \(0\),

\[
h(z)=\frac1z+c_0+c_1z+\cdots.
\]

Because \(f\) never vanishes except at \(0\), \(h\) never takes the value \(0\). Also, since \(f\) omits \(w\), \(h\) never takes the value \(1/w\), because

\[
h(z)=\frac1w
\]

would imply

\[
f(z)=w.
\]

Thus \(h\) avoids the two values

\[
0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac1w.
\]

By part (f),

\[
\left|0-\frac1w\right|\le 4.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac1{|w|}\le 4,
\]

so

\[
|w|\ge \frac14,
\]

contradicting \(|w|<1/4\). Hence every \(w\) with \(|w|<1/4\) lies in \(f(\mathbb D)\). Therefore

\[
D_{1/4}(0)\subseteq f(\mathbb D).
\]

It remains to show that \(1/4\) is best possible. Consider the Koebe function

\[
k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}.
\]

We already showed in part (e) that rotations of this function are normalized and univalent. In particular, \(k\) is normalized and univalent.

Also,

\[
k(z)=-\frac14
\]

would imply

\[
\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}=-\frac14.
\]

Thus

\[
4z=-(1-z)^2,
\]

so

\[
z^2+2z+1=0,
\]

hence

\[
(z+1)^2=0.
\]

The only solution is \(z=-1\), which is not in \(\mathbb D\). Therefore

\[
-\frac14\notin k(\mathbb D).
\]

So no radius larger than \(1/4\) can work for all normalized univalent functions.

Hence the optimal universal radius is

\[
\boxed{\frac14}.
\]
