::: problem
Let $R$ be an integral domain.
Recall that for any $R$-modules $M, N$, $\mathrm{Hom}_R(M,N)$ is also an $R$-module.
Let $M$ and $N$ be torsion $R$-modules.

(a) Suppose that either $M$ or $N$ is finitely generated as an $R$-module.
Prove that $\mathrm{Hom}_R(M,N)$ is also a torsion $R$-module.

(b) Give a counterexample showing that $\mathrm{Hom}_R(M,N)$ need not be torsion as an $R$-module if $M$ and $N$ are both infinitely generated.
Justify your answer.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $M$ is finitely generated, then there is a nonzero $r\in R$ such that
\[
rM=0.
\]
::: {.proof}
Choose generators
\[
m_1,\ldots,m_t
\]
of $M$.
Since $M$ is torsion, for each $i$ there is a nonzero $r_i\in R$ such that
\[
r_i m_i=0.
\]
Because $R$ is an integral domain, the product
\[
r=r_1\cdots r_t
\]
is nonzero.
It annihilates every generator $m_i$, hence annihilates all of $M$.
:::

<1>2. If $M$ is finitely generated, then $\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,N)$ is torsion.
::: {.proof}
Let $r\ne0$ be as in <1>1.
For every $f\in\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,N)$ and every $m\in M$,
\[
((rf)(m))=r f(m)=f(rm)=0.
\]
Thus
\[
rf=0.
\]
In fact the same nonzero element $r$ annihilates the entire module $\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,N)$.
:::

<1>3. If $N$ is finitely generated, then there is a nonzero $s\in R$ such that
\[
sN=0.
\]
::: {.proof}
Choose generators $n_1,\ldots,n_u$ of $N$.
For each $j$, torsion gives a nonzero $s_j\in R$ with
\[
s_jn_j=0.
\]
Since $R$ is a domain,
\[
s=s_1\cdots s_u\ne0,
\]
and $s$ annihilates every generator, hence all of $N$.
:::

<1>4. If $N$ is finitely generated, then $\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,N)$ is torsion.
::: {.proof}
Let $s\ne0$ be as in <1>3.
For any $f\in\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,N)$ and $m\in M$,
\[
((sf)(m))=s f(m)=0
\]
because $f(m)\in N$ and $sN=0$.
Hence $sf=0$ for every $f$.
Together with <1>2, this proves part (a).
:::

<1>5. For part (b), take
\[
R=\mathbb Z,
\qquad
M=N=\bigoplus_{p\text{ prime}}\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z.
\]
Then $M$ and $N$ are torsion $\mathbb Z$-modules and neither is finitely generated.
::: {.proof}
Every element of the direct sum has only finitely many nonzero components.
If its support is contained in the primes $p_1,\ldots,p_k$, then
\[
p_1\cdots p_k
\]
annihilates it.
Thus every element is torsion.

If $M$ were generated by finitely many elements, the union of the supports of those generators would involve only finitely many primes.
Their span would therefore have zero component at every other prime, contradicting the presence of all summands $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$.
Hence $M$, and likewise $N$, is not finitely generated.
:::

<1>6. The identity endomorphism
\[
\operatorname{id}_M\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(M,M)
\]
is not a torsion element.
::: {.proof}
Let $0\ne n\in\mathbb Z$.
Choose a prime $p$ not dividing $n$.
On the summand $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, multiplication by $n$ is a nonzero automorphism.
Therefore
\[
n\operatorname{id}_M\ne0.
\]
Since no nonzero integer annihilates $\operatorname{id}_M$, this endomorphism is not torsion.
Consequently
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(M,M)
\]
is not a torsion module, proving part (b).
:::
