::: {.problem}
Let $A$ and $B$ be subsets of a topological space $X$.
Suppose that $p$ is a limit point of the subset $A\cup B$.
Show that $p$ is also a limit point of $A$ or $B$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Suppose, for contradiction, that $p$ is neither a limit point of $A$ nor a limit point of $B$.
::: {.proof}
This is the negation of the desired conclusion.
:::

<1>2. There are neighborhoods $U$ and $V$ of $p$ such that
\[
(U\setminus\{p\})\cap A=\varnothing
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(V\setminus\{p\})\cap B=\varnothing.
\]
::: {.proof}
By definition, $p$ is a limit point of a subset $C\subseteq X$ exactly when every neighborhood of $p$ contains a point of $C$ different from $p$.
Thus the two failures assumed in <1>1 give the stated neighborhoods.
:::

<1>3. The neighborhood $W=U\cap V$ of $p$ contains no point of $A\cup B$ other than possibly $p$.
::: {.proof}
Since finite intersections of neighborhoods of $p$ are neighborhoods of $p$, $W$ is a neighborhood of $p$.
Moreover,
\[
W\setminus\{p\}
\subseteq U\setminus\{p\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
W\setminus\{p\}
\subseteq V\setminus\{p\}.
\]
Hence <1>2 gives
\[
(W\setminus\{p\})\cap A=\varnothing
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(W\setminus\{p\})\cap B=\varnothing.
\]
Therefore
\[
(W\setminus\{p\})\cap(A\cup B)=\varnothing.
\]
:::

<1>4. This contradicts the hypothesis that $p$ is a limit point of $A\cup B$.
::: {.proof}
By the limit-point definition, every neighborhood of $p$ must meet
\[
(A\cup B)\setminus\{p\}.
\]
The neighborhood $W$ from <1>3 does not.
:::

<1>5. Consequently $p$ is a limit point of $A$ or a limit point of $B$.
::: {.proof}
The contrary assumption in <1>1 is impossible by <1>4.
:::
:::
