::: {.problem}
A topological space $X$ is called *metacompact* if for every open cover $\mathcal C$ of $X$, there is a subcover $\mathcal C'$ satisfying the property that for every point $p\in X$, there are only finitely many open sets in $\mathcal C'$ containing $p$.

(a) Show that metacompactness is a homeomorphism invariant.

(b) Let $X$ be the integers with the topology $\mathcal T:=\{U\subseteq X\mid 0\in U\}\cup\{\varnothing\}$.
Show that this space is not metacompact.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Metacompactness, with the definition in the problem, is invariant under homeomorphism.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
f:X\longrightarrow Y
\]
be a homeomorphism, and suppose $X$ is metacompact.
Let $\mathcal C$ be an arbitrary open cover of $Y$.
Then
\[
f^{-1}(\mathcal C)
:=
\{f^{-1}(U):U\in\mathcal C\}
\]
is an open cover of $X$.
By metacompactness of $X$, there is a subfamily
\[
\mathcal D\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathcal C)
\]
that covers $X$ and is point-finite.

For each member $f^{-1}(U)$ of $\mathcal D$, retain the corresponding $U\in\mathcal C$, and call the resulting subfamily $\mathcal C'\subseteq\mathcal C$.
Since $f$ is onto and $\mathcal D$ covers $X$, the family $\mathcal C'$ covers $Y$.

Fix $y\in Y$ and put $x=f^{-1}(y)$.
For every $U\in\mathcal C'$,
\[
y\in U
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x\in f^{-1}(U).
\]
Thus the number of members of $\mathcal C'$ containing $y$ equals the number of corresponding members of $\mathcal D$ containing $x$, which is finite.
Hence $\mathcal C'$ is point-finite.
So $Y$ is metacompact.

Applying the same argument to the inverse homeomorphism $f^{-1}:Y\to X$ proves the converse implication.
Therefore metacompactness is a homeomorphism invariant.
:::

For part (b), let
\[
X=\mathbb Z,
\qquad
\mathcal T=\{U\subseteq X:0\in U\}\cup\{\varnothing\}.
\]

<1>2. The family
\[
\mathcal C
=
\bigl\{\{0,n\}:n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}\bigr\}
\]
is an open cover of $X$.
::: {.proof}
Every set $\{0,n\}$ contains $0$, so it belongs to $\mathcal T$.
The point $0$ lies in every member of $\mathcal C$, and each nonzero integer $n$ lies in the member $\{0,n\}$.
Hence
\[
\bigcup\mathcal C=\mathbb Z.
\]
:::

<1>3. Every subcover of $\mathcal C$ is equal to $\mathcal C$ itself.
::: {.proof}
Fix $n\neq0$.
Among the members of $\mathcal C$, the only one containing $n$ is $\{0,n\}$.
Therefore any subfamily that still covers $X$ must contain $\{0,n\}$ for every nonzero integer $n$.
Thus it contains every member of $\mathcal C$.
:::

<1>4. No subcover of $\mathcal C$ is point-finite.
::: {.proof}
By <1>3 the only subcover is $\mathcal C$.
But $0$ belongs to every member
\[
\{0,n\},
\qquad n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\},
\]
and there are infinitely many such members.
Hence $\mathcal C$ is not point-finite.
:::

<1>5. Consequently $(\mathbb Z,\mathcal T)$ is not metacompact in the sense defined in the problem.
::: {.proof}
The open cover $\mathcal C$ from <1>2 has no point-finite subcover by <1>4, contradicting the defining requirement for metacompactness.
:::
