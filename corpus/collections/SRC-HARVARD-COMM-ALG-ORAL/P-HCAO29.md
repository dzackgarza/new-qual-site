::: problem
Let $I$ be an ideal in a polynomial ring.
For $f \in I$, let $\operatorname{lt}(f)$ denote the sum of the terms of highest degree in $f$.
Explain how to compute
\[
\operatorname{lt}(I)=\langle \operatorname{lt}(f) \mid f \in I\rangle.
\]
:::


::: solution
Write the polynomial ring as \(R=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\), and introduce a new
variable \(t\). The clean computation is by homogenization and saturation.

<1>1. Let \(I=(f_1,\ldots,f_r)\), and let \(f_i^h\in k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t]\)
be the total-degree homogenization of \(f_i\). Define
\[
J=(f_1^h,\ldots,f_r^h):t^\infty.
\]
Then \(J\) is the homogeneous ideal consisting of homogenizations of elements
of \(I\), together with their homogeneous consequences.
::: proof
If \(f\in I\), write \(f=\sum a_i f_i\). After homogenizing, multiplying by a
sufficient power of \(t\) clears the degree discrepancies among the summands,
so
\[
t^N f^h\in(f_1^h,\ldots,f_r^h)
\]
for some \(N\). Hence \(f^h\in J\).

Conversely, if a homogeneous \(F\in J\), then \(t^N F\in(f_i^h)\) for some
\(N\). Setting \(t=1\) gives
\[
F(x,1)\in I.
\]
Thus the saturated homogenized ideal records exactly the homogeneous data of
all elements of \(I\).
:::

<1>2. If \(f\in R\) has degree \(d\), then
\[
f^h(x_1,\ldots,x_n,0)=\operatorname{lt}(f),
\]
where \(\operatorname{lt}(f)\) denotes its highest-degree homogeneous part.
::: proof
Write \(f=f_d+f_{d-1}+\cdots+f_0\) with each \(f_j\) homogeneous of degree
\(j\). Then
\[
f^h=f_d+t f_{d-1}+\cdots+t^d f_0.
\]
Setting \(t=0\) leaves precisely \(f_d=\operatorname{lt}(f)\).
:::

<1>3. Therefore
\[
\operatorname{lt}(I)
=\frac{J+(t)}{(t)}
\subset k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t]/(t)
\cong k[x_1,\ldots,x_n].
\]
::: proof
By <1>1, the homogeneous elements of \(J\) arise from homogenized elements of
\(I\); by <1>2, specializing \(t=0\) sends each such homogenization to its
highest-degree form. Hence the image of \(J\) modulo \(t\) is generated by
exactly the \(\operatorname{lt}(f)\) with \(f\in I\).
:::

<1>4. This gives an effective Gröbner-basis computation.
::: proof
Compute a Gröbner basis of \((f_1^h,\ldots,f_r^h)\), saturate with respect to
\(t\) (for example by the standard elimination trick introducing \(u\) and the
relation \(1-ut\)), and then specialize \(t=0\). The resulting generators
compute \(\operatorname{lt}(I)\).

The saturation step is essential: cancellations among the highest-degree parts
of the original generators can produce new leading forms, so in general
\[
(\operatorname{lt}(f_1),\ldots,\operatorname{lt}(f_r))
\ne\operatorname{lt}(I).
\]
:::
:::
