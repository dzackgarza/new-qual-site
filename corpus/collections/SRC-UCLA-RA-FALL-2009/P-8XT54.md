::: {.problem}
Let $f$ be a non-constant meromorphic function on the complex plane $\mathbb{C}$ that obeys $$f(z)=f(z+\sqrt{2})=f(z+i\sqrt{2}).$$ (In particular, the poles of these three functions coincide.)
Assume $f$ has at most one pole in the closed unit disc $\overline{D}=\{z:|z|\le 1\}$.

(a) Prove that $f$ has exactly one pole in $\overline{D}$.

(b) Prove that this is not a simple pole.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
\[
L=\sqrt2\mathbb Z+i\sqrt2\mathbb Z
\]
be the period lattice, and set
\[
a=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}.
\]

<1>1. Every complex number is congruent modulo $L$ to a point of the square
\[
Q=[-a,a]+i[-a,a],
\]
and
\[
Q\subseteq\overline D.
\]
::: {.proof}
Given $z=x+iy$, choose integers $m,n$ so that
\[
x-m\sqrt2\in[-a,a]
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
y-n\sqrt2\in[-a,a].
\]
Then
\[
z-(m+in)\sqrt2\in Q.
\]

If $w=u+iv\in Q$, then
\[
|w|^2=u^2+v^2
\le a^2+a^2
=1.
\]
Thus $Q$ lies in the closed unit disk.
:::

<1>2. If $f$ has no pole in $\overline D$, then $f$ is entire and bounded on $\mathbb C$.
::: {.proof}
Suppose $f$ had a pole $p$ somewhere in $\mathbb C$.
By <1>1, choose $\ell\in L$ such that
\[
p-\ell\in Q\subseteq\overline D.
\]
Periodicity gives
\[
f(z)=f(z+\ell),
\]
so translating the Laurent expansion at $p$ shows that $p-\ell$ is also a pole.
This contradicts the assumption that $\overline D$ contains no pole.
Hence $f$ is entire.

The continuous function $f$ is bounded on the compact square $Q$.
Every $z\in\mathbb C$ is congruent modulo $L$ to a point $w\in Q$, and periodicity gives
\[
f(z)=f(w).
\]
Therefore the bound on $Q$ is a global bound on $\mathbb C$.
:::

<1>3. The function $f$ has exactly one pole in $\overline D$.
::: {.proof}
If it had none, <1>2 would make it a bounded entire function.
Liouville's theorem would then imply that $f$ is constant, contrary to the hypothesis.
Thus there is at least one pole in $\overline D$.
The problem assumes there is at most one, so there is exactly one.
This proves part (a).
:::

Let $p$ denote the unique pole in $\overline D$.

<1>4. Every pole of $f$ belongs to the single lattice orbit
\[
p+L.
\]
::: {.proof}
Let $q$ be any pole.
By <1>1, choose $\ell\in L$ such that
\[
q-\ell\in Q\subseteq\overline D.
\]
Periodicity makes $q-\ell$ a pole.
By uniqueness from <1>3,
\[
q-\ell=p.
\]
Hence
\[
q=p+\ell\in p+L.
\]
Conversely, periodicity makes every point of $p+L$ a pole.
:::

<1>5. The square
\[
P=p+[-a,a]+i[-a,a]
\]
has exactly one pole of $f$ in its interior, namely $p$, and no pole on its boundary.
::: {.proof}
By <1>4, the poles are
\[
p+m\sqrt2+in\sqrt2,
\qquad m,n\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Relative to the center $p$, every nonzero lattice vector has at least one coordinate of absolute value at least
\[
\sqrt2>a.
\]
Therefore no nonzero lattice translate of $p$ lies in $P$.
The center $p$ lies in the interior, so it is the unique pole in $P$ and the boundary is pole-free.
:::

<1>6. One has
\[
\int_{\partial P}f(z)\,dz=0.
\]
::: {.proof}
Opposite vertical sides of $P$ differ by the period $\sqrt2$ and are traversed in opposite directions.
Thus their integrals cancel because
\[
f(z+\sqrt2)=f(z).
\]
Similarly, the two horizontal sides differ by the period $i\sqrt2$, and their integrals cancel because
\[
f(z+i\sqrt2)=f(z).
\]
Adding the four side integrals gives zero.
:::

<1>7. The residue of $f$ at $p$ is zero.
::: {.proof}
By <1>5, $p$ is the only pole inside $P$ and the boundary contains no pole.
The residue theorem and <1>6 give
\[
2\pi i\operatorname{Res}(f,p)
=\int_{\partial P}f(z)\,dz
=0.
\]
Hence
\[
\operatorname{Res}(f,p)=0.
\]
:::

<1>8. The pole $p$ is not simple.
::: {.proof}
At a simple pole, the coefficient of $(z-p)^{-1}$ in the Laurent expansion is nonzero; this coefficient is exactly the residue.
But <1>7 gives
\[
\operatorname{Res}(f,p)=0.
\]
Therefore the pole cannot be simple.
This proves part (b).
:::
