::: {.problem}
Let $n$ be positive and define
\[
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{x^i}{i!}.
\]
Prove that $f$ has $n$ distinct complex zeros $z_1,\ldots,z_n$, and that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n z_i^{-j}=0
\qquad\text{for }2\le j\le n.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

The polynomial $f$ has degree $n$, has no zero at $0$, and has $n$ complex zeros counted with multiplicity.

::: pf-proof

Its leading coefficient is $1/n!\neq0$, so $\deg f=n$, while
$$
f(0)=1.
$$
Thus $0$ is not a zero. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, $f$ has exactly $n$ complex zeros counted with multiplicity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

Every zero of $f$ is simple.

::: pf-proof

Differentiating gives
$$
f'(x)
=
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{x^i}{i!}
=
f(x)-\frac{x^n}{n!}.
$$
If $z$ were a multiple zero, then
$$
f(z)=f'(z)=0.
$$
The displayed identity would then give
$$
0
=
-\frac{z^n}{n!},
$$
so $z=0$, contradicting $f(0)=1$. Hence all zeros are simple.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

Denote the $n$ distinct zeros by
$$
z_1,\ldots,z_n.
$$
Then
$$
f(x)
=
\prod_{i=1}^n\left(1-\frac{x}{z_i}\right).
$$

::: pf-proof

By steps [](#s1){.pf-ref} and [](#s2){.pf-ref}, the zeros $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ are distinct and nonzero. The product on the right is a degree-$n$ polynomial with exactly the same zeros as $f$. Hence it is a nonzero scalar multiple of $f$. Both polynomials have value $1$ at $x=0$, so the scalar is $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

In a neighborhood of $0$,
$$
\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}
=
-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
\left(
\sum_{i=1}^n z_i^{-(m+1)}
\right)x^m.
$$

::: pf-proof

Taking the logarithmic derivative of the product in step [](#s3){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}
=
-\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{z_i^{-1}}{1-x/z_i}.
$$
For $|x|<\min_i|z_i|$, every denominator may be expanded geometrically:
$$
\frac{z_i^{-1}}{1-x/z_i}
=
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z_i^{-(m+1)}x^m.
$$
Summing over the finitely many roots gives the displayed series.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

The Taylor expansion of $f'/f$ at $0$ has constant term $1$ and has zero coefficients of
$$
x,x^2,\ldots,x^{n-1}.
$$

::: pf-proof

The identity in step [](#s2){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}
=
1-\frac{x^n}{n!\,f(x)}.
$$
Since $f(0)=1$, the function $1/f(x)$ is analytic near $0$. Therefore
$$
\frac{x^n}{n!\,f(x)}
$$
vanishes to order at least $n$ at $0$. Hence no powers $x^m$ with $1\leq m\leq n-1$ occur in the Taylor expansion of $f'/f$, while its constant term is $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

For every
$$
2\leq j\leq n,
$$
one has
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^n z_i^{-j}=0}.
$$

::: pf-proof

Compare the coefficient of $x^{j-1}$ in steps [](#s4){.pf-ref} and [](#s5){.pf-ref}. Since
$$
1\leq j-1\leq n-1,
$$
step [](#s5){.pf-ref} says this coefficient is $0$, while step [](#s4){.pf-ref} says it is
$$
-\sum_{i=1}^n z_i^{-j}.
$$
Thus the displayed reciprocal power sum vanishes.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s7}

The polynomial has $n$ distinct zeros and they satisfy all the required reciprocal power-sum identities.

::: pf-proof

Distinctness is step [](#s2){.pf-ref}, together with the degree count in step [](#s1){.pf-ref}. The identities are step [](#s6){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s7){.pf-ref} is exactly the assertion of the problem.

:::
