::: {.problem}
Prove that
\[
\int_0^\infty \frac{x}{e^x-e^{-x}}\,dx=\frac{\pi^2}{8}.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. For every $x>0$,
$$
\frac{1}{e^x-e^{-x}}
=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-(2k+1)x}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since $0<e^{-2x}<1$,
$$
\frac{1}{e^x-e^{-x}}
=
\frac{e^{-x}}{1-e^{-2x}}
=
e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-2kx}
=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-(2k+1)x}.
$$
:::

<1>2. The integral equals the sum of the reciprocal squares of the positive odd integers:
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x}{e^x-e^{-x}}\,dx
=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
All terms in the expansion of step <1>1 are nonnegative. Therefore the monotone convergence theorem gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x}{e^x-e^{-x}}\,dx
&=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\int_0^\infty x e^{-(2k+1)x}\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$
For $a>0$, integration by parts gives
$$
\int_0^\infty xe^{-ax}\,dx=\frac1{a^2}.
$$
Taking $a=2k+1$ proves the identity.
:::

<1>3. One has
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac1{(2k+1)^2}
=
\frac{\pi^2}{8}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Let $q$ be the $2\pi$-periodic square wave defined on $(-\pi,\pi)$ by
$$
q(x)=
\begin{cases}
-1,&-\pi<x<0,\\
1,&0<x<\pi.
\end{cases}
$$
Its constant and cosine Fourier coefficients vanish by oddness. Its sine coefficients are
$$
\begin{aligned}
b_m
&=
\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}q(x)\sin(mx)\,dx\\
&=
\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin(mx)\,dx\\
&=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac4{\pi m},&m\text{ odd},\\[4pt]
0,&m\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $q^2=1$ almost everywhere, Parseval's identity gives
$$
\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|q(x)|^2\,dx
=
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_m^2.
$$
Therefore
$$
2
=
\frac{16}{\pi^2}
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac1{(2k+1)^2},
$$
which rearranges to the claimed value.
:::

<1>4. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty \frac{x}{e^x-e^{-x}}\,dx
=
\frac{\pi^2}{8}.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Combine steps <1>2 and <1>3.
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 is the required identity.
:::
:::
