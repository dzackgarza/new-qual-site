::: {.problem}
$X$ is the space obtained by gluing a disk to a sphere as follows: Let $\alpha:S^1\to S^2$ be the map that “wraps $S^1$ around the equator of $S^2$ three times”—more explicitly, $\alpha$ is the composition of the map $S^1\to S^1$ sending $z$ to $z^3$ (where $S^1$ is identified with complex numbers of norm one) and the canonical inclusion $S^1\subseteq S^2$.
We glue $D^2$ onto $S^2$ by attaching its boundary $\partial D^2=S^1$ according to $\alpha$: $$X=(S^2\sqcup D^2)/\sim,$$ where $\sim$ is the equivalence relation generated by $\alpha(z)\sim z$ for all $z\in\partial D^2=S^1$.
Compute the singular homology of $X$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Give the equator $S^1\subset S^2$ its CW structure with one $0$-cell $v$ and one oriented $1$-cell $e$.
::: {.proof}
The circle has the standard CW structure obtained by attaching one $1$-cell to one $0$-cell with both endpoints identified.
:::

<1>2. Regard the northern and southern open hemispheres as two $2$-cells $u$ and $w$. With suitable choices of orientations, each has cellular boundary $e$.
::: {.proof}
The closure of each hemisphere is a disc whose boundary maps homeomorphically onto the equator. Choose the orientation on each $2$-cell so that its attaching map has degree $+1$ on the oriented equator. Thus
$$
\partial_2(u)=e,
\qquad
\partial_2(w)=e.
$$
:::

<1>3. The disk added in the problem is a third $2$-cell $d$ whose cellular boundary is $3e$.
::: {.proof}
Its attaching map is the composite
$$
S^1\xrightarrow{z\mapsto z^3}S^1\hookrightarrow S^2.
$$
Relative to the equatorial $1$-cell, this attaching map has degree $3$, so the cellular boundary coefficient is $3$.
:::

<1>4. Hence the cellular chain complex of $X$ is
$$
0\longrightarrow \mathbb Z^3
\xrightarrow{\partial_2}\mathbb Z
\xrightarrow{\partial_1}\mathbb Z
\longrightarrow0,
$$
where, in the bases $(u,w,d)$ and $e$,
$$
\partial_2(a,b,c)=a+b+3c,
\qquad
\partial_1=0.
$$
::: {.proof}
The formula for $\partial_2$ is the combination of <1>2 and <1>3. The unique $1$-cell is a loop at the unique $0$-cell, so $\partial_1=0$.
:::

<1>5. We have $H_0(X)\cong\mathbb Z$ and $H_1(X)=0$.
::: {.proof}
Since $\partial_1=0$,
$$
H_0(X)=C_0\cong\mathbb Z.
$$
Also $\partial_2$ is surjective because $\partial_2(1,0,0)=1$. Therefore
$$
H_1(X)=\ker\partial_1/\operatorname{im}\partial_2
=\mathbb Z/\mathbb Z=0.
$$
:::

<1>6. The second homology is
$$
H_2(X)\cong\mathbb Z^2.
$$
::: {.proof}
There are no cells above dimension $2$, so
$$
H_2(X)=\ker\partial_2
=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb Z^3:a+b+3c=0\}.
$$
This is free abelian of rank $2$; for example
$$
(1,-1,0),\qquad (-3,0,1)
$$
form a basis.
:::

<1>7. Thus the singular homology groups are
$$
H_k(X;\mathbb Z)\cong
\begin{cases}
\mathbb Z,&k=0,\\
\mathbb Z^2,&k=2,\\
0,&k\neq0,2.
\end{cases}
$$
::: {.proof}
Cellular homology computes the singular homology of a CW complex. Combine <1>5 and <1>6, and note that there are no cells in dimensions $k\ge3$.
:::
:::
