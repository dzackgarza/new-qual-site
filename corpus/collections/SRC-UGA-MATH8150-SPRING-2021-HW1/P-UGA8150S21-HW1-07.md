::: {.problem}
Prove that $f ( z ) = \abs{ z } ^ { 2 }$ has a derivative only at $z = 0$ , but nowhere else.
:::

::: {.solution}

Let
$$
f(z)=\abs{z}^2=z\overline z.
$$

## Differentiability at $0$

::: pf

::: pf-step

For $h\ne0$,
$$
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}
=
\frac{\abs{h}^2}{h}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
f(0)=0,
$$
the difference quotient is exactly the displayed expression.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

One has
$$
\boxed{
\frac{\abs{h}^2}{h}
=
\overline h}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Because
$$
\abs{h}^2=h\overline h,
$$
division by $h\ne0$ gives the identity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Therefore
$$
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}
=
0.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}, the quotient is $\overline h$, which tends to $0$ as
$h\to0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Hence
$$
\boxed{
f'(0)=0}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} is precisely the definition of the complex derivative at $0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

For $h\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{
\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}
=
\overline{z_0}
+
\overline h
+
z_0\frac{\overline h}{h}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Expand:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\abs{z_0+h}^2-\abs{z_0}^2
&=
(z_0+h)(\overline{z_0}+\overline h)
-
z_0\overline{z_0}\\
&=
z_0\overline h
+
h\overline{z_0}
+
h\overline h.
\end{aligned}
$$
Divide by $h$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Along real increments
$$
h=t\in\RR,
\qquad
t\to0,
$$
one has
$$
\boxed{
\frac{\overline h}{h}=1}.
$$


::: pf-proof

For real $t$,
$$
\overline t=t.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Therefore along real increments, the difference quotient tends to
$$
\boxed{
\overline{z_0}+z_0}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Use step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}. Along $h=t\to0$,
$$
\overline h\to0
$$
and step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} gives
$$
z_0\frac{\overline h}{h}=z_0.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Along purely imaginary increments
$$
h=it,
\qquad
t\in\RR,
\qquad
t\to0,
$$
one has
$$
\boxed{
\frac{\overline h}{h}=-1}.
$$


::: pf-proof

If $h=it$, then
$$
\overline h=-it,
$$
so
$$
\frac{\overline h}{h}
=
\frac{-it}{it}
=
-1.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Therefore along purely imaginary increments, the difference quotient
tends to
$$
\boxed{
\overline{z_0}-z_0}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Use step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}. Along $h=it\to0$,
$$
\overline h\to0
$$
and step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} gives
$$
z_0\frac{\overline h}{h}=-z_0.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

If the complex derivative $f'(z_0)$ existed, the two limits in
steps [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} would have to agree.


::: pf-proof

A complex limit is independent of the path along which the increment tends
to $0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

Equality of the two candidate limits would imply
$$
\overline{z_0}+z_0
=
\overline{z_0}-z_0.
$$


::: pf-proof

Set the limits from steps [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} equal.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

Hence
$$
2z_0=0,
$$
so
$$
z_0=0.
$$


::: pf-proof

Subtract
$$
\overline{z_0}
$$
from both sides of step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

This contradicts the assumption
$$
z_0\ne0.
$$


::: pf-proof

The point $z_0$ was fixed as nonzero before step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

Therefore $f$ is not complex differentiable at any
$$
z_0\ne0.
$$


::: pf-proof

If it were differentiable at such a point, steps [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-13){.pf-ref} would give a
contradiction.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-15}

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
f(z)=\abs{z}^2
\text{ is complex differentiable exactly at }z=0}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Differentiability at $0$ is step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref}, and non-differentiability elsewhere
is step [](#s1-14){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-15){.pf-ref} is the required conclusion.

:::
