::: {.problem}
Let $G$ be the subgroup of $\text{GL}_3(\mathbb C)$ generated by the three matrices `\begin{align*} A=
\begin{pmatrix}
  0 & 0 & 1\\
  0 & 1 & 0\\
  1 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\quad B=
\begin{pmatrix}
  0 & 0 & 1\\
  1 & 0 & 0\\
  0 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\quad C=
\begin{pmatrix}
  i & 0 & 0\\
  0 & 1 & 0\\
  0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}`{=tex}

where $i^2=-1$.
Here $\mathbb C$ denotes the complex field.

- Compute the order of $G$.

- Find a matrix in $G$ of largest possible order (as an element of $G$) and compute this order.

- Compute the number of elements in $G$ with this largest order.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The matrices $A$ and $B$ generate the permutation-matrix copy of $S_3$ in $\operatorname{GL}_3(\mathbb C)$.
::: {.proof}
The matrix $A$ interchanges the first and third standard basis vectors and fixes the second, so it represents the transposition $(1\,3)$. The matrix $B$ sends
\[
e_1\mapsto e_2,\qquad e_2\mapsto e_3,\qquad e_3\mapsto e_1,
\]
so it represents the $3$-cycle $(1\,2\,3)$. A transposition and a $3$-cycle generate $S_3$.
:::

<1>2. Conjugates of $C$ by the permutation subgroup generate
\[
D=\{\operatorname{diag}(i^a,i^b,i^c):a,b,c\in\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\}
\cong C_4^3.
\]
::: {.proof}
Conjugating $C=\operatorname{diag}(i,1,1)$ by permutation matrices moves the entry $i$ to any of the three diagonal positions. Thus $G$ contains
\[
\operatorname{diag}(i,1,1),\quad
\operatorname{diag}(1,i,1),\quad
\operatorname{diag}(1,1,i).
\]
These commuting matrices independently generate all diagonal matrices whose diagonal entries lie in
\[
\mu_4=\{1,i,-1,-i\}.
\]
Hence they generate $D\cong C_4^3$, which has order $4^3=64$.
:::

<1>3. Every element of $G$ has a unique expression
\[
dP_\sigma,
\qquad d\in D,\ \sigma\in S_3,
\]
and therefore
\[
|G|=64\cdot6=384.
\]
::: {.proof}
The subgroup $D$ is normalized by permutation matrices, since conjugating a diagonal matrix merely permutes its diagonal entries. Thus the subgroup generated by $D$ and the permutation matrices is the semidirect product
\[
D\rtimes S_3.
\]
Their intersection is trivial: the only diagonal permutation matrix is the identity. Hence the expression $dP_\sigma$ is unique, and
\[
|G|=|D||S_3|=64\cdot6=384.
\]
:::

<1>4. Let $g=dP_\sigma$ and suppose $\sigma$ has a cycle
\[
(j_1\,j_2\,\dots\,j_\ell).
\]
If the corresponding diagonal entries of $d$ are $z_{j_1},\dots,z_{j_\ell}\in\mu_4$, then the restriction of $g$ to those coordinates has order
\[
\ell\,\operatorname{ord}(z_{j_1}\cdots z_{j_\ell}).
\]
::: {.proof}
After $\ell$ applications of $g$, the permutation part returns each coordinate in the cycle to itself, and the accumulated scalar on every coordinate in that cycle is
\[
s=z_{j_1}\cdots z_{j_\ell}.
\]
Thus the $\ell$th power of the cycle block is scalar multiplication by $s$. If $g^k$ is the identity on the block, then the permutation part forces $\ell\mid k$, say $k=\ell m$, and then $s^m=1$. Hence the least such $k$ is
\[
\ell\,\operatorname{ord}(s).
\]
:::

<1>5. Elements whose permutation part is the identity have order at most $4$, and those whose permutation part is a transposition have order at most $8$.
::: {.proof}
For the identity permutation, the element lies in $D$, whose exponent is $4$.

For a transposition, there is one cycle of length $2$ and one fixed point. By <1>4, the $2$-cycle block has order at most
\[
2\cdot4=8,
\]
while the fixed coordinate contributes order at most $4$. The order of the whole element is the least common multiple of these block orders, hence at most $8$.
:::

<1>6. If the permutation part is a $3$-cycle, the element has order at most $12$, and order $12$ occurs exactly when the product of its three diagonal entries has order $4$ in $\mu_4$.
::: {.proof}
A $3$-cycle has a single cycle block of length $3$. By <1>4, its order is
\[
3\,\operatorname{ord}(z_1z_2z_3).
\]
The product lies in $\mu_4$, so its order is at most $4$. Thus the element order is at most $12$, with equality exactly when
\[
z_1z_2z_3\in\{i,-i\}.
\]
:::

<1>7. The matrix
\[
CB=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&i\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}
\]
has order $12$.
::: {.proof}
The action of $CB$ on the standard basis is
\[
e_1\mapsto e_2,\qquad
e_2\mapsto e_3,\qquad
e_3\mapsto i e_1.
\]
Hence
\[
(CB)^3=iI_3.
\]
Therefore $(CB)^{12}=I_3$. Conversely, if $(CB)^k=I_3$, the permutation part forces $3\mid k$, say $k=3m$. Then
\[
I_3=(CB)^{3m}=i^mI_3,
\]
so $4\mid m$. Hence $12\mid k$, and the order is exactly $12$.
:::

<1>8. The largest possible element order in $G$ is therefore
\[
\boxed{12}.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>5 and <1>6, every element has order at most $12$. By <1>7, an element of order $12$ exists.
:::

<1>9. Exactly $64$ elements of $G$ have order $12$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>5, an element of order $12$ must have permutation part a $3$-cycle. There are exactly two $3$-cycles in $S_3$.

Fix one of them. By <1>6, an element $dP_\sigma$ has order $12$ exactly when the product of the three diagonal entries of $d$ is $i$ or $-i$. For each prescribed product in $\mu_4$, the first two diagonal entries may be chosen arbitrarily in $4^2=16$ ways, and then the third is uniquely determined. Thus there are
\[
2\cdot16=32
\]
valid diagonal matrices for each fixed $3$-cycle. Multiplying by the two choices of $3$-cycle gives
\[
2\cdot32=64
\]
elements of order $12$.
:::
:::
