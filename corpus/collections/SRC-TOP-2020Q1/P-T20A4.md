::: {.problem}
Let $\mathcal B=\{[a,b):a,b\in\mathbb R\}$; let $\mathbb R_\ell$ denote $\mathbb R$ equipped with the topology generated by the basis $\mathcal B$ (you do not need to prove $\mathcal B$ is a basis).
Decide (with proof) if the following sets are compact in $\mathbb R_\ell$:

(a) $A=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}\cup\{0\}$.

(b) $B=\{-1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}\cup\{0\}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The set
$$
A=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}\cup\{0\}
$$
is compact in $\mathbb R_\ell$.
::: {.proof}
Let $\mathcal U$ be an open cover of $A$, and choose $U_0\in\mathcal U$ with $0\in U_0$. Because the lower-limit intervals form a basis, there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$
[0,\varepsilon)\subseteq U_0.
$$
For all sufficiently large $n$, $1/n<\varepsilon$, so $1/n\in U_0$. Only finitely many points $1,1/2,\dots,1/N$ remain, and finitely many members of $\mathcal U$ cover those points. Together with $U_0$ they form a finite subcover.
:::

<1>2. The set
$$
B=\{-1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}\cup\{0\}
$$
is not compact in $\mathbb R_\ell$.
::: {.proof}
Consider
$$
U_0=[0,1)
$$
and, for $n\ge1$,
$$
U_n=[-1/n,-1/(n+1)).
$$
These are open in the lower-limit topology. The set $U_0$ contains $0$, and each point $-1/n$ lies in $U_n$, so they cover $B$. Moreover $U_n\cap B=\{-1/n\}$ for $n\ge1$, while $U_0\cap B=\{0\}$. Hence any finite subfamily covers only finitely many points of $B$, so no finite subcover exists.
:::

<1>3. Therefore $A$ is compact and $B$ is not compact in $\mathbb R_\ell$.
::: {.proof}
Combine <1>1 and <1>2.
:::
:::
