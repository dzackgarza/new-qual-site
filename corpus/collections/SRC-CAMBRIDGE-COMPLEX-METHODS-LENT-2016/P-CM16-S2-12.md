::: {.problem}
Establish the following:

(i) $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{(1+x^2)^3}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{7\pi}{16e}$;

(ii) $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\pi}{2}$;

(iii) $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\log x}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0$.

[For part (iii), integrate $(\log z)^2/(1+z^2)$ around a keyhole, or $\log z/(1+z^2)$ along the real axis (or both). What goes wrong if you integrate $\log z/(1+z^2)$ around a keyhole?]
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

One has
$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{(1+x^2)^3}\,dx
=\frac{7\pi}{16e}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Integrate
$$
F(z)=\frac{e^{iz}}{(1+z^2)^3}
$$
over the upper semicircle of radius $R>2$. On the semicircular arc, $\abs{e^{iz}}\leq1$ and
$$
\abs{1+z^2}=\abs{z-i}\,\abs{z+i}\geq(R-1)^2,
$$
so the arc integral has magnitude at most
$$
\frac{\pi R}{(R-1)^6},
$$
which tends to $0$.

The only pole in the upper half-plane is the third-order pole at $z=i$. Its residue is
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}F(z)
=\frac1{2!}
\evaluated{
\frac{d^2}{dz^2}
\left(
\frac{e^{iz}}{(z+i)^3}
\right)
}_{z=i}.
$$
Writing $w=z+i$, the second derivative is
$$
e^{iz}
\left(
-w^{-3}-6iw^{-4}+12w^{-5}
\right).
$$
At $z=i$, one has $w=2i$, and therefore
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}F(z)
=-\frac{7i}{16e}.
$$
The residue theorem gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{ix}}{(1+x^2)^3}\,dx
=2\pi i\left(-\frac{7i}{16e}\right)
=\frac{7\pi}{8e}.
$$
The imaginary part of the integrand is odd, while the real part is even. Taking real parts and halving gives the required value $7\pi/(16e)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The Dirichlet integral satisfies
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx
=\frac\pi2.
$$


::: pf-proof

Integrate $e^{iz}/z$ over the upper-half-plane contour consisting of $[-R,-\varepsilon]$, a clockwise semicircle of radius $\varepsilon$ above the origin, $[\varepsilon,R]$, and the large upper semicircle of radius $R$. The contour encloses no pole, so its integral is $0$.

On the large arc $z=Re^{i\theta}$,
$$
\abs{\frac{e^{iz}}z\,dz}
=e^{-R\sin\theta}\,d\theta.
$$
Using $\sin\theta\geq2\theta/\pi$ for $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$ and symmetry about $\pi/2$, the large-arc integral has magnitude at most
$$
2\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-2R\theta/\pi}\,d\theta
\leq\frac\pi R,
$$
which tends to $0$.

On the small clockwise arc, $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta$ decreasing from $\pi$ to $0$, so
$$
\int_{\text{small arc}}\frac{e^{iz}}z\,dz
=\int_\pi^0 i e^{i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}}\,d\theta
\longrightarrow-i\pi.
$$
Consequently
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}x\,dx
=i\pi.
$$
The principal value of $\cos x/x$ is $0$ by oddness, while $\sin x/x$ is even. Taking imaginary parts yields
$$
2\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx=\pi.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

One has
$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2}\,dx
=\frac\pi2}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Integrating by parts on $[\varepsilon,R]$ gives
$$
\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2}\,dx
=\left[-\frac{\sin^2x}{x}\right]_{\varepsilon}^{R}
+\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\sin 2x}{x}\,dx.
$$
The boundary term tends to $0$ because $\sin^2x/x=O(x)$ at $0$ and is $O(1/x)$ at infinity. Thus
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2}\,dx
=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin 2x}{x}\,dx.
$$
With $u=2x$, the right-hand side becomes
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du,
$$
which equals $\pi/2$ by step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

One has
$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty\frac{\log x}{1+x^2}\,dx=0}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The integral converges absolutely near $0$ because $\abs{\log x}$ is integrable there, and near infinity because
$$
\frac{\log x}{1+x^2}
=O\!\left(\frac{\log x}{x^2}\right).
$$
Let its value be $I$. Under $x=1/t$,
$$
I
=\int_0^\infty
\frac{-\log t}{1+t^2}\,dt
=-I.
$$
Hence $I=0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

A keyhole contour applied directly to $\Log z/(1+z^2)$ does not determine the integral in step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} because the two copies of the target integral cancel.


::: pf-proof

Take the branch $0<\arg z<2\pi$. On the upper side of the positive real axis,
$$
\Log x=\log x,
$$
while on the lower side,
$$
\Log x=\log x+2\pi i.
$$
If
$$
I_{\varepsilon,R}
=\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\log x}{1+x^2}\,dx,
\qquad
A_{\varepsilon,R}
=\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{dx}{1+x^2},
$$
then the two straight sides of the keyhole contribute
$$
I_{\varepsilon,R}
-\left(I_{\varepsilon,R}+2\pi iA_{\varepsilon,R}\right)
=-2\pi iA_{\varepsilon,R}.
$$
Thus the desired logarithmic integral disappears from the contour equation. Squaring $\Log z$ prevents that cancellation because
$$
(\log x+2\pi i)^2-(\log x)^2
=4\pi i\log x-4\pi^2,
$$
which retains a term proportional to the target integral.

:::
