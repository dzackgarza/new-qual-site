::: {.problem}
Evaluate $\lim_{R\to\infty} \int_{-R}^{R} \frac{x\,\mathrm{d}x}{1 + x + x^2}$. Why is the limit here rather than just the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$?
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The symmetric limit is
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{R\to\infty}
\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{x\,dx}{1+x+x^2}
=-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt3}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Write
$$
x=\frac12(2x+1)-\frac12.
$$
Since
$$
x^2+x+1
=\left(x+\frac12\right)^2+\frac34,
$$
an antiderivative is
$$
\frac12\log(x^2+x+1)
-\frac1{\sqrt3}
\arctan\!\left(\frac{2x+1}{\sqrt3}\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{x\,dx}{1+x+x^2}
=\frac12
\log\!\left(
\frac{R^2+R+1}{R^2-R+1}
\right)
-\frac1{\sqrt3}
\left[
\arctan\!\left(\frac{2R+1}{\sqrt3}\right)
-\arctan\!\left(\frac{-2R+1}{\sqrt3}\right)
\right].
$$
The logarithmic term tends to $0$, while the bracketed term tends to $\pi$. This gives the displayed limit.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The ordinary improper integral
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{x\,dx}{1+x+x^2}
$$
does not exist; the limit in the problem is its Cauchy principal value.


::: pf-proof

As $\abs{x}\to\infty$,
$$
\frac{x}{1+x+x^2}
=\frac1x+O(x^{-2}).
$$
Hence
$$
\int_0^R\frac{x\,dx}{1+x+x^2}
=\log R+O(1)
\longrightarrow+\infty,
$$
whereas
$$
\int_{-R}^0\frac{x\,dx}{1+x+x^2}
=-\log R+O(1)
\longrightarrow-\infty.
$$
The two one-sided improper integrals therefore fail to converge. Their logarithmic divergences cancel only under the symmetric truncation used in step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}.

:::
