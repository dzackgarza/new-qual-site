::: {.problem}
Write down the location and type of each of the singularities of the following functions:

(i) $\dfrac{1}{z^{3}(z-1)^{2}}$ (ii) $\tan z$ (iii) $z\coth z$ (iv) $\dfrac{e^{z} - e}{(1-z)^{3}}$ (v) $\exp(\tan z)$ (vi) $\sinh\dfrac{z}{z^{2} - 1}$ (vii) $\log(1 + e^{z})$ (viii) $\tan(z^{-1})$
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The function
$$
\frac1{z^3(z-1)^2}
$$
has a pole of order $3$ at $z=0$ and a pole of order $2$ at $z=1$, with no other finite singularities.


::: pf-proof

The denominator has a zero of order $3$ at $0$ and a zero of order $2$ at $1$, while the numerator is the nonzero constant $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The singularities of $\tan z$ are simple poles at
$$
\boxed{z=\frac\pi2+k\pi,\qquad k\in\ZZ}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since $\tan z=\sin z/\cos z$, singularities occur at the zeros of $\cos z$. These are exactly $\pi/2+k\pi$. They are simple because $-\sin(\pi/2+k\pi)\neq0$, and the numerator $\sin z$ is nonzero there.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The point $z=0$ is a removable singularity of $z\coth z$, and every
$$
\boxed{z=k\pi i,\qquad k\in\ZZ\setminus\{0\}}
$$
is a simple pole.


::: pf-proof

The zeros of $\sinh z$ are exactly $k\pi i$, and each is simple because $\cosh(k\pi i)=(-1)^k\neq0$. Hence $\coth z=\cosh z/\sinh z$ has a simple pole at each such point. At $0$,
$$
z\coth z
=z\frac{1+O(z^2)}{z+O(z^3)}
=1+O(z^2),
$$
so multiplication by $z$ removes that pole. At every nonzero $k\pi i$, the factor $z$ is nonzero and the pole remains simple.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The function
$$
\frac{e^z-e}{(1-z)^3}
$$
has exactly one finite singularity, a pole of order $2$ at
$$
\boxed{z=1}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The numerator vanishes at $1$, and
$$
e^z-e=e(z-1)+O\bigl((z-1)^2\bigr),
$$
so its zero there is simple. Since
$$
(1-z)^3=-(z-1)^3,
$$
one power cancels and a pole of order $2$ remains. The denominator has no other zeros.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The singularities of $\exp(\tan z)$ are essential singularities at
$$
\boxed{z=\frac\pi2+k\pi,\qquad k\in\ZZ}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Fix $z_0=\pi/2+k\pi$. By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}, $\tan z$ has a simple pole there, so
$$
\tan z=\frac{a_{-1}}{z-z_0}+g(z)
$$
with $a_{-1}\neq0$ and $g$ holomorphic near $z_0$. Therefore
$$
e^{\tan z}
=e^{g(z)}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{a_{-1}^n}{n!}(z-z_0)^{-n}.
$$
Its Laurent series has infinitely many nonzero negative-power terms, so $z_0$ is essential. There are no other finite singularities because $\tan z$ is holomorphic elsewhere and the exponential is entire.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The function
$$
\sinh\!\left(\frac{z}{z^2-1}\right)
$$
has essential singularities exactly at
$$
\boxed{z=1\quad\text{and}\quad z=-1}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The rational function
$$
r(z)=\frac{z}{z^2-1}
$$
has a simple pole at each of $1$ and $-1$. Hence
$$
u(z)=\frac1{r(z)}=\frac{z^2-1}{z}
$$
is holomorphic near either $z_0\in\{1,-1\}$, with a simple zero there. It is therefore a local holomorphic coordinate at $z_0$. In that coordinate,
$$
\sinh(r(z))
=\sinh(1/u)
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{-(2n+1)}}{(2n+1)!},
$$
which has infinitely many negative-power terms. Thus both points are essential singularities. The inner rational function is holomorphic away from $\pm1$, so there are no other finite singularities.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

The intrinsic singularities of $\log(1+e^z)$ are logarithmic branch points at
$$
\boxed{z=(2k+1)\pi i,\qquad k\in\ZZ}.
$$
For the principal logarithm, the chosen branch cut additionally removes the rays
$$
\{x+(2k+1)\pi i:x\geq0\},
$$
whose endpoints are precisely these branch points.


::: pf-proof

The zeros of $1+e^z$ satisfy $e^z=-1$, hence are exactly
$$
z_k=(2k+1)\pi i.
$$
They are simple because $(1+e^z)'=e^z=-1$ at $z_k$. Thus locally
$$
1+e^z=(z-z_k)u(z),
\qquad u(z_k)=-1\neq0,
$$
so any local logarithm has the form
$$
\log(z-z_k)+\log u(z).
$$
The first term acquires $2\pi i$ after one circuit about $z_k$, proving that $z_k$ is a logarithmic branch point.

For the principal branch, $1+e^{x+iy}$ lies on $(-\infty,0]$ exactly when $y=(2k+1)\pi$ and $x\geq0$. Those preimages form the displayed branch cuts; points in their interiors depend on the branch choice and are not additional intrinsic branch points.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The function $\tan(z^{-1})$ has simple poles at
$$
\boxed{
z=\frac{2}{(2k+1)\pi},
\qquad k\in\ZZ},
$$
and $z=0$ is a non-isolated singularity, namely an accumulation point of these poles.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}, $\tan w$ has simple poles at $w=(2k+1)\pi/2$. For $z\neq0$, substitution $w=1/z$ therefore gives poles at
$$
\frac1z=\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2},
$$
which are the displayed points. The map $z\mapsto1/z$ has nonzero derivative at each of them, so the poles remain simple. Their locations tend to $0$ as $\abs{k}\to\infty$. Consequently every punctured neighborhood of $0$ contains poles of $\tan(1/z)$, so $0$ is not an isolated singularity and cannot be classified as an isolated removable singularity, pole, or essential singularity.

:::
