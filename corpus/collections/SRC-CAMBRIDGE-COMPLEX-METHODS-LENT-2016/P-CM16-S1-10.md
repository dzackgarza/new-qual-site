::: {.problem}
Show that

$g(z) = e^z$ maps the strip $\mathcal{S} = \{z : 0 < \operatorname{Im} z < \pi\}$ onto the UHP $\{z : \operatorname{Im} z > 0\}$,

$h(z) = \sin z$ maps the half-strip $\mathcal{H} = \{z : -\frac{\pi}{2} < \operatorname{Re} z < \frac{\pi}{2},\ \operatorname{Im} z > 0\}$ onto the UHP.

Find a conformal map $f \colon \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{S}$.
Hence find a function $\phi(x,y)$ which is harmonic on the half-strip $\mathcal{H}$ with the following limiting values on its boundary $\partial\mathcal{H}$:
$$
\phi(x,y) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{on } \partial\mathcal{H} \text{ in the LHP } (x < 0), \\ 1 & \text{on } \partial\mathcal{H} \text{ in the RHP } (x > 0). \end{cases}
$$

Give $\phi$ as a function of $x$ and $y$.
Is there only one such function?
:::

::: {.solution}

Let
$$
\mathcal S=\{z:0<\operatorname{Im}z<\pi\},
\qquad
\mathcal H=\left\{z:-\frac\pi2<\operatorname{Re}z<\frac\pi2,
\ \operatorname{Im}z>0\right\}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The map $g(z)=e^z$ is a conformal bijection from $\mathcal S$ onto the upper half-plane.


::: pf-proof

If $z=x+iy\in\mathcal S$, then
$$
e^z=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)
$$
has positive imaginary part because $0<y<\pi$. Conversely, if $w=re^{i\theta}$ lies in the upper half-plane, then $r>0$ and $0<\theta<\pi$, and
$$
z=\log r+i\theta\in\mathcal S
$$
satisfies $e^z=w$.
If $e^{z_1}=e^{z_2}$, then $z_1-z_2\in2\pi i\ZZ$; since the imaginary parts of $z_1,z_2$ both lie in $(0,\pi)$, this forces $z_1=z_2$.
Finally $g'(z)=e^z\ne0$, so $g$ is conformal.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The map $h(z)=\sin z$ is a conformal bijection from $\mathcal H$ onto the upper half-plane.


::: pf-proof

For $z=x+iy\in\mathcal H$,
$$
\sin z=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y,
$$
and $\cos x>0$, $\sinh y>0$, so $\operatorname{Im}(\sin z)>0$.

If $\sin z_1=\sin z_2$, then either
$$
z_1-z_2\in2\pi\ZZ
$$
or
$$
z_1+z_2\in\pi+2\pi\ZZ.
$$
The first alternative forces $z_1=z_2$ because $\operatorname{Re}(z_1-z_2)\in(-\pi,\pi)$.
The second is impossible because $\operatorname{Im}(z_1+z_2)>0$.
Thus $h$ is injective.
Also $h'(z)=\cos z$ has zeros only at $\pi/2+k\pi$, none of which lie in $\mathcal H$.

It remains to prove surjectivity.
The image $h(\mathcal H)$ is open in the upper half-plane.
It is also relatively closed there.
Indeed, suppose $\sin z_n\to w$ with $w$ in the upper half-plane and $z_n=x_n+iy_n\in\mathcal H$.
Since
$$
\abs{\sin(x_n+iy_n)}^2=\sin^2x_n+\sinh^2y_n,
$$
the sequence $(y_n)$ is bounded.
A subsequential limit cannot lie on $y=0$ or on $x=\pm\pi/2$, because $\sin z$ is real on each of those boundary pieces whereas $\operatorname{Im}w>0$.
Hence a subsequence converges to a point $z\in\mathcal H$, and continuity gives $\sin z=w$.
Thus $h(\mathcal H)$ is nonempty, open, and closed in the connected upper half-plane, so it is the whole upper half-plane.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Let $L$ be the logarithm branch on the upper half-plane with
$$
L(re^{i\theta})=\log r+i\theta,
\qquad 0<\theta<\pi.
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{f(z)=L(\sin z)}
$$
is a conformal bijection from $\mathcal H$ onto $\mathcal S$.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}, $L$ is the inverse of $g\colon\mathcal S\to\{\operatorname{Im}w>0\}$.
By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}, $h=\sin$ is a conformal bijection from $\mathcal H$ onto that same upper half-plane.
Therefore $f=g^{-1}\circ h=L\circ\sin$ is a conformal bijection $\mathcal H\to\mathcal S$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The harmonic function with the prescribed limiting boundary values is
$$
\phi(z)=1-\frac1\pi\operatorname{Im}f(z)
=1-\frac1\pi\operatorname{Arg}(\sin z),
$$
where $0<\operatorname{Arg}(\sin z)<\pi$ in $\mathcal H$.


::: pf-proof

Since $f$ is holomorphic, $\operatorname{Im}f$ is harmonic, hence so is $\phi$.

On the boundary of $\mathcal H$ with $x>0$, the function $\sin z$ approaches the positive real axis: on the bottom edge $\sin x>0$, and on the right vertical edge $\sin(\pi/2+iy)=\cosh y>0$.
Therefore $\operatorname{Arg}(\sin z)\to0$ and $\phi\to1$.

On the boundary with $x<0$, the function $\sin z$ approaches the negative real axis: on the bottom edge $\sin x<0$, and on the left vertical edge $\sin(-\pi/2+iy)=-\cosh y<0$.
Approaching from the upper half-plane gives $\operatorname{Arg}(\sin z)\to\pi$, and hence $\phi\to0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

In terms of $x$ and $y$ alone,
$$
\boxed{
\phi(x,y)=\frac12+\frac1\pi
\tan^{-1}\!\left(
\frac{\sin x\cosh y}{\cos x\sinh y}
\right)},
\qquad
-\frac\pi2<x<\frac\pi2,
\quad y>0,
$$
where $\tan^{-1}$ takes values in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$.


::: pf-proof

Write
$$
\sin(x+iy)=a+ib,
\qquad
a=\sin x\cosh y,
\qquad
b=\cos x\sinh y.
$$
On $\mathcal H$ one has $b>0$.
If $\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(a+ib)\in(0,\pi)$, then
$$
\theta=\frac\pi2-\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac ab\right).
$$
Substituting this identity into step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} gives the displayed formula.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The boundary data do not determine a unique harmonic function on the unbounded half-strip.


::: pf-proof

The function
$$
u(x,y)=\cos x\sinh y=\operatorname{Im}(\sin(x+iy))
$$
is harmonic and nonzero on $\mathcal H$.
It tends to $0$ on every finite boundary point of $\mathcal H$: on $y=0$ because $\sinh y=0$, and on $x=\pm\pi/2$ because $\cos x=0$.
Therefore, for every $C\in\RR$,
$$
\phi_C(x,y)=\phi(x,y)+C\cos x\sinh y
$$
is harmonic on $\mathcal H$ and has exactly the same prescribed limiting boundary values.
Additional growth or boundedness conditions would be needed for uniqueness.

:::
