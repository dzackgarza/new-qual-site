::: {.problem}
*Starred problem.*

Suppose that $f(x)$ has period $2\pi$ and let $g(x) = f(x)e^{-a\abs{x}}$ where $a > 0$. Show that the Fourier transform of $g$ is given by
$$
\widetilde{g}(k) = \frac{F(k - ia)}{1 - e^{-2\pi i(k - ia)}} - \frac{F(k + ia)}{1 - e^{-2\pi i(k + ia)}}
$$
where $F(k) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} f(x)e^{-ikx} \, \mathrm{d}x$.

Assuming that $F$ is analytic, sketch the locations of the singularities of $\widetilde{g}$ in the complex $k$-plane. Further assuming that $F$ decays sufficiently quickly at infinity, use the Fourier inversion theorem and a suitable contour to show that
$$
g(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} F(n)e^{(in - a)x}
$$
for $x > 0$ and derive a similar result when $x < 0$.

Deduce that
$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} F(n)e^{inx}.
$$

[This shows how the Fourier transform representation of a function reduces to a Fourier series if the function is periodic.]
:::

::: {.solution}

Write
$$
\widetilde g(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx,
\qquad
F(z)=\int_0^{2\pi}f(x)e^{-izx}\,dx.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The Fourier transform of $g$ is
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde g(k)
=
\frac{F(k-ia)}{1-e^{-2\pi i(k-ia)}}
-
\frac{F(k+ia)}{1-e^{-2\pi i(k+ia)}}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Split the transform at $0$. On the positive half-line, periodicity of $f$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty f(x)e^{-ax}e^{-ikx}\,dx
&=
\sum_{m=0}^\infty
\int_{2\pi m}^{2\pi(m+1)}
f(x)e^{-ax}e^{-ikx}\,dx \\
&=
\sum_{m=0}^\infty
e^{-2\pi i m(k-ia)}
\int_0^{2\pi}
f(y)e^{-i(k-ia)y}\,dy \\
&=
\frac{F(k-ia)}
{1-e^{-2\pi i(k-ia)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $a>0$, so the geometric series converges for real $k$.

On the negative half-line, decompose into the intervals
$[-2\pi(m+1),-2\pi m]$ and put $x=y-2\pi(m+1)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\infty}^0 f(x)e^{ax}e^{-ikx}\,dx
&=
\sum_{m=0}^\infty
e^{2\pi i(m+1)(k+ia)}
\int_0^{2\pi}
f(y)e^{-i(k+ia)y}\,dy \\
&=
\frac{e^{2\pi i(k+ia)}}{1-e^{2\pi i(k+ia)}}F(k+ia) \\
&=
-\frac{F(k+ia)}
{1-e^{-2\pi i(k+ia)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding the two half-line integrals proves the formula.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The possible singularities of the meromorphic continuation of
$\widetilde g$ lie at
$$
\boxed{k=n+ia\quad\text{and}\quad k=n-ia,
\qquad n\in\ZZ}.
$$
At a non-removable singularity the poles are simple, with residues
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{k=n+ia}\widetilde g(k)
=\frac{F(n)}{2\pi i},
\qquad
\operatorname{Res}_{k=n-ia}\widetilde g(k)
=-\frac{F(n)}{2\pi i}.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}, a denominator vanishes precisely when
$$
e^{-2\pi i(k\mp ia)}=1,
$$
equivalently when $k=n\pm ia$ for some $n\in\ZZ$.
The derivative of
$1-e^{-2\pi i(k\mp ia)}$ at such a point is $2\pi i$.
This gives the displayed residues. If $F(n)=0$, the corresponding
singularity may be removable.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

For $x>0$,
$$
\boxed{
g(x)=\frac1{2\pi}
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
F(n)e^{(in-a)x}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Fourier inversion gives
$$
g(x)=\frac1{2\pi}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\widetilde g(k)e^{ikx}\,dk.
$$
For $x>0$, close the contour in the upper half-plane. Under the stated
decay hypothesis, an exhausting sequence of contours can be chosen whose
closing arcs contribute $0$ in the limit. By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}, the enclosed poles
are $k=n+ia$, and
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{k=n+ia}
\bigl(\widetilde g(k)e^{ikx}\bigr)
=
\frac{F(n)}{2\pi i}e^{i(n+ia)x}
=
\frac{F(n)}{2\pi i}e^{(in-a)x}.
$$
The residue theorem therefore yields
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\widetilde g(k)e^{ikx}\,dk
=
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
F(n)e^{(in-a)x},
$$
and Fourier inversion gives the claim.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

For $x<0$,
$$
\boxed{
g(x)=\frac1{2\pi}
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
F(n)e^{(in+a)x}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

For $x<0$, close the Fourier-inversion contour in the lower half-plane.
The contour is clockwise, and step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{k=n-ia}
\bigl(\widetilde g(k)e^{ikx}\bigr)
=
-\frac{F(n)}{2\pi i}e^{i(n-ia)x}
=
-\frac{F(n)}{2\pi i}e^{(in+a)x}.
$$
Hence the residue theorem gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\widetilde g(k)e^{ikx}\,dk
=
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
F(n)e^{(in+a)x},
$$
because the clockwise orientation contributes the second minus sign.
Fourier inversion proves the claim.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The Fourier series of $f$ is
$$
\boxed{
f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
F(n)e^{inx}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

For $x>0$, $g(x)=f(x)e^{-ax}$, so step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} gives the displayed identity
after multiplication by $e^{ax}$. For $x<0$, the same identity follows
from step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} because $g(x)=f(x)e^{ax}$.

The series on the right is $2\pi$-periodic. Thus the equality extends to
every real $x$: for any $x$, choose an integer $m$ with $x+2\pi m>0$,
apply the already proved positive-$x$ identity there, and use the
$2\pi$-periodicity of both sides.

:::

series of $f$.
