::: {.problem}
Let
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \abs{x} < \frac{1}{2}a, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}; \end{cases} \qquad g(x) = \begin{cases} a - \abs{x} & \abs{x} < a, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
$$

Show that
$$
\widetilde{f}(k) = \frac{2}{k}\sin\frac{ak}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad \widetilde{g}(k) = \frac{4}{k^{2}}\sin^{2}\frac{ak}{2}.
$$

Verify by contour integration the inversion formula for $f(x)$, including the value at $x = \frac{1}{2}a$. What is the convolution of $f$ with itself?
:::

::: {.solution}

Use the Fourier-transform convention
$$
\widetilde h(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx,
\qquad
h(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde h(k)e^{ikx}\,dk
$$
at points where the inversion formula applies.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The Fourier transform of $f$ is
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde f(k)=\frac2k\sin\frac{ak}{2}},
$$
with the value at $k=0$ understood by continuity as $\widetilde f(0)=a$.


::: pf-proof

By definition,
$$
\widetilde f(k)
=\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}e^{-ikx}\,dx.
$$
For $k\neq0$ this is
$$
\frac{e^{ika/2}-e^{-ika/2}}{ik}
=\frac2k\sin\frac{ak}{2}.
$$
At $k=0$, the defining integral is $a$, which is also the limit of the displayed expression.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The Fourier transform of $g$ is
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde g(k)
=\frac4{k^2}\sin^2\frac{ak}{2}},
$$
with the value at $k=0$ understood by continuity as $\widetilde g(0)=a^2$.


::: pf-proof

The function $g$ is even, so
$$
\widetilde g(k)
=2\int_0^a(a-x)\cos(kx)\,dx.
$$
Integration by parts gives
$$
\int_0^a(a-x)\cos(kx)\,dx
=\frac{1-\cos(ak)}{k^2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\widetilde g(k)
=\frac{2(1-\cos ak)}{k^2}
=\frac4{k^2}\sin^2\frac{ak}{2}.
$$
At $k=0$, the defining integral is the area of the triangle $g$, namely $a^2$, and the same value follows by taking the limit.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

For real $u\neq0$,
$$
\operatorname{PV}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iku}}k\,dk
=i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(u),
$$
whereas the principal value is $0$ when $u=0$.


::: pf-proof

Suppose first that $u>0$. Integrate $e^{iuz}/z$ over the upper-half-plane contour consisting of $[-R,-\varepsilon]$, a clockwise semicircular indentation of radius $\varepsilon$ above $0$, $[\varepsilon,R]$, and the large upper semicircle. The contour encloses no pole. The large-arc integral tends to $0$ by the same Jordan-lemma estimate used for the Dirichlet integral, while the small clockwise arc tends to $-i\pi$. Hence
$$
\operatorname{PV}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iku}}k\,dk
=i\pi.
$$
For $u<0$, close the corresponding contour in the lower half-plane; the same argument gives $-i\pi$. If $u=0$, the integrand is $1/k$, whose symmetric principal value is $0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Fourier inversion gives
$$
\boxed{
\frac1{2\pi}
\operatorname{PV}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\widetilde f(k)e^{ikx}\,dk
=
\begin{cases}
1,&\abs{x}<a/2,\\[2mm]
1/2,&\abs{x}=a/2,\\[2mm]
0,&\abs{x}>a/2.
\end{cases}}
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref},
$$
\widetilde f(k)e^{ikx}
=\frac{
e^{ik(x+a/2)}-e^{ik(x-a/2)}
}{ik}.
$$
Therefore the inverse transform equals
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}
\operatorname{PV}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{
e^{ik(x+a/2)}-e^{ik(x-a/2)}
}{k}\,dk.
$$
Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\frac12
\left[
\operatorname{sgn}\left(x+\frac a2\right)
-\operatorname{sgn}\left(x-\frac a2\right)
\right],
$$
with $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$ for the principal-value contribution. This is $1$ inside the interval, $0$ outside it, and $1/2$ at either endpoint. Thus inversion recovers $f$ at every continuity point and the midpoint of its jump at $x=\pm a/2$, in particular the requested value $1/2$ at $x=a/2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The convolution of $f$ with itself is
$$
\boxed{
(f*f)(x)
=
\begin{cases}
a-\abs{x},&\abs{x}<a,\\
0,&\abs{x}\geq a,
\end{cases}}
$$
so $f*f=g$.


::: pf-proof

By definition,
$$
(f*f)(x)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(x-t)\,dt.
$$
The integrand is $1$ exactly when
$$
t\in[-a/2,a/2]
\cap[x-a/2,x+a/2].
$$
Thus the convolution is the length of this intersection. The length is $a-\abs{x}$ for $\abs{x}<a$ and $0$ for $\abs{x}\geq a$, which is exactly $g(x)$.

:::
