::: {.problem}
Find the first two non-vanishing coefficients in the Taylor expansion about the origin of the following functions, assuming principal branches when there is a choice. You may make use of standard series expansions for $\log(1 + z)$, etc.
$$
z/\log(1 + z), \qquad (\cos z)^{1/2} - 1, \qquad \log(1 + e^{z}), \qquad e^{e^{z}}.
$$

State the range of values of $z$ for which each series converges.

How would your answers differ if you assumed branches different from the principal branch?
:::

::: {.solution}

All Taylor series below are centered at $z=0$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

For the principal logarithm,
$$
\boxed{
\frac{z}{\log(1+z)}
=1+\frac12z-\frac1{12}z^2+O(z^3)},
$$
so the first two nonzero coefficients are $1$ and $1/2$, and the radius of convergence is $1$.


::: pf-proof

The standard logarithm series gives
$$
\log(1+z)
=z\left(1-\frac z2+\frac{z^2}{3}+O(z^3)\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{z}{\log(1+z)}
=\left(1-\frac z2+\frac{z^2}{3}+O(z^3)\right)^{-1}
=1+\frac z2-\frac{z^2}{12}+O(z^3).
$$
On $\abs{z}<1$, the principal logarithm is holomorphic and vanishes only at $z=0$, where the displayed quotient has a removable singularity. Hence the quotient is holomorphic throughout $\abs{z}<1$. At $z=-1$ it cannot extend holomorphically: along $z=-1+t$ with $t\downarrow0$ the quotient tends to $0$, but
$$
\frac{(-1+t)/\log t}{t}
=\frac{-1+t}{t\log t}
$$
has unbounded magnitude, so a derivative at $z=-1$ cannot exist after assigning the limiting value $0$. Thus the Taylor radius is exactly $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

For the principal square root,
$$
\boxed{
(\cos z)^{1/2}-1
=-\frac14z^2-\frac1{96}z^4+O(z^6)},
$$
so the first two nonzero coefficients are $-1/4$ at degree $2$ and $-1/96$ at degree $4$, and the radius of convergence is $\pi/2$.


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
\cos z=1-\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{z^4}{24}+O(z^6)
$$
and
$$
(1+u)^{1/2}=1+\frac u2-\frac{u^2}{8}+O(u^3),
$$
substitution gives
$$
(\cos z)^{1/2}
=1-\frac{z^2}{4}
+\left(\frac1{48}-\frac1{32}\right)z^4
+O(z^6)
=1-\frac{z^2}{4}-\frac{z^4}{96}+O(z^6).
$$
The function $\cos z$ has no zero in $\abs{z}<\pi/2$, so on that simply connected disk it has a unique holomorphic square root taking the value $1$ at the origin. At $z=\pm\pi/2$, $\cos z$ has a simple zero. A square of a holomorphic function can only have zeros of even order, so no holomorphic square root extends across either point. Hence the radius is exactly $\pi/2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

For the principal logarithm,
$$
\boxed{
\log(1+e^z)
=\log2+\frac12z+\frac18z^2+O(z^3)},
$$
so the first two nonzero coefficients are $\log2$ and $1/2$, and the radius of convergence is $\pi$.


::: pf-proof

Write
$$
1+e^z
=2\left(1+\frac z2+\frac{z^2}{4}+O(z^3)\right).
$$
Using $\log(1+u)=u-u^2/2+O(u^3)$ gives
$$
\log(1+e^z)
=\log2
+\left(\frac z2+\frac{z^2}{4}\right)
-\frac12\left(\frac z2\right)^2
+O(z^3)
=\log2+\frac z2+\frac{z^2}{8}+O(z^3).
$$
The zeros of $1+e^z$ are
$$
z=(2k+1)\pi i,
\qquad k\in\ZZ.
$$
Hence there are no zeros in $\abs{z}<\pi$. On this simply connected disk, $1+e^z$ therefore has a holomorphic logarithm agreeing at the origin with the principal branch. The nearest zeros, $\pm\pi i$, lie at distance $\pi$ and are simple. A holomorphic logarithm cannot extend across a zero of its exponential, so the Taylor radius is exactly $\pi$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The entire function $e^{e^z}$ has expansion
$$
\boxed{
e^{e^z}=e+ez+ez^2+O(z^3)},
$$
so its first two nonzero coefficients are $e$ and $e$, and its Taylor series converges for every $z\in\CC$.


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
e^z-1=z+\frac{z^2}{2}+O(z^3),
$$
one has
$$
e^{e^z}
=e\exp(e^z-1)
=e\left(1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{z^2}{2}+O(z^3)\right)
=e(1+z+z^2+O(z^3)).
$$
Both exponential maps are entire, so their composition is entire and its Taylor radius is infinite.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

For a nonprincipal logarithm branch in part (i), indexed near $z=0$ by
$$
L_k(1+z)=\log(1+z)+2\pi i k,
\qquad k\in\ZZ\setminus\{0\},
$$
the first two nonzero terms become
$$
\boxed{
\frac{z}{L_k(1+z)}
=\frac{z}{2\pi i k}
-\frac{z^2}{(2\pi i k)^2}
+O(z^3)},
$$
while the radius remains $1$.


::: pf-proof

Put $c=2\pi i k\ne0$. Then
$$
L_k(1+z)=c+z-\frac{z^2}{2}+O(z^3),
$$
and therefore
$$
\frac{z}{L_k(1+z)}
=z\left(\frac1c-\frac z{c^2}+O(z^2)\right).
$$
The logarithmic branch point at $z=-1$ is unchanged by adding the constant $2\pi i k$, so the convergence radius is still $1$.
Indeed, for $\abs{z}<1$ one has $\operatorname{Re}(1+z)>0$, so the principal value of $\operatorname{Im}\log(1+z)$ lies in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Hence $L_k(1+z)$ cannot vanish when $k\ne0$, and there is no nearer pole.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

For the other square-root branch in part (ii), the series is
$$
\boxed{
-(\cos z)^{1/2}-1
=-2+\frac14z^2+\frac1{96}z^4+O(z^6)},
$$
with the same radius $\pi/2$.


::: pf-proof

On a connected neighborhood of the origin, the only two holomorphic square roots of $\cos z$ differ by a sign. Negating the expansion from step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} and then subtracting $1$ gives the displayed series. The zero set of $\cos z$ is unchanged, so the convergence radius is unchanged.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

For a different logarithm branch in part (iii), the series differs only in its constant term:
$$
\boxed{
L_k(1+e^z)
=\log2+2\pi i k+\frac12z+\frac18z^2+O(z^3)},
\qquad k\in\ZZ,
$$
and its radius remains $\pi$. Part (iv) has no branch choice.


::: pf-proof

Any two local holomorphic logarithms of the nonvanishing function $1+e^z$ differ by an integral multiple of $2\pi i$. Hence changing the branch adds only the constant $2\pi i k$ and leaves every positive-degree Taylor coefficient unchanged. The nearest zeros of $1+e^z$ remain $\pm\pi i$, so the radius remains $\pi$. The function $e^{e^z}$ is single-valued and entire, so there is no branch to choose in part (iv).

:::
