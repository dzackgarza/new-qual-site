::: {.problem}
Use Corollary 2 of Lagrange’s Theorem to prove that the order of $U(n)$ is even when $n > 2$.
:::

::: {.solution}

The exercise is Gallian, *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* (7th ed.), Chapter 7 exercise 18,
printed p. 150, and is item 20 of the retained Chapter 7 solution outline
(`assets/attachments/Ch7Sltns.pdf`). Its argument is kept, with the congruence
that the outline writes as an equality stated correctly.

Throughout, $n>2$ is an integer and $U(n)$ is the group of positive integers less than $n$
and relatively prime to $n$, under multiplication modulo $n$ (printed p. 44).

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #member}

$n-1\in U(n)$.

::: pf-proof

$1\le n-1<n$ because $n>2$, so $n-1$ is a positive integer less than $n$. Any common
divisor $d$ of $n-1$ and $n$ divides $n-(n-1)=1$, so $\gcd(n-1,n)=1$ and $n-1$ is
relatively prime to $n$. Hence $n-1\in U(n)$ by the definition of $U(n)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #order-two}

The order of $n-1$ in $U(n)$ is $2$.

::: pf-proof

$(n-1)^{2}=n^{2}-2n+1=n(n-2)+1\equiv1\pmod{n}$, so the class of $n-1$ has square the
identity of $U(n)$; and $n-1\not\equiv1\pmod n$, since $n$ divides $(n-1)-1=n-2$ only if
$n\le2$, which is excluded. Hence the order of $n-1$ divides $2$ and is not $1$, so it
is $2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #even}

$\cardinality{U(n)}$ is even.

::: pf-proof

$U(n)$ is a finite group and $n-1$ is one of its elements, of order $2$ by step
[](#order-two){.pf-ref}. Corollary 2 of Lagrange's Theorem says the order
of an element divides the order of the group, so $2$ divides $\cardinality{U(n)}$, that is
$\cardinality{U(n)}=2k$ for some positive integer $k$: the order is even.

:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.remark}
Corollary 2 of Lagrange’s Theorem states that the order of an element of a finite group divides the order of the group.
:::
