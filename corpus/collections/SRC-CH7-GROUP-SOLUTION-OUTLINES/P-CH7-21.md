::: {.problem}
Suppose $G$ is a finite group of order $n$, and $m$ is relatively prime to $n$. If $g \in G$ and $g^{m} = e$, prove that $g = e$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The book prints a selected answer to this exercise — **Gallian, *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* (7th ed.), *Selected Answers*, printed p. A10** (`tmp/sources/Gallian-Contemporary-Abstract-Algebra-7e.pdf`, PDF p. 599): "Use Corollary 4 of Lagrange's Theorem (Theorem 7.1) together with Theorem 0.2." That is the plan carried out below, with both divisibilities and the conclusion written out. Write $e$ for the identity of $G$ and $k$ for the order of $g$, the least positive integer with $g^{k}=e$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #divides-m}
$k$ divides $m$.

::: pf-proof
Divide: $m=qk+r$ with integers $q\ge0$ and $0\le r<k$. Then $e=g^{m}=(g^{k})^{q}g^{r}=g^{r}$, so $r$ is a nonnegative exponent killing $g$; by the minimality of $k$ among the positive such exponents, $r=0$ and $k$ divides $m$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #divides-n}
$k$ divides $n=\cardinality{G}$.

::: pf-proof
The powers of $g$ form the cyclic subgroup $\langle g\rangle$, of order $k$, and Lagrange's theorem gives that $k$ divides $\cardinality{G}=n$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #order-one}
$k=1$.

::: pf-proof
By steps [](#divides-m){.pf-ref} and [](#divides-n){.pf-ref} the positive integer $k$ divides both $m$ and $n$, hence divides their greatest common divisor, which is $1$ by hypothesis. The only positive divisor of $1$ is $1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #answer}
$\boxed{g=e}$.

::: pf-proof
By definition of the order, $g^{k}=e$, and step [](#order-one){.pf-ref} turns this into $g^{1}=e$, that is $g=e$, which is the assertion of the exercise.
:::
