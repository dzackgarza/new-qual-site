::: {.problem}
Suppose that $K$ is a proper subgroup of $H$ and $H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$. If $\cardinality{K} = 42$ and $\cardinality{G} = 420$, what are the possible orders of $H$?
:::

::: {.solution}
The exercise is Gallian, *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* (7th ed.), Chapter 7 Exercise 14, and is entry "# 16" of the retained Chapter 7 solution outline (`assets/attachments/Ch7Sltns.pdf`). The outline derives the candidate list $42,84,210,420$ from Lagrange's theorem and excludes $42$ and $420$ by properness; that establishes only that $h=\cardinality{H}$ lies in $\theset{84,210}$, and it does not show that either value occurs. The existence argument below, including the order formula for powers in a cyclic group, is therefore supplied here rather than transcribed from the outline. The divisor enumeration is also proved explicitly.

Write $h=\cardinality{H}$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #candidates}
The order $h$ is one of $42,84,210,420$.

::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #divisibility}
The integer $h$ satisfies $42\mid h$ and $h\mid420$.

::: pf-proof
Since $K$ is a subgroup of the finite group $H$, Lagrange's theorem gives $\cardinality{K}\mid h$, that is, $42\mid h$. Since $H$ is a subgroup of the finite group $G$, it likewise gives $h\mid\cardinality{G}=420$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #enumeration}
Writing $h=42d$, the quotient $d$ is a positive divisor of $10$, so $d\in\theset{1,2,5,10}$.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#divisibility){.pf-ref} supplies a positive integer $d$ with $h=42d$. Substitution into $h\mid420$ gives $42d\mid42\cdot10$, so $d\mid10$. Conversely, every positive divisor $d$ of $10$ gives $42d\mid420$. The positive divisors of $10$ are exactly $1,2,5,10$; multiplying them by $42$ gives the four displayed candidates.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #properness}
Neither $h=42$ nor $h=420$ is compatible with the proper inclusions.

::: pf-proof
If $h=42=\cardinality{K}$, then the finite subset $K$ of $H$ has the same cardinality as $H$, so $K=H$, contrary to $K$ being a proper subgroup. If $h=420=\cardinality{G}$, then similarly $H=G$, contrary to $H$ being a proper subgroup. Thus the first and last candidates from step [](#candidates){.pf-ref} are impossible.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #cyclic-order}
Let $G=\langle g\rangle$ be a cyclic group of order $n$. For every integer $k$, the subgroup $\langle g^{k}\rangle$ has order $n/\gcd(n,k)$.

::: pf-proof
Let $d=\gcd(n,k)$ and write $k=dq$ and $n=dm$, so that $\gcd(m,q)=1$. Then $(g^{k})^{m}=g^{dmq}=(g^{n})^{q}=e$, so the order of $g^{k}$ divides $m$. Conversely, if $(g^{k})^{r}=e$, then $n\mid kr$, that is, $dm\mid dqr$, hence $m\mid qr$; since $\gcd(m,q)=1$, it follows that $m\mid r$. Therefore the order of $g^{k}$ is exactly $m=n/d$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #order-84}
There are a group $G$ of order $420$, a subgroup $K$ of order $42$ and a subgroup $H$ of order $84$ such that $K$ is a proper subgroup of $H$ and $H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$.

::: pf-proof
Take $G=\langle g\rangle$ to be a cyclic group of order $420$; the additive group $\ZZ/420\ZZ$ is an example, and we write its group law multiplicatively. Let $K=\langle g^{10}\rangle$ and $H=\langle g^{5}\rangle$. By step [](#cyclic-order){.pf-ref}, $\cardinality{K}=420/\gcd(420,10)=420/10=42$ and $\cardinality{H}=420/\gcd(420,5)=420/5=84$. Since $g^{10}=(g^{5})^{2}$, every power of $g^{10}$ is a power of $g^{5}$, so $K\subseteq H$; as $42<84$, the inclusion is proper. Finally $84<420$, so $H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #order-210}
There are a group $G$ of order $420$, a subgroup $K$ of order $42$ and a subgroup $H$ of order $210$ such that $K$ is a proper subgroup of $H$ and $H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$.

::: pf-proof
Again take $G=\langle g\rangle$ cyclic of order $420$, and let $K=\langle g^{10}\rangle$ and $H=\langle g^{2}\rangle$. By step [](#cyclic-order){.pf-ref}, $\cardinality{K}=420/\gcd(420,10)=420/10=42$ and $\cardinality{H}=420/\gcd(420,2)=420/2=210$. Since $g^{10}=(g^{2})^{5}$, we have $K\subseteq H$, proper because $42<210$; and $210<420$, so $H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The possible orders of $H$ are $\boxed{84\text{ and }210}$.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#candidates){.pf-ref} and [](#properness){.pf-ref} show that no order other than $84$ and $210$ is compatible with the hypotheses. Steps [](#order-84){.pf-ref} and [](#order-210){.pf-ref} show conversely that each of $84$ and $210$ is attained. Hence the possible orders are exactly $84$ and $210$.
:::
