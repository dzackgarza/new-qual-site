::: {.problem}
Let $G$ be a group of order $60$. What are the possible orders for the subgroups of $G$?
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #necessary}

Every subgroup order divides $60$.

::: pf-proof

By Lagrange's theorem the order of a subgroup of a finite group divides the
order of the group, so $|H|$ is a divisor of $|G|=60$. Hence only the twelve
numbers $1,2,3,4,5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60$ can occur.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #sufficient}

Each divisor of $60$ is the order of a subgroup of some group of order $60$,
namely of $\ZZ_{60}$.

::: pf-proof

Let $d$ divide $60$ and put $m=60/d$, so that $m\in\ZZ_{60}$. The order of $m$
in the additive group $\ZZ_{60}$ is $60/\gcd(60,m)=60/m=d$, since
$\gcd(60,60/d)=60/d$. So $\langle m\rangle=\{0,m,2m,\dots,(d-1)m\}$ is a
subgroup of order $d$. In particular $\langle 60\rangle=\{0\}$ has order $1$
and $\langle 1\rangle$ has order $60$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #answer}

The possible orders are exactly the twelve divisors of $60$.

::: pf-proof

$60=2^{2}\cdot 3\cdot 5$ has $(2+1)(1+1)(1+1)=12$ divisors, listed in step
[](#necessary){.pf-ref}; each is attained in a group of order $60$ by step
[](#sufficient){.pf-ref}. For a fixed group $G$ of order $60$ not every
divisor need occur — $A_{5}$ has no subgroup of order $30$ — so the question is
answered as the set of orders Lagrange's theorem permits and that some group of
order $60$ realises.

:::
