::: {.problem}
Show that every group of order at most $5$ is commutative.
Give an example of a noncommutative group of order $6$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $G$ be a finite group with $\abs G\leq5$.

<1>1. If $\abs G\in\{1,2,3,5\}$, then $G$ is abelian.

::: {.proof}
The group of order $1$ is trivial. If $\abs G=p$ is prime, choose any nonidentity element $g\in G$. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of $g$ divides $p$ and is greater than $1$, so it equals $p$. Hence
$$
G=\langle g\rangle
$$
is cyclic and therefore abelian. This applies to $p=2,3,5$.
:::

<1>2. Every group of order $4$ is abelian.

::: {.proof}
If $G$ contains an element of order $4$, then that element generates $G$, so $G$ is cyclic.

Otherwise every nonidentity element has order $2$, by Lagrange's theorem. Hence every $x\in G$ satisfies $x^{-1}=x$. For arbitrary $a,b\in G$,
$$
ab=(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba.
$$
Thus $G$ is abelian.
:::

<1>3. Every group of order at most $5$ is commutative.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>2 cover all possible orders $1,2,3,4,5$.
:::

<1>4. The symmetric group $S_3$ is a noncommutative group of order $6$.

::: {.proof}
There are $3!=6$ permutations of three letters, so $\abs{S_3}=6$. Moreover,
$$
(12)(23)=(123)
\qquad\text{whereas}\qquad
(23)(12)=(132),
$$
so multiplication is not commutative.
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>3 proves the first assertion, and step <1>4 supplies the requested example of order $6$.
:::
