::: {.problem}
Let $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
If $$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=0$$ for all rational numbers $a<b$, prove that $f(x)=0$ for almost all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.
:::


::: {.solution}
Define
\[
F(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt,\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
\]
Since $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, $F$ is absolutely continuous on every bounded interval and therefore continuous on $\mathbb R$.

<1>1. $F$ is constant on $\mathbb Q$.
::: {.proof}
If $a<b$ are rational, then by hypothesis
\[
F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=0.
\]
Thus $F(a)=F(b)$ for every pair of rational numbers $a,b$. Hence there is a constant $c$ such that
\[
F(q)=c\qquad(q\in\mathbb Q).
\]
:::

<1>2. $F$ is constant on $\mathbb R$.
::: {.proof}
Fix $x\in\mathbb R$ and choose rationals $q_n\to x$. By continuity of $F$,
\[
F(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F(q_n)=c.
\]
Hence $F\equiv c$ on $\mathbb R$. Since $F(0)=0$, in fact $c=0$.
:::

<1>3. Therefore $f=0$ almost everywhere.
::: {.proof}
By the fundamental theorem of calculus for Lebesgue integrals,
\[
F'(x)=f(x)
\]
for almost every $x\in\mathbb R$. But $F$ is constant by <1>2, so $F'(x)=0$ wherever the derivative exists. Therefore
\[
f(x)=0
\]
for almost every $x\in\mathbb R$.
:::
:::
