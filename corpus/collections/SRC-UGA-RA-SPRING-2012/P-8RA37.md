::: problem
Let $V$ be the vector space of absolutely continuous functions on $[0,1]$ endowed with the norm
\[
\|f\|:=\int_0^1|f(t)|\,dt+\int_0^1|f'(t)|\,dt,\qquad f\in V.
\]
Show that $(V,\|\cdot\|)$ is a Banach space.
You do not need to prove that $\|\cdot\|$ is a norm.
:::


::: solution
<1>1. Show that the norm controls the value at the left endpoint.
::: proof
Let $f\in V$. For every $x\in[0,1]$, absolute continuity gives
\[
f(0)=f(x)-\int_0^x f'(t)\,dt.
\]
Hence
\[
|f(0)|\le |f(x)|+\int_0^1|f'(t)|\,dt.
\]
Integrating this inequality in $x$ over $[0,1]$ yields
\[
|f(0)|
\le
\|f\|_{L^1}+\|f'\|_{L^1}
=
\|f\|.
\]
:::

<1>2. Extract limits of the initial values and derivatives.
::: proof
Let $(f_n)$ be Cauchy in $V$. Then
\[
\|f_n'-f_m'\|_1\le \|f_n-f_m\|,
\]
so $(f_n')$ is Cauchy in $L^1([0,1])$. Since $L^1$ is complete, there exists $g\in L^1([0,1])$ such that
\[
f_n'\to g
\quad\text{in }L^1.
\]

By Step 1,
\[
|f_n(0)-f_m(0)|
\le
\|f_n-f_m\|,
\]
so $(f_n(0))$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb R$. Let
\[
f_n(0)\to c.
\]
:::

<1>3. Reconstruct the candidate limit.
::: proof
Define
\[
f(x):=c+\int_0^x g(t)\,dt.
\]
Then $f$ is absolutely continuous on $[0,1]$ and
\[
f'=g
\]
almost everywhere.

Also, since
\[
f_n(x)=f_n(0)+\int_0^x f_n'(t)\,dt,
\]
we have for every $x\in[0,1]$,
\[
|f_n(x)-f(x)|
\le
|f_n(0)-c|+\|f_n'-g\|_1.
\]
Therefore
\[
\|f_n-f\|_\infty
\le
|f_n(0)-c|+\|f_n'-g\|_1
\longrightarrow0.
\]
In particular,
\[
\|f_n-f\|_1\to0.
\]
Together with $f_n'\to g=f'$ in $L^1$, this gives
\[
\|f_n-f\|
=
\|f_n-f\|_1+\|f_n'-f'\|_1
\longrightarrow0.
\]
Thus every Cauchy sequence in $V$ converges in $V$, so
\[
\boxed{(V,\|\cdot\|)\text{ is a Banach space}.}
\]
:::
:::
