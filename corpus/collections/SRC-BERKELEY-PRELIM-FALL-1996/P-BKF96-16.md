::: {.problem}
Let
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}
2&-1&0\\
-1&2&-1\\
0&-1&2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Show that every real matrix $B$ satisfying $AB=BA$ has the form
\[
B=aI+bA+cA^2
\]
for some $a,b,c\in\mathbb R$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The characteristic polynomial of $A$ has the three distinct roots
$$
\lambda_1=2-\sqrt2,
\qquad
\lambda_2=2,
\qquad
\lambda_3=2+\sqrt2.
$$

::: {.proof}
Set
$$
t=2-\lambda.
$$
Then
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)
=
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
t&-1&0\\
-1&t&-1\\
0&-1&t
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Expanding along the first row,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A-\lambda I)
&=
t(t^2-1)-t\\
&=
t(t^2-2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
t=0,\ \pm\sqrt2,
$$
which gives the three displayed values of $\lambda$.
:::

<1>2. The space $\RR^3$ is the direct sum of the three one-dimensional
eigenspaces
$$
E_{\lambda_1}\oplus E_{\lambda_2}\oplus E_{\lambda_3}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 gives three distinct eigenvalues of a $3\times3$ matrix. Their
eigenvectors are linearly independent, so the three eigenspaces are each
one-dimensional and their direct sum is all of $\RR^3$.
:::

<1>3. If $AB=BA$, then every eigenspace $E_{\lambda_j}$ is
$B$-invariant.

::: {.proof}
If
$$
Av=\lambda_jv,
$$
then
$$
A(Bv)
=
B(Av)
=
\lambda_jBv.
$$
Hence
$$
Bv\in E_{\lambda_j}.
$$
:::

<1>4. There are real numbers $\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3$ such that
$$
Bv=\beta_jv
$$
for every
$$
v\in E_{\lambda_j}.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>2, each $E_{\lambda_j}$ is one-dimensional. Step <1>3 shows
that $B$ restricts to an endomorphism of that line, so it acts there by a
scalar. Since both the line and $B$ are real, that scalar is real.
:::

<1>5. There is a real polynomial
$$
p(t)=a+bt+ct^2
$$
such that
$$
p(\lambda_j)=\beta_j
\qquad
j=1,2,3.
$$

::: {.proof}
The three numbers $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$ are distinct by step
<1>1. Lagrange interpolation therefore gives a unique polynomial of degree
at most $2$ taking the prescribed real values $\beta_j$ at those three
points. Its coefficients are real because all interpolation data are real.
:::

<1>6. One has
$$
B=p(A).
$$

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
v\in E_{\lambda_j}.
$$
Then step <1>4 gives
$$
Bv=\beta_jv,
$$
while step <1>5 gives
$$
p(A)v
=
p(\lambda_j)v
=
\beta_jv.
$$
Thus $B$ and $p(A)$ agree on every eigenspace. By step <1>2, those
eigenspaces span $\RR^3$, so the two linear maps are equal.
:::

<1>7. Therefore
$$
\boxed{B=aI+bA+cA^2}
$$
for some real numbers $a,b,c$.

::: {.proof}
Expand $p(A)$ using the polynomial from step <1>5 and apply step <1>6.
:::

<1>8. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>7 is the required form.
:::
