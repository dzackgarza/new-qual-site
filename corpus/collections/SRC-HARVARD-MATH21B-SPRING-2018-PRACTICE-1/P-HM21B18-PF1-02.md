::: {.problem}
(a) For
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}
100&2&3&4\\4&100&2&3\\3&4&100&2\\2&3&4&100
\end{pmatrix},
$$
decide whether $A$ is symmetric, whether $(1,i,i^2,i^3)^T$ is an eigenvector, whether $100+2i-3-4i$ is an eigenvalue, whether $A$ is invertible, and whether it is diagonalizable over $\CC$ or $\RR$.

(b) Match
$$
f'+f=e^{-t},\quad f'-f=e^{-t},\quad f''+f=e^{-t},\quad f''=e^{-t}
$$
to source graphs A--D. In the source, graph A is positive and decays to
zero, graph B grows exponentially, graph C is oscillatory, and graph D is
an increasing curve that is asymptotically affine.

(c) For a real $3\times3$ matrix, with “diagonalizable” in this part
meaning diagonalizable over $\CC$, decide which statements are true:
diagonalizable with real eigenvalues implies symmetric; symmetric implies
diagonalizable with real eigenvalues; all eigenvalues of algebraic
multiplicity one implies diagonalizable; diagonalizable implies all
eigenvalues have algebraic multiplicity one.
:::

::: {.solution}

Let
$$
v=(1,i,i^2,i^3)^T=(1,i,-1,-i)^T.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

In part (a), $A$ is not symmetric.


::: pf-proof

The entries in positions $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ are $2$ and $4$,
respectively. Hence $A^T\neq A$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The vector $v$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue
$$
\lambda=100+2i-3-4i=97-2i.
$$


::: pf-proof

Direct multiplication gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
Av
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
100+2i-3-4i\\
4+100i-2-3i\\
3+4i-100-2i\\
2+3i-4-100i
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
97-2i\\
2+97i\\
-97+2i\\
-2-97i
\end{pmatrix}
=(97-2i)
\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\\-1\\-i\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $Av=\lambda v$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The eigenvalues of $A$ over $\CC$ are
$$
109,\qquad 97-2i,\qquad 97,\qquad 97+2i.
$$


::: pf-proof

Let $\zeta\in\{1,i,-1,-i\}$. For
$$
v_\zeta=(1,\zeta,\zeta^2,\zeta^3)^T,
$$
the circulant form of $A$ gives
$$
Av_\zeta
=(100+2\zeta+3\zeta^2+4\zeta^3)v_\zeta.
$$
Substituting $\zeta=1,i,-1,-i$ gives, respectively,
$$
109,\quad97-2i,\quad97,\quad97+2i.
$$
The four vectors $v_\zeta$ are linearly independent because they are the
columns of a Fourier matrix with distinct fourth roots of unity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Therefore $A$ is invertible and diagonalizable over $\CC$,
but it is not diagonalizable over $\RR$.


::: pf-proof

None of the four eigenvalues in step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} is zero, so $\det A\neq0$ and
$A$ is invertible. The four linearly independent eigenvectors in
step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} give a complex diagonalization.

If a real matrix were diagonalizable over $\RR$, all diagonal
entries in a real diagonal form, hence all of its eigenvalues, would be
real. Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} exhibits the nonreal eigenvalues $97\pm2i$, so no real
diagonalization exists.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

In part (b), $f'+f=e^{-t}$ matches graph A.


::: pf-proof

Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^t$ gives
$$
(e^tf)'=1.
$$
Hence
$$
f(t)=(t+C)e^{-t}.
$$
For suitable $C$, this is positive and decays to $0$ as $t\to\infty$,
which is the qualitative behavior of graph A.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

In part (b), $f'-f=e^{-t}$ matches graph B.


::: pf-proof

Multiplication by $e^{-t}$ gives
$$
(e^{-t}f)'=e^{-2t}.
$$
Thus
$$
f(t)=Ce^t-\frac12e^{-t}.
$$
For $C>0$, the $Ce^t$ term produces the exponential growth shown in
graph B.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

In part (b), $f''+f=e^{-t}$ matches graph C.


::: pf-proof

The homogeneous solutions are $\cos t$ and $\sin t$, while
$\frac12e^{-t}$ is a particular solution. Thus
$$
f(t)=C_1\cos t+C_2\sin t+\frac12e^{-t}.
$$
Its nonzero homogeneous part is oscillatory, with only a decaying
exponential correction, matching graph C.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

In part (b), $f''=e^{-t}$ matches graph D.


::: pf-proof

Integrating twice gives
$$
f(t)=e^{-t}+C_1t+C_2.
$$
Hence $f''(t)=e^{-t}>0$ and $f(t)-(C_1t+C_2)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$.
For the constants represented by the source graph, this is the increasing
asymptotically affine curve D.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

In part (c), “diagonalizable with real eigenvalues implies
symmetric” is false.


::: pf-proof

The matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&0\\
0&2&0\\
0&0&3
\end{pmatrix}
$$
has three distinct real eigenvalues $1,2,3$, so it is diagonalizable over
$\RR$, but it is not symmetric.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

In part (c), “symmetric implies diagonalizable with real
eigenvalues” is true.


::: pf-proof

This is exactly the real spectral theorem.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

In part (c), “all eigenvalues of algebraic multiplicity one
implies diagonalizable” is true.


::: pf-proof

Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.
For a $3\times3$ matrix whose eigenvalues all have algebraic multiplicity
one, the characteristic polynomial has three distinct roots, giving three
linearly independent eigenvectors. Hence the matrix is diagonalizable.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

In part (c), “diagonalizable implies all eigenvalues have
algebraic multiplicity one” is false.


::: pf-proof

The identity matrix $I_3$ is diagonal, hence diagonalizable, but its only
eigenvalue $1$ has algebraic multiplicity $3$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

The complete answers are
$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{(a) not symmetric; }v\text{ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue }
97-2i;\\
\text{invertible; diagonalizable over }\CC
\text{ but not over }\RR;\\
\text{(b) }f'+f=e^{-t}\mapsto A,\quad
f'-f=e^{-t}\mapsto B,\quad
f''+f=e^{-t}\mapsto C,\quad
f''=e^{-t}\mapsto D;\\
\text{(c) only the second and third statements are true.}
\end{gathered}}
$$


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} establish part (a), steps [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} establish part
(b), and steps [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} establish part (c).

:::
