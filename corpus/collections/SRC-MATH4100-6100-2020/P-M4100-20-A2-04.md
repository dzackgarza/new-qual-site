::: {.problem}
(a) Let $(x_n)$ be bounded. Prove that if
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\abs{x_n}=0,
$$
then $x_n\to0$.

(b) Prove that every bounded divergent sequence has at least two subsequences converging to different limits.

(c) Find $\liminf a_n$ and $\limsup a_n$ for
$$
a_n=\lfloor\sin n\rfloor.
$$

(d) Find all subsequential limits of each sequence
$$
x_n=4+5(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
\quad
x_n=\cos(n\pi/3),
$$
and
$$
x_n=(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}+2(-1)^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

If
$$
\abs{x_n}\longrightarrow0,
$$
then
$$
x_n\longrightarrow0.
$$


::: pf-proof

For every $n$,
$$
\abs{x_n-0}=\abs{x_n}.
$$
Thus the defining condition for $\abs{x_n}\to0$ is exactly the defining condition
for $x_n\to0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

Part (a) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Every bounded divergent sequence has a convergent subsequence.


::: pf-proof

This is the Bolzano--Weierstrass Theorem.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Let $x_{n_k}\to a$ be a convergent subsequence of a bounded divergent
sequence $(x_n)$. Then there exists $\varepsilon>0$ for which infinitely many
terms of $(x_n)$ satisfy
$$
\abs{x_n-a}\geq\varepsilon.
$$


::: pf-proof

If for every $\varepsilon>0$ only finitely many terms satisfied
$\abs{x_n-a}\geq\varepsilon$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ all sufficiently
large $n$ would satisfy
$$
\abs{x_n-a}<\varepsilon,
$$
which would mean $x_n\to a$. This contradicts divergence.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

There is a second subsequence converging to some $b\neq a$.


::: pf-proof

Choose a subsequence $(x_{m_j})$ consisting of terms satisfying
$$
\abs{x_{m_j}-a}\geq\varepsilon,
$$
where $\varepsilon$ is from step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref}. This subsequence is bounded, so
Bolzano--Weierstrass gives a convergent further subsequence
$$
x_{m_{j_\ell}}\longrightarrow b.
$$
Passing to the limit in
$$
\abs{x_{m_{j_\ell}}-a}\geq\varepsilon
$$
gives
$$
\abs{b-a}\geq\varepsilon>0.
$$
Hence $b\neq a$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Part (b) follows.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} produce two subsequences with distinct limits.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

For every positive integer $n$,
$$
\lfloor\sin n\rfloor
\in\{-1,0\}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
-1\leq\sin n\leq1,
$$
the floor can only be $-1$, $0$, or $1$. It cannot be $1$, because that would
require $\sin n=1$, hence
$$
n=\frac\pi2+2k\pi
$$
for some integer $k$, impossible for integer $n$ because $\pi$ is irrational.
Thus only $-1$ and $0$ occur.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The values $0$ and $-1$ each occur infinitely often in
$$
a_n=\lfloor\sin n\rfloor.
$$


::: pf-proof

For every integer $k\geq0$, the interval
$$
(2k\pi,(2k+1)\pi)
$$
has length $\pi>1$, so it contains an integer $n$. On this interval,
$\sin n>0$, hence $a_n=0$.

Likewise, the interval
$$
((2k+1)\pi,(2k+2)\pi)
$$
has length $\pi>1$ and contains an integer $m$. On this interval,
$\sin m<0$, hence $a_m=-1$.

As $k$ increases, these intervals are disjoint and move to infinity, so each
value occurs infinitely often.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

For $a_n=\lfloor\sin n\rfloor$,
$$
\boxed{\liminf a_n=-1,\qquad\limsup a_n=0}.
$$


::: pf-proof

By steps [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}, the only subsequential limits are $-1$ and $0$, and both
actually occur along constant subsequences. Their infimum is $-1$ and their
supremum is $0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Part (c) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

For
$$
x_n=4+5(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
$$
the set of subsequential limits is
$$
\boxed{\{-1,9\}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The parity of $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ alternates in blocks and repeats with
period $4$. Thus
$$
(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\in\{-1,1\},
$$
and both values occur infinitely often. Therefore $x_n$ takes only the values
$$
4-5=-1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
4+5=9,
$$
each infinitely often. These and only these are subsequential limits.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

For
$$
x_n=\cos(n\pi/3),
$$
the set of subsequential limits is
$$
\boxed{\{-1,-1/2,1/2,1\}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The sequence is periodic with period $6$. For residues $n=1,2,3,4,5,0$
modulo $6$, its values are respectively
$$
\frac12,-\frac12,-1,-\frac12,\frac12,1.
$$
Each residue class occurs infinitely often, so each of the four displayed
values is a subsequential limit. No other value occurs.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

For
$$
x_n=(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}+2(-1)^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor},
$$
the set of subsequential limits is
$$
\boxed{\{-3,-1,1,3\}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The first sign has period $4$, because
$$
\left\lfloor\frac{n+4}{2}\right\rfloor
=
\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor+2,
$$
and the second sign has period $6$, because
$$
\left\lfloor\frac{n+6}{3}\right\rfloor
=
\left\lfloor\frac n3\right\rfloor+2.
$$
Hence $x_n$ has period $12$.

For $n=1,2,3,4$ one obtains respectively
$$
3,1,-3,-1.
$$
Thus all four possible sums
$$
(\pm1)+2(\pm1)\in\{-3,-1,1,3\}
$$
occur, and periodicity makes each occur infinitely often. Since no other value
is possible, these are exactly the subsequential limits.

:::

:::
