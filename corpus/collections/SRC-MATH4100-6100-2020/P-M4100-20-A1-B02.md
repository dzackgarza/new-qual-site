::: {.problem}
(a) Use the order-limit result from Assignment 1 Problem 8 to deduce the Nested Interval Property from the Monotone Convergence Theorem.

(b) Conversely, deduce the Monotone Convergence Theorem from the Nested Interval Property.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Assume the Monotone Convergence Theorem, and let
$$
[a_1,b_1]\supseteq[a_2,b_2]\supseteq\cdots
$$
be nested nonempty closed bounded intervals.


::: pf-proof

Nestedness gives
$$
a_1\leq a_2\leq\cdots
$$
and
$$
b_1\geq b_2\geq\cdots.
$$
Moreover, $a_n\leq b_n\leq b_1$ for every $n$, so $(a_n)$ is increasing and
bounded above.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The sequence $(a_n)$ converges to some $a\in\RR$.


::: pf-proof

This follows from the Monotone Convergence Theorem applied to the increasing
bounded sequence in step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

For every $n$,
$$
a_n\leq a\leq b_n.
$$


::: pf-proof

The first inequality follows from Assignment 1 Problem 8(b), since $(a_n)$ is
increasing and converges to $a$.

Fix $n$.  For every $m\geq n$, nestedness gives
$$
a_m\leq b_n.
$$
Since the tail $(a_m)_{m\geq n}$ also converges to $a$, Assignment 1
Problem 8(a) implies
$$
a\leq b_n.
$$
Thus $a_n\leq a\leq b_n$ for every $n$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Therefore
$$
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty[a_n,b_n]\neq\varnothing.
$$


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} shows that the point $a$ belongs to every interval.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

If additionally $b_n-a_n\to0$, then the intersection contains exactly
one point.


::: pf-proof

Suppose $x$ and $y$ both belong to every interval.  Then for every $n$,
$$
\abs{x-y}\leq b_n-a_n.
$$
Letting $n\to\infty$ gives $\abs{x-y}=0$, so $x=y$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Part (a) follows.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} give the Nested Interval Property, including its uniqueness
statement for intervals whose lengths tend to zero.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Assume now the Nested Interval Property, and let $(a_n)$ be increasing
and bounded above.


::: pf-proof

It remains to prove that $(a_n)$ converges.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

If some term $a_N$ is an upper bound for the set
$$
S=\{a_n:n\geq1\},
$$
then $(a_n)$ converges.


::: pf-proof

Because the sequence is increasing, for every $n\geq N$,
$$
a_N\leq a_n.
$$
If $a_N$ is also an upper bound for $S$, then $a_n\leq a_N$.  Hence
$a_n=a_N$ for all $n\geq N$, so the sequence is eventually constant and
therefore converges.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Suppose no term of the sequence is an upper bound for $S$.  Choose an
upper bound $u_1$ for $S$ and put
$$
\ell_1=a_1.
$$
Then recursively construct nested intervals
$$
I_k=[\ell_k,u_k]
$$
such that $u_k$ is an upper bound for $S$, $\ell_k$ is not an upper bound for
$S$, and
$$
u_k-\ell_k=2^{-(k-1)}(u_1-\ell_1).
$$


::: pf-proof

Assume $I_k=[\ell_k,u_k]$ has been constructed.  Let
$$
m_k=\frac{\ell_k+u_k}{2}.
$$
If $m_k$ is an upper bound for $S$, set
$$
\ell_{k+1}=\ell_k,
\qquad
u_{k+1}=m_k.
$$
If $m_k$ is not an upper bound for $S$, set
$$
\ell_{k+1}=m_k,
\qquad
u_{k+1}=u_k.
$$
In either case $I_{k+1}\subseteq I_k$, the left endpoint is not an upper
bound, the right endpoint is an upper bound, and the interval length is halved.
Induction gives the stated construction.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

The intersection
$$
\bigcap_{k=1}^\infty I_k
$$
consists of a single point $L$.


::: pf-proof

The intervals are nested, nonempty, closed, and bounded.  Their lengths tend to
zero by step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}.  The Nested Interval Property therefore gives a nonempty
intersection, and its shrinking-length clause makes that intersection a
singleton.  Denote its unique point by $L$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

The point $L$ is an upper bound for $S$.


::: pf-proof

Suppose some $s\in S$ satisfied $s>L$.  Since
$$
u_k-\ell_k\longrightarrow0,
$$
choose $k$ with
$$
u_k-\ell_k<s-L.
$$
Because $L\in I_k$, one has $L\geq\ell_k$, hence
$$
u_k-L\leq u_k-\ell_k<s-L.
$$
Therefore $u_k<s$, contradicting the fact that $u_k$ is an upper bound for
$S$.  Thus every $s\in S$ satisfies $s\leq L$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

No number smaller than $L$ is an upper bound for $S$.


::: pf-proof

Let $x<L$.  Choose $k$ so large that
$$
u_k-\ell_k<L-x.
$$
Since $L\leq u_k$,
$$
L-\ell_k\leq u_k-\ell_k<L-x,
$$
so $x<\ell_k$.  By construction, $\ell_k$ is not an upper bound for $S$.
Hence there exists $s\in S$ with
$$
s>\ell_k>x.
$$
Thus $x$ is not an upper bound for $S$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

The number $L$ is the least upper bound of $S$.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref} shows that $L$ is an upper bound.  Step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} shows that every
smaller real number fails to be an upper bound.  Therefore
$$
L=\sup S.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

The increasing bounded sequence $(a_n)$ converges to $L$.


::: pf-proof

Let $\varepsilon>0$.  Since $L-\varepsilon<L=\sup S$, the number
$L-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound for $S$.  Hence there exists $N$ such
that
$$
a_N>L-\varepsilon.
$$
For every $n\geq N$, monotonicity and the upper-bound property of $L$ give
$$
L-\varepsilon<a_N\leq a_n\leq L.
$$
Therefore
$$
\abs{a_n-L}<\varepsilon
$$
for every $n\geq N$.  Hence $a_n\to L$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-15}

Part (b) follows.


::: pf-proof

If some term is an upper bound, convergence follows from step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}.  Otherwise
steps [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-14){.pf-ref} prove convergence.  Thus every increasing sequence bounded
above converges.  Applying the result to $(-a_n)$ gives the decreasing
bounded-below case, so the full Monotone Convergence Theorem follows.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} proves the Nested Interval Property from monotone convergence, and
step [](#s1-15){.pf-ref} proves the converse.

:::
