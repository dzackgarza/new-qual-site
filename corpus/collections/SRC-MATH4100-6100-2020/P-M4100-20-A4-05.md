::: {.problem}
Decide whether each statement is true or false. Give a counterexample for every false statement and a proof for every true statement.

(a) For every $E\subseteq\RR$, the complement $(\overline E)^c$ is open.

(b) If a set has an isolated point, then it cannot be open.

(c) If $E\subseteq\RR$ is nonempty and bounded, then $\sup E$ is a limit point of $E$.

(d) Every nonempty compact subset of $\RR$ has a largest element.

(e) An open subset of $\RR$ containing every rational number must equal $\RR$.

(f) An arbitrary intersection of compact subsets of $\RR$ is compact.

(g) If
$$
F_1\supseteq F_2\supseteq F_3\supseteq\cdots
$$
is a nested sequence of nonempty closed sets, then
$$
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty F_n\ne\varnothing.
$$

(h) Every finite set is compact.

(i) Every countable set is compact.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Statement (a) is $\boxed{\mathrm{T}}$.


::: pf-proof

For every $E\subseteq\RR$, the closure $\overline E$ is closed by
definition. The complement of a closed set is open, so
$$
(\overline E)^c
$$
is open.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

Statement (b) is $\boxed{\mathrm{T}}$.


::: pf-proof

Suppose $x$ is an isolated point of $E$. Then there exists
$\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\cap E=\{x\}.
$$
If $E$ were open, there would exist $\delta>0$ with
$$
(x-\delta,x+\delta)\subseteq E.
$$
Taking
$$
0<r<\min\{\varepsilon,\delta\}
$$
gives $x+r\in E\cap(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)$ with $x+r\neq x$, a
contradiction. Hence a set with an isolated point cannot be open.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Statement (c) is $\boxed{\mathrm{F}}$.


::: pf-proof

Take
$$
E=\{1\}.
$$
Then $E$ is nonempty and bounded and
$$
\sup E=1,
$$
but $1$ is not a limit point of $E$, because
$$
(1-1/2,1+1/2)\cap(E\setminus\{1\})=\varnothing.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Statement (d) is $\boxed{\mathrm{T}}$.


::: pf-proof

Let $K\subseteq\RR$ be nonempty and compact. By Heine--Borel, $K$ is
bounded and closed. Since $K$ is nonempty and bounded above,
$$
s=\sup K
$$
exists.

For every $n$, the number $s-1/n$ is not an upper bound for $K$, so choose
$$
x_n\in K
$$
with
$$
s-\frac1n<x_n\leq s.
$$
Then $x_n\to s$. Since $K$ is closed, $s\in K$. Thus $s$ is the largest
element of $K$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Statement (e) is $\boxed{\mathrm{F}}$.


::: pf-proof

The set
$$
U=\RR\setminus\{\sqrt2\}
$$
is open because $\{\sqrt2\}$ is closed. It contains every rational number,
since $\sqrt2$ is irrational, but
$$
U\neq\RR.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Statement (f) is $\boxed{\mathrm{T}}$ for any nonempty family of
compact subsets of $\RR$.


::: pf-proof

Let $(K_i)_{i\in I}$ be a nonempty family of compact subsets of $\RR$, and
set
$$
K=\bigcap_{i\in I}K_i.
$$
Each $K_i$ is closed by Heine--Borel, so $K$ is closed as an arbitrary
intersection of closed sets. Choose one index $i_0\in I$. Then
$$
K\subseteq K_{i_0},
$$
so $K$ is bounded because $K_{i_0}$ is bounded. Hence $K$ is closed and
bounded, and therefore compact by Heine--Borel. The empty intersection case is
excluded here; under the convention that the intersection of an empty family
is $\RR$, that degenerate case would not be compact.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Statement (g) is $\boxed{\mathrm{F}}$.


::: pf-proof

For
$$
F_n=[n,\infty),
$$
each $F_n$ is nonempty and closed, and
$$
F_1\supseteq F_2\supseteq F_3\supseteq\cdots.
$$
However, no real number belongs to every $F_n$: given $x\in\RR$, choose
$n>x$. Then $x\notin F_n$. Thus
$$
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}F_n=\varnothing.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Statement (h) is $\boxed{\mathrm{T}}$.


::: pf-proof

Let
$$
F=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}
$$
be finite, and let $\mathcal U$ be an open cover of $F$. For each
$j=1,\ldots,m$, choose one member $U_j\in\mathcal U$ with
$$
x_j\in U_j.
$$
Then
$$
F\subseteq U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_m,
$$
so every open cover has a finite subcover. Hence $F$ is compact.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Statement (i) is $\boxed{\mathrm{F}}$.


::: pf-proof

The set $\NN$ is countable but not compact. For example,
$$
\{(-n,n):n\in\NN\}
$$
is an open cover of $\NN$ with no finite subcover: a finite subfamily is
contained in $(-N,N)$ for some $N$ and therefore misses natural numbers larger
than $N$.

:::
