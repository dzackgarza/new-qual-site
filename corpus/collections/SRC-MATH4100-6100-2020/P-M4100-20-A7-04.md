::: {.problem}
Give an example of each of the following, or provide a short argument for why the request is impossible.

(a) A continuous function defined on [0, 1] with range (0, 1).

(b) A continuous function defined on (0, 1) with range [0, 1].

(c) A continuous function defined on (0, 1] with range (0, 1).
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Part (a) is impossible.


::: pf-proof

Suppose
$$
f:[0,1]\to\RR
$$
were continuous with range $(0,1)$. The interval $[0,1]$ is compact, and the
continuous image of a compact set is compact. Therefore
$$
f([0,1])
$$
would be compact.

But
$$
(0,1)
$$
is not compact: it is not closed in $\RR$. Hence no such continuous function
exists.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

Define
$$
f(x)=\frac{1+\sin(2\pi x)}2,
\qquad
x\in(0,1).
$$
Then $f$ is continuous and has range $[0,1]$.


::: pf-proof

Continuity is immediate from continuity of the sine function.

For $x\in(0,1)$,
$$
-1\leq\sin(2\pi x)\leq1,
$$
so
$$
0\leq f(x)\leq1.
$$
Moreover,
$$
f\left(\frac14\right)=1
$$
and
$$
f\left(\frac34\right)=0.
$$
Since $(0,1)$ is connected and $f$ is continuous, its image is an interval
containing both $0$ and $1$. Hence
$$
f((0,1))=[0,1].
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Part (b) follows.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} gives the requested example.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Define
$$
g(x)=\frac{\sin(1/x)}{x},
\qquad
x\in(0,1].
$$
Then $g$ is continuous and unbounded both above and below.


::: pf-proof

Continuity follows because $x\neq0$ on the domain.

For
$$
t_k=\frac\pi2+2\pi k
$$
and
$$
x_k=\frac1{t_k},
$$
one has $x_k\in(0,1]$ for all sufficiently large $k$, and
$$
g(x_k)
=
t_k\sin(t_k)
=
t_k
\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

Likewise, for
$$
s_k=\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi k
$$
and
$$
y_k=\frac1{s_k},
$$
one has
$$
g(y_k)
=
s_k\sin(s_k)
=
-s_k
\longrightarrow-\infty.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The range of $g$ is all of $\RR$.


::: pf-proof

The interval $(0,1]$ is connected, so the continuous image
$$
g((0,1])
$$
is an interval in $\RR$. By step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref}, this interval is unbounded above and
unbounded below. The only interval in $\RR$ with both properties is $\RR$
itself.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Define
$$
\phi(t)
=
\frac12
+
\frac{t}{2(1+\abs{t})},
\qquad
t\in\RR.
$$
Then $\phi$ is continuous and maps $\RR$ onto $(0,1)$.


::: pf-proof

Continuity is immediate. For every real $t$,
$$
-1
<
\frac{t}{1+\abs{t}}
<
1,
$$
so
$$
0<\phi(t)<1.
$$

As $t\to+\infty$,
$$
\phi(t)\to1,
$$
and as $t\to-\infty$,
$$
\phi(t)\to0.
$$
Since $\phi(\RR)$ is an interval and contains values arbitrarily close to
both $0$ and $1$ while remaining inside $(0,1)$, one has
$$
\phi(\RR)=(0,1).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

The function
$$
F=\phi\circ g:(0,1]\to(0,1)
$$
is continuous and has range $(0,1)$.


::: pf-proof

It is a composition of continuous functions. By step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref},
$$
g((0,1])=\RR,
$$
and by step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref},
$$
\phi(\RR)=(0,1).
$$
Therefore
$$
F((0,1])
=
(0,1).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Part (c) follows.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} gives the requested example.

:::
