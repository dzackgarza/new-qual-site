::: {.problem}
Let

$$
g ( x ) = \sum _ { n = 0 } ^ { \infty } { \frac { 1 } { 1 + n ^ { 2 } x } } .
$$

(a) Prove that the series defining g does not converge uniformly on $( 0 , \infty )$

(b) Prove that g is however a continuous function on $( 0 , \infty )$

(c) Is g differentiable?
If so, is the derivative function $g ^ { \prime }$ continuous?
:::

::: {.solution}

Write
$$
u_n(x)=\frac1{1+n^2x},
\qquad
x>0.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

For every $n\geq1$,
$$
\sup_{x>0}u_n(x)=1.
$$


::: pf-proof

For every $x>0$,
$$
0<u_n(x)<1.
$$
As $x\to0^+$,
$$
u_n(x)
=
\frac1{1+n^2x}
\longrightarrow1.
$$
Thus the supremum over $(0,\infty)$ is $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The sequence $(u_n)$ does not converge uniformly to $0$ on
$(0,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

Uniform convergence to $0$ would require
$$
\sup_{x>0}\abs{u_n(x)}
\longrightarrow0.
$$
But step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} shows that this supremum equals $1$ for every $n\geq1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The series defining $g$ does not converge uniformly on
$(0,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

If
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_n(x)
$$
converged uniformly, then its terms $u_n$ would converge uniformly to $0$.
This contradicts step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Part (a) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Fix $a>0$. For every $x\geq a$ and every $n\geq1$,
$$
0<u_n(x)
\leq
\frac1{n^2a}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since $x\geq a$,
$$
1+n^2x
\geq
n^2a.
$$
Taking reciprocals gives the inequality.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The series defining $g$ converges uniformly on every interval
$[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$.


::: pf-proof

The $n=0$ term is the constant function $1$. For $n\geq1$, step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} gives
the uniform bound
$$
\abs{u_n(x)}
\leq
\frac1{a n^2}.
$$
The numerical series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{a n^2}
$$
converges, so the Weierstrass $M$-test gives uniform convergence.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

The function $g$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

Fix $x_0>0$ and choose
$$
a=\frac{x_0}{2}.
$$
By step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref}, the series converges uniformly on $[a,\infty)$, which contains
a neighborhood of $x_0$. Every summand is continuous there, so the uniform
limit is continuous at $x_0$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $g$ is continuous
on $(0,\infty)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Part (b) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

For $n\geq1$,
$$
u_n'(x)
=
-\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Differentiate $(1+n^2x)^{-1}$ by the chain rule.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Fix $a>0$. For every $x\geq a$ and every $n\geq1$,
$$
\abs{u_n'(x)}
\leq
\frac1{a^2n^2}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
1+n^2x
\geq
n^2a,
$$
one has
$$
\abs{u_n'(x)}
=
\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}
\leq
\frac{n^2}{n^4a^2}
=
\frac1{a^2n^2}.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

The derivative series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_n'(x)
$$
converges uniformly on every interval $[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} gives the uniform majorant
$$
\frac1{a^2n^2},
$$
and the corresponding numerical series converges. Apply the Weierstrass
$M$-test.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

The function $g$ is differentiable on $(0,\infty)$, with
$$
\boxed{
g'(x)
=
-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Fix $x_0>0$ and choose $a=x_0/2$. On $[a,\infty)$, the original series
converges at every point by step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref}, and the derivative series converges
uniformly by step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref}. Hence the standard theorem on termwise
differentiation applies on this interval. In particular, $g$ is differentiable
at $x_0$ with the displayed derivative. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, the formula
holds throughout $(0,\infty)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

The derivative $g'$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

Fix $x_0>0$ and again choose $a=x_0/2$. Each derivative term is continuous on
$[a,\infty)$, and step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref} gives uniform convergence of their series.
Therefore $g'$ is continuous at $x_0$ as a uniform limit of continuous
functions. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $g'$ is continuous on the whole domain.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

Part (c) follows.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} proves differentiability and step [](#s1-13){.pf-ref} proves continuity of the
derivative.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}, and [](#s1-14){.pf-ref} establish parts (a), (b), and (c), respectively.

:::
