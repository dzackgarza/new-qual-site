::: {.problem}
For each $n \in \NN$ and $x \in [0, \infty)$, let

$$
f_n(x) = \frac{x}{1 + x^n} \qquad \text{and} \qquad g_n(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x \geq 1/n, \\ nx & \text{if } 0 \leq x < 1/n. \end{cases}
$$

Answer the following questions for the sequences $\{f_n\}$ and $\{g_n\}$:

(a) Find the pointwise limit on $[0, \infty)$.

(b) Explain how we know that the convergence cannot be uniform on $[0, \infty)$.

(c) Choose a small set over which the convergence is uniform and prove that this is the case.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

If $0\leq x<1$, then
$$
f_n(x)\longrightarrow x.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since $0\leq x<1$,
$$
x^n\longrightarrow0.
$$
Hence
$$
f_n(x)
=
\frac{x}{1+x^n}
\longrightarrow
x.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

At $x=1$,
$$
f_n(1)=\frac12
$$
for every $n$.


::: pf-proof

Direct substitution gives
$$
f_n(1)
=
\frac1{1+1}
=
\frac12.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

If $x>1$, then
$$
f_n(x)\longrightarrow0.
$$


::: pf-proof

One has
$$
0<f_n(x)
=
\frac{x}{1+x^n}
\leq
\frac{x}{x^n}
=
x^{1-n}.
$$
Since $x>1$,
$$
x^{1-n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The squeeze theorem gives the claim.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Therefore the pointwise limit of $(f_n)$ is
$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
x,&0\leq x<1,\\
1/2,&x=1,\\
0,&x>1.
\end{cases}
$$


::: pf-proof

Combine steps [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The pointwise limit of $(g_n)$ is
$$
g(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x=0,\\
1,&x>0.
\end{cases}
$$


::: pf-proof

At $x=0$,
$$
g_n(0)=0
$$
for every $n$.

Fix $x>0$. For all sufficiently large $n$,
$$
\frac1n\leq x,
$$
so by definition
$$
g_n(x)=1.
$$
Thus $g_n(x)\to1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Part (a) follows.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} give the two pointwise limits.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

The convergence $(f_n)\to f$ is not uniform on $[0,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

Each $f_n$ is continuous on $[0,\infty)$. If $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then
the uniform limit theorem would imply that $f$ is continuous.

But by step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref},
$$
\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=1
$$
while
$$
f(1)=\frac12.
$$
Thus $f$ is discontinuous at $1$, a contradiction.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The convergence $(g_n)\to g$ is not uniform on $[0,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

Each $g_n$ is continuous: the two formulas agree at $x=1/n$ because
$$
n\left(\frac1n\right)=1.
$$
If the convergence were uniform, the limit $g$ would be continuous.
But by step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref},
$$
g(0)=0
$$
while
$$
g(x)=1
$$
for every $x>0$, so $g$ is discontinuous at $0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Part (b) follows.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} rule out uniform convergence on the full domain.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

The convergence $f_n\to f$ is uniform on
$$
\left[0,\frac12\right].
$$


::: pf-proof

On this interval, $f(x)=x$. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
|f_n(x)-f(x)|
&=
\left|
\frac{x}{1+x^n}-x
\right|\\
&=
\frac{x^{n+1}}{1+x^n}\\
&\leq
x^{n+1}\\
&\leq
\left(\frac12\right)^{n+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\sup_{0\leq x\leq1/2}|f_n(x)-f(x)|
\leq
2^{-(n+1)}
\longrightarrow0.
$$
This is uniform convergence.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

The convergence $g_n\to g$ is uniform on $[1,\infty)$.


::: pf-proof

For every $n\geq1$ and every $x\geq1$,
$$
x\geq1\geq\frac1n.
$$
Thus
$$
g_n(x)=1=g(x)
$$
throughout $[1,\infty)$. Hence the convergence is in fact exact there.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

Part (c) follows.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-11){.pf-ref} give explicit sets on which the two convergences are
uniform.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}, and [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} establish parts (a), (b), and (c), respectively.

:::
