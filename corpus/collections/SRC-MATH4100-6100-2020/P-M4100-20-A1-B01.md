::: {.problem}
Let
$$
b_n=\frac{a_1+\cdots+a_n}{n}.
$$
Prove that $a_n\to0$ implies $b_n\to0$. Is the converse true? What conclusion holds if $a_n\to L$?
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

If $a_n\to0$, then $b_n\to0$.


::: pf-proof

Let $\varepsilon>0$.  Since $a_n\to0$, there exists $N\geq1$ such that
$$
\abs{a_k}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
$$
for every $k\geq N$.  Put
$$
C=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\abs{a_k}.
$$
Choose $M\geq N$ so large that
$$
\frac{C}{M}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
Then for every $n\geq M$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\abs{b_n}
&=
\abs{
\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n a_k
}\\
&\leq
\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\abs{a_k}
+
\frac1n\sum_{k=N}^n\abs{a_k}\\
&\leq
\frac{C}{n}
+
\frac{n-N+1}{n}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\
&<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
+
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
=\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence $b_n\to0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The converse is false.


::: pf-proof

Take
$$
a_n=(-1)^n.
$$
Then $(a_n)$ does not converge.  However, the partial sums
$$
\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^k
$$
are either $0$ or $-1$, so
$$
\abs{b_n}
=
\frac1n
\abs{
\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^k
}
\leq\frac1n
\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $b_n\to0$ does not imply $a_n\to0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

If $a_n\to L$, then
$$
\boxed{b_n\to L}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Set
$$
c_n=a_n-L.
$$
Then $c_n\to0$.  By step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref},
$$
\frac{c_1+\cdots+c_n}{n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
But
$$
\frac{c_1+\cdots+c_n}{n}
=
\frac{a_1+\cdots+a_n}{n}-L
=b_n-L.
$$
Therefore $b_n-L\to0$, so $b_n\to L$.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} proves the first assertion, step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} answers the converse question,
and step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} proves the general Cesàro convergence statement.

:::
