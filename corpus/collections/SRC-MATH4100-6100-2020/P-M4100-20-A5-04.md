::: {.problem}
Let $B$ be the set of all Bernoulli sequences $(x_n)$ with $x_n\in\{0,1\}$.

(a) Prove that
$$
\rho((x_n),(y_n))
=
\sum_{n=0}^\infty 2^{-n}\abs{x_n-y_n}
$$
defines a metric on $B$.

(b) Prove that the set of all Bernoulli sequences beginning with $0,1$ is both open and closed.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

For every $x=(x_n),y=(y_n)\in B$, the series
$$
\rho(x,y)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\abs{x_n-y_n}
$$
converges.


::: pf-proof

Since $x_n,y_n\in\{0,1\}$,
$$
0\leq \abs{x_n-y_n}\leq1.
$$
Hence
$$
0
\leq
2^{-n}\abs{x_n-y_n}
\leq
2^{-n}.
$$
The geometric series
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}=2
$$
converges, so comparison gives convergence of the defining series.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The function $\rho$ is nonnegative and symmetric.


::: pf-proof

Every summand is nonnegative, so $\rho(x,y)\geq0$. Also,
$$
\abs{x_n-y_n}=\abs{y_n-x_n}
$$
for every $n$, hence
$$
\rho(x,y)=\rho(y,x).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

One has
$$
\rho(x,y)=0
\iff
x=y.
$$


::: pf-proof

If $x=y$, then every summand is zero, so $\rho(x,y)=0$.

Conversely, suppose $\rho(x,y)=0$. Since all summands are nonnegative, every
summand must be zero:
$$
2^{-n}\abs{x_n-y_n}=0
$$
for every $n$. Thus
$$
x_n=y_n
$$
for every $n$, so $x=y$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The triangle inequality holds:
$$
\rho(x,z)
\leq
\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z).
$$


::: pf-proof

For every $n$, the ordinary triangle inequality in $\RR$ gives
$$
\abs{x_n-z_n}
\leq
\abs{x_n-y_n}+\abs{y_n-z_n}.
$$
Multiplying by $2^{-n}$ and summing gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(x,z)
&=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\abs{x_n-z_n}\\
&\leq
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\abs{x_n-y_n}
+
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\abs{y_n-z_n}\\
&=
\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z).
\end{aligned}
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Therefore $\rho$ is a metric on $B$.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} verify that $\rho$ is well-defined and satisfies all metric
axioms.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Part (a) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Let
$$
C=
\{x=(x_n)\in B:x_0=0,\ x_1=1\}.
$$
If $x\in C$ and
$$
\rho(x,y)<\frac12,
$$
then $y\in C$.


::: pf-proof

If $y_0\neq x_0$, then
$$
\rho(x,y)
\geq
2^0\abs{x_0-y_0}
=1,
$$
contrary to $\rho(x,y)<1/2$.

If $y_1\neq x_1$, then
$$
\rho(x,y)
\geq
2^{-1}\abs{x_1-y_1}
=\frac12,
$$
again a contradiction.

Hence
$$
y_0=x_0=0,
\qquad
y_1=x_1=1,
$$
so $y\in C$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The set $C$ is open.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}, for every $x\in C$,
$$
B_{1/2}(x)\subseteq C.
$$
Thus every point of $C$ has an open ball contained in $C$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

If $x\in B\setminus C$ and
$$
\rho(x,y)<\frac12,
$$
then $y\in B\setminus C$.


::: pf-proof

Since $x\notin C$, either $x_0=1$ or else $x_0=0$ and $x_1=0$.

If $x_0=1$, then the argument from step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} shows that
$\rho(x,y)<1/2$ forces
$$
y_0=x_0=1,
$$
so $y\notin C$.

If $x_0=0$ and $x_1=0$, then $\rho(x,y)<1/2$ forces
$$
y_0=0,
\qquad
y_1=0,
$$
again by the coordinate-weight estimates from step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}. Hence $y\notin C$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

The complement $B\setminus C$ is open.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}, for every $x\in B\setminus C$,
$$
B_{1/2}(x)\subseteq B\setminus C.
$$
Hence the complement is open.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

The set $C$ is closed.


::: pf-proof

A subset of a metric space is closed exactly when its complement is open.
Apply step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

Part (b) follows:
$$
\boxed{C\text{ is both open and closed}.}
$$


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} proves openness and step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref} proves closedness.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} establish parts (a) and (b), respectively.

:::
