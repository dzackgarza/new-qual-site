::: {.problem}
Let $X$ be an infinite set and define
$$
d_0(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
1,&x\ne y,\\
0,&x=y.
\end{cases}
$$

(a) Prove that $d_0$ is a metric.

(b) Determine which subsets of $X$ are open, which are closed, and which are compact.

(c) Show that, in general,
$$
\overline{B_\varepsilon(x)}
\ne
\{y\in X:d_0(x,y)\le\varepsilon\}.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The function $d_0$ is nonnegative, symmetric, and satisfies
$$
d_0(x,y)=0\iff x=y.
$$


::: pf-proof

By definition, the only possible values of $d_0$ are $0$ and $1$, so it is
nonnegative. The condition $x\neq y$ is symmetric in $x$ and $y$, hence
$$
d_0(x,y)=d_0(y,x).
$$
The definition also gives $d_0(x,y)=0$ exactly when $x=y$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The triangle inequality
$$
d_0(x,z)\leq d_0(x,y)+d_0(y,z)
$$
holds for all $x,y,z\in X$.


::: pf-proof

If $x=z$, then the left side is $0$, so the inequality is immediate.

Suppose $x\neq z$. Then
$$
d_0(x,z)=1.
$$
It is impossible for both $d_0(x,y)$ and $d_0(y,z)$ to be $0$, because that
would imply $x=y=z$, contradicting $x\neq z$. Thus at least one of the two
terms on the right equals $1$, so
$$
d_0(x,y)+d_0(y,z)\geq1=d_0(x,z).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Therefore $d_0$ is a metric on $X$.


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} verify all metric axioms.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Part (a) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

For every $x\in X$,
$$
B_{1/2}(x)=\{x\}.
$$


::: pf-proof

If $y=x$, then
$$
d_0(x,y)=0<\frac12.
$$
If $y\neq x$, then
$$
d_0(x,y)=1>\frac12.
$$
Thus exactly the point $x$ lies in the ball.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Every subset of $X$ is open.


::: pf-proof

Let $A\subseteq X$. By step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref},
$$
A
=
\bigcup_{x\in A}B_{1/2}(x),
$$
so $A$ is a union of open balls and hence open.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Every subset of $X$ is closed.


::: pf-proof

If $A\subseteq X$, then $X\setminus A$ is open by step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref}. Therefore $A$
is closed.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Every finite subset of $X$ is compact.


::: pf-proof

Let
$$
F=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}
$$
be finite and let $\mathcal U$ be an open cover of $F$. For each $j$, choose
$U_j\in\mathcal U$ with $x_j\in U_j$. Then
$$
F\subseteq U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_m,
$$
so the cover has a finite subcover.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

No infinite subset of $X$ is compact.


::: pf-proof

Let $A\subseteq X$ be infinite. By step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}, the family
$$
\mathcal U=\{\{x\}:x\in A\}
$$
is an open cover of $A$. Any finite subfamily covers only finitely many points,
so it cannot cover the infinite set $A$. Thus $A$ is not compact.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

The open, closed, and compact subsets are therefore:
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{every subset of }X\text{ is open},\\
&\text{every subset of }X\text{ is closed},\\
&\text{a subset of }X\text{ is compact iff it is finite}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Combine steps [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

Part (b) follows.


::: pf-proof

This is step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

For $\varepsilon=1$,
$$
B_1(x)=\{x\}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The open ball uses the strict inequality
$$
d_0(x,y)<1.
$$
Only $y=x$ has distance less than $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

The closure of $B_1(x)$ is
$$
\overline{B_1(x)}=\{x\}.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}, every subset is closed, in particular the singleton $\{x\}$.
Use step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

The closed radius-$1$ ball is all of $X$:
$$
\{y\in X:d_0(x,y)\leq1\}=X.
$$


::: pf-proof

Every value of $d_0$ is either $0$ or $1$, so every point satisfies the
displayed inequality.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-15}

Hence
$$
\boxed{
\overline{B_1(x)}
\neq
\{y\in X:d_0(x,y)\leq1\}.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

By steps [](#s1-13){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-14){.pf-ref}, the two sets are respectively $\{x\}$ and $X$.
Since $X$ is infinite, $X\neq\{x\}$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-16}

Part (c) follows.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-15){.pf-ref} gives the required counterexample to the proposed equality.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-11){.pf-ref}, and [](#s1-16){.pf-ref} establish parts (a), (b), and (c), respectively.

:::
