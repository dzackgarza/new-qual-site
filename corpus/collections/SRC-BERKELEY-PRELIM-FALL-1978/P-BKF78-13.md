::: {.problem}
1. Suppose $u,v:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ are continuously differentiable and
\[
\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}.
\]
Show that there is a function $f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ such that
\[
u=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},
\qquad
v=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}.
\]

2. Prove that there is no function $f:\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb R$ such that
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. For part 1, define
$$
f(x,y)
=
\int_0^x u(t,0)\,dt
+
\int_0^y v(x,s)\,ds.
$$

::: {.proof}
The functions $u$ and $v$ are continuously differentiable, so both
integrals are well defined for every $(x,y)\in\RR^2$ and may be
differentiated by the fundamental theorem of calculus and
differentiation under the integral sign.
:::

<1>2. The function in step <1>1 satisfies
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x,y)=v(x,y).
$$

::: {.proof}
The first integral in step <1>1 is independent of $y$, while the
fundamental theorem of calculus gives
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
\int_0^y v(x,s)\,ds
=
v(x,y).
$$
:::

<1>3. The same function satisfies
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,y)=u(x,y).
$$

::: {.proof}
Differentiating step <1>1 with respect to $x$ gives
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,y)
=
u(x,0)
+
\int_0^y
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x,s)\,ds.
$$
By the hypothesis
$$
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
=
\frac{\partial u}{\partial y},
$$
the right-hand side is
$$
u(x,0)
+
\int_0^y
\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x,s)\,ds
=
u(x,y),
$$
again by the fundamental theorem of calculus.
:::

<1>4. Thus part 1 is proved.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>2 and <1>3 give exactly
$$
u=f_x,
\qquad
v=f_y.
$$
:::

<1>5. For part 2, suppose for contradiction that such an $f$ exists,
and define
$$
h(t)=f(\cos t,\sin t),
\qquad
0\le t\le2\pi.
$$
Then
$$
h'(t)=1
$$
for every $t$.

::: {.proof}
The prescribed partial derivatives are continuous on
$\RR^2\setminus\{0\}$, so the chain rule applies along the unit
circle. There,
$$
x^2+y^2=1,
$$
and therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
h'(t)
&=
f_x(\cos t,\sin t)(-\sin t)
+
f_y(\cos t,\sin t)\cos t\\
&=
(-\sin t)(-\sin t)
+
(\cos t)(\cos t)\\
&=
1.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>6. The identity in step <1>5 is impossible.

::: {.proof}
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
$$
h(2\pi)-h(0)
=
\int_0^{2\pi}h'(t)\,dt
=
2\pi.
$$
But
$$
(\cos0,\sin0)
=
(\cos2\pi,\sin2\pi)
=
(1,0),
$$
so $h(2\pi)=h(0)$, a contradiction.
:::

<1>7. Consequently, no function satisfying the equations in part 2
exists.

::: {.proof}
The assumption of existence led to the contradiction in step <1>6.
:::

<1>8. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 proves part 1, and step <1>7 proves part 2.
:::
