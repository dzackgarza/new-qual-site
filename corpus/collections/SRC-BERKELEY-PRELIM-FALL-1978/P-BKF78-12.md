::: {.problem}
Let $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ satisfy the following conditions: $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}$, $f$ is continuous at $0$, and
\[
\lim_{p\to0}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(p)=0
\]
for $i=1,\ldots,n$.
Prove that $f$ is differentiable at $0$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Use the Euclidean norm on $\RR^n$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #uniform-partial-bound}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that
$$
0<\norm p<\delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\abs{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(p)}
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt n}
$$
for every $i=1,\ldots,n$.

::: pf-proof
For each $i$, the hypothesis
$$
\lim_{p\to0}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(p)=0
$$
gives a radius $\delta_i>0$ on which the displayed bound holds for
the $i$th partial derivative. Since there are only finitely many
indices, take
$$
\delta=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\delta_i.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #difference-bound}
If $0<\norm x<\delta$, then
$$
\abs{f(x)-f(0)}
<
\varepsilon\norm x.
$$

::: pf-proof
Fix such an $x$ and define
$$
g(t)=f(tx),
\qquad 0\leq t\leq1.
$$
The continuity of $f$ at $0$ makes $g$ continuous at $t=0$. For
$0<t\leq1$, the point $tx$ is nonzero, so differentiability of $f$ at
$tx$ implies continuity there; hence $g$ is continuous on $[0,1]$.
The chain rule also shows that $g$ is differentiable for $0<t<1$.
The one-variable mean value theorem therefore gives
$c\in(0,1)$ such that
$$
f(x)-f(0)
=
g'(c)
=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(cx)x_i.
$$
Because
$$
0<\norm{cx}<\delta,
$$
step [](#uniform-partial-bound){.pf-ref} yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\abs{f(x)-f(0)}
&\leq
\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt n}
\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{x_i}\\
&\leq
\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt n}
\sqrt n\norm x\\
&=
\varepsilon\norm x,
\end{aligned}
$$
where the second inequality is Cauchy--Schwarz.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #differentiable-at-zero}
The function $f$ is differentiable at $0$ with derivative the
zero linear map.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#difference-bound){.pf-ref} shows that for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
0<\norm x<\delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\abs{f(x)-f(0)}}{\norm x}<\varepsilon.
$$
Hence
$$
\lim_{x\to0}
\frac{\abs{f(x)-f(0)}}{\norm x}
=
0.
$$
This is precisely differentiability at $0$ with derivative equal to
the zero linear map.
:::

:::

::: pf-qed
Step [](#differentiable-at-zero){.pf-ref} is the required conclusion.
:::
