::: {.problem}
Recall the cube quotient from
[[P-UCTOP290-S3-5|sheet 3, Problem 5]]: opposite front and back
faces and opposite top and bottom faces are identified by
translations, while the left and right faces are identified by
a quarter turn. Describe the universal cover and deduce that the
quotient is a $3$-manifold.
:::

::: {.solution}

Use coordinates $(x,y,z)$ on the cube, with $x$ running from left
to right, and let $\rho(y,z)=(1-z,y)$ be the quarter turn of the
square. On $\RR^3$ let $\Gamma$ be generated by
$$
u(x,y,z)=(x,y+1,z),\qquad
v(x,y,z)=(x,y,z+1),\qquad
t(x,y,z)=(x+1,\rho(y,z)).
$$
The unit cube is a fundamental domain, and its paired faces give
precisely the source quotient $X=\RR^3/\Gamma$.

Every element of $\Gamma$ has the form
$u^m v^n t^k$. If $k\ne0$, it moves the $x$ coordinate by $k$.
If $k=0$ and $(m,n)\ne(0,0)$, it is a nonzero translation in the
$(y,z)$ plane. Thus the action is free. It is properly
discontinuous: only finitely many $x$ shifts and lattice
translations can move a fixed compact set to meet itself.
Consequently
$$
\RR^3\longrightarrow X
$$
is a covering map. Since $\RR^3$ is simply connected, it is the
universal cover.

Every point of $X$ has a neighborhood evenly covered by open
subsets of $\RR^3$, each homeomorphic to an open $3$-ball.
Therefore $X$ is a $3$-manifold. The finite cube quotient is
compact, and it has no boundary because all faces are paired.

:::
