::: {.problem}
(a) State the definition of what it means for a topological space to be regular.
Since there is some ambiguity in the literature about the definition of the term “regular”, include the Hausdorff property in the definition (some use “regular Hausdorff” or “$T_3$” for the same thing).

(b) Prove that a subspace of a regular space is also regular.

(c) Prove that a product of two regular spaces (equipped with the product topology) is also regular.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. A topological space $X$ is **regular** (in the convention of the problem) if $X$ is Hausdorff and whenever $x\in X$ and $F\subseteq X$ is closed with $x\notin F$, there exist disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ such that
$$
x\in U,\qquad F\subseteq V.
$$
::: {.proof}
This is the stated $T_3$ convention: regularity together with the Hausdorff axiom.
:::

<1>2. In a regular space, if $x\in O$ with $O$ open, then there is an open set $U$ such that
$$
x\in U\subseteq \overline U\subseteq O.
$$
::: {.proof}
Apply regularity to the point $x$ and the closed set $F=X\setminus O$. Choose disjoint open sets $U,W$ with $x\in U$ and $F\subseteq W$. Since $U\cap W=\varnothing$, we have $\overline U\subseteq X\setminus W\subseteq O$.
:::

<1>3. Let $A\subseteq X$ be a subspace of a regular space $X$. Then $A$ is Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
The Hausdorff property is hereditary: if $a\neq a'$ in $A$, choose disjoint open neighborhoods $U,U'$ of them in $X$; then $U\cap A$ and $U'\cap A$ are disjoint open neighborhoods in $A$.
:::

<1>4. Let $a\in A$ and let $F\subseteq A$ be closed with $a\notin F$. Then $a$ and $F$ have disjoint open neighborhoods in $A$.
::: {.proof}
Because $F$ is closed in the subspace $A$, there is a closed set $C\subseteq X$ with
$$
F=A\cap C.
$$
Since $a\in A$ and $a\notin F$, we have $a\notin C$. By regularity of $X$, choose disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ with $a\in U$ and $C\subseteq V$. Then $U\cap A$ and $V\cap A$ are disjoint open subsets of $A$, the first contains $a$, and the second contains $F$.
:::

<1>5. Hence every subspace of a regular space is regular.
::: {.proof}
Combine <1>3 and <1>4 with the definition in <1>1.
:::

<1>6. Let $X$ and $Y$ be regular. Then $X\times Y$ is Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
If $(x_1,y_1)\neq(x_2,y_2)$, at least one coordinate differs. Separate the differing coordinates by disjoint open sets in the corresponding Hausdorff factor and take their products with the whole other factor.
:::

<1>7. Let $(x,y)\in X\times Y$ and let $O\subseteq X\times Y$ be an open neighborhood of $(x,y)$. There are open sets $U_0\subseteq X$ and $V_0\subseteq Y$ such that
$$
(x,y)\in U_0\times V_0\subseteq O.
$$
::: {.proof}
The sets $U_0\times V_0$ form a basis for the product topology.
:::

<1>8. There exist open sets $U\subseteq X$ and $V\subseteq Y$ satisfying
$$
x\in U\subseteq\overline U\subseteq U_0,
\qquad
y\in V\subseteq\overline V\subseteq V_0.
$$
::: {.proof}
Apply the shrinking property <1>2 separately in the regular spaces $X$ and $Y$.
:::

<1>9. The open set $U\times V$ satisfies
$$
(x,y)\in U\times V\subseteq \overline{U\times V}\subseteq O.
$$
::: {.proof}
For products of two subsets,
$$
\overline{U\times V}=\overline U\times\overline V.
$$
Hence <1>8 gives
$$
\overline{U\times V}\subseteq U_0\times V_0\subseteq O.
$$
:::

<1>10. Therefore $X\times Y$ is regular.
::: {.proof}
By <1>6 it is Hausdorff. Given a point $(x,y)$ and a closed set $F$ not containing it, apply <1>9 to the open neighborhood $O=(X\times Y)\setminus F$. Then $U\times V$ and $(X\times Y)\setminus\overline{U\times V}$ are disjoint open sets containing $(x,y)$ and $F$, respectively. Thus the definition in <1>1 holds.
:::
:::
