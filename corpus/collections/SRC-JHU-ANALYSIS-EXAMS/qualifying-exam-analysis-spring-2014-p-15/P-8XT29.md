This problem is about the integral

$$I = \int_0^\infty \frac{\cos u \, du}{u^4 + 1}.$$

(a) Show directly that $I$ is a convergent improper Riemann integral.

(b) Is

$$\int_{[0,\infty)} \frac{\cos u}{u^4 + 1} \, d\mu(u)$$

a well-defined Lebesgue integral, where $\mu$ denotes the Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$?

(c) (main part) Evaluate the integral in (a).

::: solution
Both integrals exist, agree, and have the value
$$
\boxed{\frac{\pi e^{-1/\sqrt2}}{2\sqrt2}
\left(\cos\frac1{\sqrt2}+\sin\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)}.
$$

<1>1. Parts (a) and (b): absolute convergence holds in both senses.

::: proof
The function $h(u)=\cos u/(1+u^4)$ is continuous on
$[0,\infty)$. It is bounded by one in absolute value
on $[0,1]$ and by $u^{-4}$ for $u\geq1$. Since
$\int_1^\infty u^{-4}\,du=1/3$, comparison proves
absolute convergence of the improper Riemann integral.
The same bounds prove $h\in L^1([0,\infty),\mu)$, since
continuity implies measurability [@Fol13]. On each finite
interval the Riemann and Lebesgue integrals coincide;
dominated convergence, with the displayed integrable
majorant, shows that their infinite-interval limits agree.
:::

<1>2. The exponential integrand has two relevant simple poles.

::: proof
Consider $F(z)=e^{iz}/(1+z^4)$. The zeros of the denominator
in the upper half-plane are
$$
\alpha=\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\beta=\frac{-1+i}{\sqrt2}.
$$
They are simple because $4z^3$ is nonzero there, so the
residue sum is $S=e^{i\alpha}/(4\alpha^3)+e^{i\beta}/(4\beta^3)$
[@SS03]. Set $s=1/\sqrt2$. Then
$$
\alpha^{-3}=\frac{-1-i}{\sqrt2},\quad
\beta^{-3}=\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2},\quad
e^{i\alpha}=e^{-s}e^{is},\quad e^{i\beta}=e^{-s}e^{-is}.
$$
It follows that
$$
S=\frac{e^{-s}}{4\sqrt2}
\bigl((-1-i)e^{is}+(1-i)e^{-is}\bigr)
=-\frac{ie^{-s}}{2\sqrt2}(\cos s+\sin s).
$$
:::

<1>3. Part (c): the upper semicircle evaluates the real integral.

::: proof
For $R>1$, close $[-R,R]$ with the counterclockwise
upper semicircle $\Gamma_R$. On this arc,
$|e^{iz}|\leq1$ and $|1+z^4|\geq R^4-1$, so
$$
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R}F(z)\,dz\right|
\leq\frac{\pi R}{R^4-1}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The residue theorem gives the contour integral $2\pi iS$.
The real-line integral of $F$ is absolutely convergent
by the same comparison as in step <1>1. Letting $R\to\infty$
and taking real parts therefore gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\cos u}{1+u^4}\,du
=\frac{\pi e^{-s}}{\sqrt2}(\cos s+\sin s).
$$
Evenness makes the left side equal to $2I$. Dividing
by two and substituting $s=1/\sqrt2$ proves the answer.
:::
:::
