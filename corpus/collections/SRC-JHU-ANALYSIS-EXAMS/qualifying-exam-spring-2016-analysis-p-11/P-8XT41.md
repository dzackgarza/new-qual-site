Assume that $f : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ is an entire function, not identically equal to 0 and let $\mathcal{Z} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : f(z) = 0\}$.
Prove that if $\mathcal{Z}$ is unbounded, then $f$ has an essential singularity at $\infty$.

::: solution
Consider $g(w)=f(1/w)$ for $w\ne0$. It is holomorphic
on the punctured plane. The type of the isolated singularity
of $f$ at infinity is, by definition, the type of $g$ at zero.
Since the zero set is unbounded, choose distinct zeros
$z_n$ with $|z_n|\to\infty$ and $z_n\ne0$. Then
$w_n=1/z_n$ are distinct, tend to zero, and satisfy
$g(w_n)=0$.

<1>1. The singularity of $g$ at zero is not removable.

::: proof
If it were removable, $g$ would have a holomorphic extension
$G$ to a disk about zero. The zeros $w_n$ would accumulate
at the interior point zero. The identity theorem would
give $G=0$ on that disk [@SS03]. Thus $f(z)=g(1/z)=0$
for all sufficiently large $|z|$. Applying the identity
theorem to the entire function $f$ would give $f=0$
on $\mathbb C$, contrary to the hypothesis.
:::

<1>2. The singularity is not a pole and hence is essential.

::: proof
If zero were a pole of order $m\geq1$, one could write
$g(w)=w^{-m}h(w)$ near zero, with $h$ holomorphic and
$h(0)\ne0$ [@SS03]. Continuity would make $h$, and hence
$g$, nonzero in a sufficiently small punctured disk.
This contradicts the sequence of zeros $w_n\to0$.

An isolated singularity is removable, a pole, or essential
[@SS03]. The first two cases have been excluded, so
$g$ has an essential singularity at zero. Equivalently,
$f$ has an essential singularity at infinity.
:::
:::
