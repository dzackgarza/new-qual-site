::: problem
Let $f:D(0,1)\to\mathbb C$ be a function such that $f^2$ and $f^3$ are holomorphic. Prove that $f$ is holomorphic.
:::

::: solution
Put $A=f^2$ and $B=f^3$, both holomorphic by hypothesis.
If $A$ is identically zero, then $f(z)^2=0$ at every point,
so $f=0$ and the conclusion holds. Suppose $A\not\equiv0$.

<1>1. The function $f$ is holomorphic off the discrete zero set of $A$.

::: proof
The zeros of the nonzero holomorphic function $A$ on the
connected disk are isolated [@SS03]. At any point where
$A\ne0$, the identity $f=B/A$ follows from the definitions.
The quotient is holomorphic on the open set where its
denominator is nonzero. Thus $f$ is holomorphic there.
:::

<1>2. At every zero of $A$, the quotient extends with exactly the given value of $f$.

::: proof
Let $a$ be a zero of $A$ and choose a disk about $a$
containing no other zero. On its punctured interior,
step <1>1 applies and
$$
|f(z)|^2=|A(z)|\longrightarrow |A(a)|=0
\qquad(z\to a).
$$
Hence $f(z)\to0$ and in particular $f$ is bounded there.
The removable-singularity theorem extends it holomorphically
to $a$ with value zero [@SS03]. But $f(a)^2=A(a)=0$
already forces $f(a)=0$. The extension therefore agrees
with the original function even at $a$. Applying this
at every isolated zero proves that the given $f$ itself
is holomorphic throughout the disk.
:::
:::
