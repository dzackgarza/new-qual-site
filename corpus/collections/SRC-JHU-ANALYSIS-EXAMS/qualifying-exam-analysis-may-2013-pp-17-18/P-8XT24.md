::: {.problem}
Calculate the following integral:

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{1/2}}{4 + x^2} \, dx.$$
:::

::: {.solution}
The integral equals $\boxed{\pi/2}$.

<1>1. The integral converges at both endpoints.

::: {.proof}
For $0<x\leq1$, the positive integrand is at most
$x^{1/2}/4$, which is integrable near zero. For $x\geq1$,
it is at most $x^{-3/2}$, which is integrable at infinity.
Denote the resulting finite integral by $I$.
:::

<1>2. An indented upper semicircle gives a residue identity.

::: {.proof}
Choose the logarithm branch with
$-\pi/2<\arg z<3\pi/2$, and define
$z^{1/2}=\exp(\operatorname{Log}z/2)$ [@SS03]. Its cut
is the nonpositive imaginary axis; hence the branch is
holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed upper
half-plane away from zero. For $x>0$, it gives
$x^{1/2}=\sqrt x$ and $(-x)^{1/2}=i\sqrt x$.

For $0<\varepsilon<1<2<R$, integrate
$F(z)=z^{1/2}/(z^2+4)$ along $[-R,-\varepsilon]$, a
clockwise upper semicircle of radius $\varepsilon$,
$[\varepsilon,R]$, and the counterclockwise upper
semicircle of radius $R$. This positively oriented
contour encloses only the pole $2i$. Since $(2i)^{1/2}=1+i$,
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{2i}F=\frac{1+i}{4i}.
$$
The substitution $z=-x$ on the negative real segment
shows that the two straight segments contribute
$$
(1+i)\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\sqrt x}{x^2+4}\,dx.
$$
The residue theorem equates this contribution plus the
two arc integrals to $\pi(1+i)/2$ [@SS03].
:::

<1>3. The arc integrals vanish, determining $I$.

::: {.proof}
On the outer semicircle, $|z^{1/2}|=\sqrt R$ and
$|z^2+4|\geq R^2-4$. Its integral therefore has modulus
at most $\pi R^{3/2}/(R^2-4)$, which tends to zero.
On the inner semicircle the corresponding bound is
$\pi\varepsilon^{3/2}/(4-\varepsilon^2)$, also tending
to zero. These estimates use the arc lengths $\pi R$
and $\pi\varepsilon$ and do not depend on orientation.

Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ in
step <1>2 gives
$$
(1+i)I=\frac{\pi}{2}(1+i).
$$
The endpoint limits exist by step <1>1, and $1+i\ne0$.
Division yields $I=\pi/2$.
:::
:::
