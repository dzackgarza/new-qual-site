::: {.problem}
Show that
\[
f(x)=\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}
\]
belongs to $L^1(\mathbb R)$. Then evaluate
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The function is absolutely integrable.
::: {.proof}
Since $|\cos x|\le1$,
\[
\int_{\mathbb R}\left|\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\right|\,dx
\le
\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{dx}{1+x^2}
=\pi<\infty.
\]
Hence $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$.
:::

<1>2. Evaluate the integral by residues.
::: {.proof}
Consider
\[
F(z)=\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}
\]
and integrate over the upper-half-plane semicircle of radius $R>1$. The only pole inside the contour is the simple pole at $z=i$, with residue
\[
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}F
=\lim_{z\to i}\frac{e^{iz}}{z+i}
=\frac{e^{-1}}{2i}.
\]
Therefore the residue theorem gives
\[
\int_{-R}^R\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}\,dx
+\int_{\Gamma_R}\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}\,dz
=2\pi i\cdot\frac{e^{-1}}{2i}
=\frac{\pi}{e}.
\]
On the upper semicircle, $|e^{iz}|=e^{-\operatorname{Im}z}\le1$, so for $R>1$,
\[
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R}\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}\,dz\right|
\le
\frac{\pi R}{R^2-1}\longrightarrow0.
\]
Letting $R\to\infty$ yields
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{e}.
\]
Taking real parts,
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{e}.
\]
The integrand is even, so
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{2e}.
\]
