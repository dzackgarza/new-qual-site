::: {.problem}
Assume real value functions u, v of two variables have continuous partial derivatives at $( x _ { 0 } , y _ { 0 } )$ . Show that $f = u + i v$ has derivative $f ^ { \prime } ( z _ { 0 } )$ at $z _ { 0 } = x _ { 0 } + i y _ { 0 }$ if and only if

$$
\lim_{r\to0}\frac{1}{\pi r^2}\int_{|z-z_0|=r}f(z)\,dz=0.
$$
:::

Write
$$
A=f_x(z_0)=u_x(x_0,y_0)+iv_x(x_0,y_0),
\qquad
B=f_y(z_0)=u_y(x_0,y_0)+iv_y(x_0,y_0).
$$

::: pf

::: pf-step

As $h=x+iy\to0$,
$$
f(z_0+h)=f(z_0)+Ax+By+o(\abs h).
$$


::: pf-proof

The hypotheses on the first partial derivatives imply that the real-valued functions $u$ and $v$ are differentiable as functions of $(x,y)$ at $(x_0,y_0)$. Hence
$$
u(x_0+x,y_0+y)
=u(x_0,y_0)+u_x\,x+u_y\,y+o(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}),
$$
and similarly
$$
v(x_0+x,y_0+y)
=v(x_0,y_0)+v_x\,x+v_y\,y+o(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}),
$$
where the partial derivatives on the right are evaluated at $(x_0,y_0)$. Combining the two expansions gives the displayed formula.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
\lim_{r\to0}\frac1{\pi r^2}
\int_{\abs{z-z_0}=r}f(z)\,dz
=iA-B.
$$


::: pf-proof

Parametrize the positively oriented circle by
$$
z=z_0+re^{it},
\qquad 0\leq t\leq2\pi.
$$
Then
$$
x=r\cos t,
\qquad
y=r\sin t,
\qquad
dz=ire^{it}\,dt.
$$
The integral of the constant term $f(z_0)$ is zero. By step <1>1, uniformly on the circle as $r\to0$,
$$
f(z)-f(z_0)=Ar\cos t+Br\sin t+o(r).
$$
Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\abs{z-z_0}=r}f(z)\,dz
&=ir^2A\int_0^{2\pi}\cos t\,e^{it}\,dt
+ir^2B\int_0^{2\pi}\sin t\,e^{it}\,dt
+o(r^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Since
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\cos t\,e^{it}\,dt=\pi,
\qquad
\int_0^{2\pi}\sin t\,e^{it}\,dt=i\pi,
$$
it follows that
$$
\int_{\abs{z-z_0}=r}f(z)\,dz
=\pi r^2(iA-B)+o(r^2).
$$
Division by $\pi r^2$ and passage to the limit gives the claim.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The circle-integral limit is zero if and only if the Cauchy--Riemann equations hold at $(x_0,y_0)$.


::: pf-proof

By step <1>2, the limit vanishes exactly when
$$
B=iA.
$$
Writing this out gives
$$
u_y+iv_y=i(u_x+iv_x)=-v_x+iu_x.
$$
Equality of real and imaginary parts is therefore equivalent to
$$
u_x=v_y,
\qquad
u_y=-v_x,
$$
which are the Cauchy--Riemann equations at $(x_0,y_0)$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The function $f$ is complex differentiable at $z_0$ if and only if the Cauchy--Riemann equations hold at $(x_0,y_0)$.


::: pf-proof

Under the real differentiability established in step <1>1, the Cauchy--Riemann equations are equivalent to complex differentiability. Indeed, if they hold, then $B=iA$, and
$$
Ax+By=A(x+iy)=Ah.
$$
Thus step <1>1 becomes
$$
f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)=Ah+o(\abs h),
$$
so
$$
\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}h\longrightarrow A.
$$
Conversely, if the complex derivative exists, restricting the difference quotient to real increments gives $f'(z_0)=A$, while restricting to purely imaginary increments gives $f'(z_0)=B/i=-iB$. Hence $A=-iB$, equivalently $B=iA$, which is the Cauchy--Riemann condition.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{f'(z_0)\text{ exists}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\lim_{r\to0}\frac1{\pi r^2}
\int_{\abs{z-z_0}=r}f(z)\,dz=0.}
$$


::: pf-proof

By step <1>3, the vanishing circle-integral limit is equivalent to the Cauchy--Riemann equations. By step <1>4, those equations are equivalent to complex differentiability at $z_0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step <1>5 is the required equivalence.

:::
