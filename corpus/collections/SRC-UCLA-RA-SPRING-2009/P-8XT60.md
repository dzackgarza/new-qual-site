::: {.problem}
Let $f(x)$ be a non-decreasing function on $[0,1]$.
You may assume the theorem that $f$ is differentiable almost everywhere.

(a) Prove that $\int_0^1 f'(x)\,dx\le f(1)-f(0)$.
(Hint: Fatou.)

(b) Let $\{f_n\}$ be a sequence of non-decreasing functions on the unit interval $[0,1]$, such that the series $F(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n(x)$ converges for all $x\in[0,1]$.
Prove that $F'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n'(x)$ almost everywhere on $[0,1]$.

Hint: Let $r_n(x)=\sum_{k\ge n}f_k(x)$.
It is enough to show $r_n'(x)\to 0$ a.e. Take a subsequence $r_{n_j}$ such that $r_{n_j}(1)-r_{n_j}(0)\to 0$ and use part (a).
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $f$ is non-decreasing on $[0,1]$, then
\[
\int_0^1 f'(x)\,dx\le f(1)-f(0).
\]
::: {.proof}
Extend $f$ to $[0,\infty)$ by setting $f(x)=f(1)$ for $x>1$.
For $h>0$ define
\[
q_h(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},
\qquad 0\le x\le1.
\]
Because $f$ is non-decreasing, $q_h\ge0$.
At every point where $f'$ exists,
\[
q_h(x)\longrightarrow f'(x)
\qquad(h\to0^+).
\]
Choose any sequence $h_j\downarrow0$. By Fatou's lemma,
\[
\int_0^1 f'(x)\,dx
\le \liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_0^1 q_{h_j}(x)\,dx.
\]
For every $h>0$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 q_h(x)\,dx
&=\frac1h\left(
\int_1^{1+h}f(u)\,du-
\int_0^h f(u)\,du
\right)\\
&\le \frac1h\bigl(hf(1)-hf(0)\bigr)\\
&=f(1)-f(0).
\end{aligned}
\]
The claimed inequality follows.
:::

For part (b), let
\[
S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^N f_n(x),
\qquad
r_N(x)=F(x)-S_N(x)=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}f_n(x).
\]
Every $S_N$, $r_N$, and $F$ is non-decreasing: for $x<y$, the corresponding inequalities hold for every finite partial sum and persist after taking limits.
Hence all of these functions are differentiable almost everywhere.
After removing one null set, we may assume simultaneously that $F'$, every $f_n'$, and every $r_N'$ exist.

<1>2. On this common full-measure set,
\[
0\le r_{N+1}'(x)\le r_N'(x)
\qquad(N\ge1).
\]
::: {.proof}
Each $r_N$ is non-decreasing, so $r_N'(x)\ge0$ wherever the derivative exists.
Moreover
\[
r_N-r_{N+1}=f_{N+1},
\]
so at every point in the common differentiability set,
\[
r_N'(x)-r_{N+1}'(x)=f_{N+1}'(x)\ge0.
\]
:::

<1>3. One has
\[
r_N'(x)\longrightarrow0
\]
for almost every $x\in[0,1]$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>2, the pointwise limit
\[
h(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}r_N'(x)
\]
exists and is nonnegative on the common full-measure set.
Part (a), applied to $r_N$, gives
\[
0\le\int_0^1 r_N'(x)\,dx
\le r_N(1)-r_N(0).
\]
Because the defining series for $F$ converges at both endpoints,
\[
r_N(1)\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
r_N(0)\longrightarrow0.
\]
Thus
\[
r_N(1)-r_N(0)\longrightarrow0.
\]
Since $0\le h\le r_N'$ for every $N$,
\[
0\le\int_0^1 h(x)\,dx
\le\int_0^1 r_N'(x)\,dx
\le r_N(1)-r_N(0).
\]
Letting $N\to\infty$ shows $\int_0^1 h=0$. Since $h\ge0$, one has $h=0$ almost everywhere.
:::

<1>4. Therefore
\[
F'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n'(x)
\]
for almost every $x\in[0,1]$.
::: {.proof}
At every point in the common full-measure set,
\[
F'(x)=S_N'(x)+r_N'(x)
=\sum_{n=1}^N f_n'(x)+r_N'(x).
\]
By <1>3, the last term tends to zero for almost every $x$.
Passing to the limit in $N$ gives
\[
F'(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N f_n'(x)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n'(x)
\]
almost everywhere.
:::
