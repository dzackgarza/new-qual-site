::: {.problem}
Let $p:\widetilde X\to X$ be a covering map, and $f:Y\to X$ be a continuous map.
Define $$\widetilde Y=\{(y,\widetilde x)\in Y\times\widetilde X:f(y)=p(\widetilde x)\}\subseteq Y\times\widetilde X,$$ with the subspace topology inherited from $Y\times\widetilde X$, and define $q:\widetilde Y\to Y$ by $q(y,\widetilde x)=y$.
Show that $q$ is also a covering map.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The map $q:\widetilde Y\to Y$ is continuous and surjective.
::: {.proof}
The projection
\[
\operatorname{pr}_1:Y\times\widetilde X\longrightarrow Y
\]
is continuous, and $q$ is its restriction to the subspace $\widetilde Y$, so $q$ is continuous.

Let $y\in Y$.
Since $p:\widetilde X\to X$ is a covering map, it is surjective, so there is some $\widetilde x\in\widetilde X$ with
\[
p(\widetilde x)=f(y).
\]
Then $(y,\widetilde x)\in\widetilde Y$ and
\[
q(y,\widetilde x)=y.
\]
Thus $q$ is surjective.
:::

<1>2. Fix $y_0\in Y$ and set $x_0=f(y_0)$.
Choose an evenly covered open neighborhood $U$ of $x_0$ for $p$, so that
\[
p^{-1}(U)=\coprod_{\alpha\in A}U_\alpha,
\]
where every $U_\alpha$ is open in $\widetilde X$ and
\[
p_\alpha:=p|_{U_\alpha}:U_\alpha\longrightarrow U
\]
is a homeomorphism.
Let
\[
V=f^{-1}(U).
\]
Then $V$ is an open neighborhood of $y_0$ in $Y$.
::: {.proof}
The existence of $U$ and the decomposition of $p^{-1}(U)$ are exactly the covering-map property for $p$ at $x_0$.
Since $f$ is continuous and $U$ is open,
\[
V=f^{-1}(U)
\]
is open in $Y$; moreover $f(y_0)=x_0\in U$, so $y_0\in V$.
:::

<1>3. For each $\alpha\in A$, define
\[
W_\alpha
=
\widetilde Y\cap(V\times U_\alpha).
\]
Then the sets $W_\alpha$ are pairwise disjoint and open in $\widetilde Y$, and
\[
q^{-1}(V)=\coprod_{\alpha\in A}W_\alpha.
\]
::: {.proof}
Because $V$ is open in $Y$ and $U_\alpha$ is open in $\widetilde X$, the product
\[
V\times U_\alpha
\]
is open in $Y\times\widetilde X$.
Hence its intersection $W_\alpha$ with the subspace $\widetilde Y$ is open in $\widetilde Y$.

The $U_\alpha$ are pairwise disjoint, so the $W_\alpha$ are pairwise disjoint.

Now $(y,\widetilde x)\in q^{-1}(V)$ exactly when
\[
y\in V,
\qquad
f(y)=p(\widetilde x).
\]
Since $y\in V=f^{-1}(U)$, we have $f(y)\in U$, and therefore
\[
p(\widetilde x)=f(y)\in U.
\]
Thus
\[
\widetilde x\in p^{-1}(U)=\coprod_{\alpha\in A}U_\alpha,
\]
so $(y,\widetilde x)$ belongs to exactly one $W_\alpha$.
This proves the displayed decomposition.
:::

<1>4. For every $\alpha\in A$, the restriction
\[
q_\alpha:=q|_{W_\alpha}:W_\alpha\longrightarrow V
\]
is a homeomorphism.
::: {.proof}
Since
\[
p_\alpha:U_\alpha\longrightarrow U
\]
is a homeomorphism, define
\[
s_\alpha=p_\alpha^{-1}:U\longrightarrow U_\alpha.
\]
For $y\in V$, set
\[
r_\alpha(y)=\bigl(y,s_\alpha(f(y))\bigr).
\]
Because $f(y)\in U$ and
\[
p\bigl(s_\alpha(f(y))\bigr)=f(y),
\]
the point $r_\alpha(y)$ lies in $\widetilde Y$; its second coordinate lies in $U_\alpha$, so in fact
\[
r_\alpha(y)\in W_\alpha.
\]

The map $r_\alpha:V\to W_\alpha$ is continuous because its two coordinate maps
\[
y\longmapsto y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
y\longmapsto s_\alpha(f(y))
\]
are continuous.
Also
\[
q_\alpha(r_\alpha(y))=y.
\]

Conversely, if $(y,\widetilde x)\in W_\alpha$, then
\[
p_\alpha(\widetilde x)=p(\widetilde x)=f(y).
\]
Since $s_\alpha=p_\alpha^{-1}$,
\[
s_\alpha(f(y))=\widetilde x,
\]
and hence
\[
r_\alpha(q_\alpha(y,\widetilde x))
=
r_\alpha(y)
=
(y,\widetilde x).
\]
Thus $r_\alpha$ and $q_\alpha$ are continuous inverse maps, so $q_\alpha$ is a homeomorphism.
:::

<1>5. Therefore $q:\widetilde Y\to Y$ is a covering map.
::: {.proof}
For the arbitrary point $y_0\in Y$, <1>2 gives an open neighborhood $V$.
By <1>3,
\[
q^{-1}(V)=\coprod_{\alpha\in A}W_\alpha
\]
is a disjoint union of open subsets of $\widetilde Y$, and by <1>4 each restriction
\[
q|_{W_\alpha}:W_\alpha\longrightarrow V
\]
is a homeomorphism.
Thus $V$ is evenly covered by $q$.
Since $y_0$ was arbitrary, every point of $Y$ has an evenly covered neighborhood, so $q$ is a covering map.
:::
