::: problem
Suppose $A$ is an uncountable subset of $\mathbb{R}$ and $A'$ is the set of limit points of $A$.
Prove $A \cap A'$ is uncountable.
:::

::: solution
The set
\[
A\setminus A'
\]
consists exactly of the isolated points of $A$.

For each $x\in A\setminus A'$, choose an open interval $I_x$ such that
\[
I_x\cap A=\{x\}.
\]
Because the intervals with rational endpoints form a countable base for $\mathbb R$, we may choose rational numbers $p_x<q_x$ such that
\[
x\in(p_x,q_x)\subset I_x.
\]

The map
\[
x\longmapsto(p_x,q_x)
\]
is injective. Indeed, if two isolated points $x\ne y$ had the same rational interval $(p,q)$, then both $x$ and $y$ would belong to $(p,q)\subset I_x$, contradicting
\[
I_x\cap A=\{x\}.
\]

There are only countably many pairs of rational numbers, so $A\setminus A'$ is countable. Since
\[
A=(A\cap A')\sqcup(A\setminus A')
\]
and $A$ is uncountable, $A\cap A'$ cannot be countable. Therefore
\[
\boxed{A\cap A'\text{ is uncountable}.}
\]
:::
