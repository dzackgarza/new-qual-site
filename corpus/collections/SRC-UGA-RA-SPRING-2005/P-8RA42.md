::: problem
Let $f$ be continuously differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, and suppose that $f$ and $f'$ are both integrable.
Prove
\[
\lim_{k \rightarrow \infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sin(kx)f(x)\, dx = 0.
\]
:::

::: solution
<1>1. Show that $f(x)\to0$ as $x\to\pm\infty$.
::: proof
Since $f'\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, for $y>x$,
\[
f(y)-f(x)=\int_x^y f'(t)\,dt.
\]
Hence $(f(x))_{x\to\infty}$ is Cauchy: if $x,y$ are both large,
\[
|f(y)-f(x)|\le\int_{\min(x,y)}^\infty|f'(t)|\,dt\longrightarrow0.
\]
Thus the finite limit
\[
L_+:=\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)
\]
exists. Because $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, this limit must be $0$; otherwise $|f|$ would be bounded below by a positive constant on a tail and could not be integrable.

The same argument on the negative half-line gives
\[
\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=0.
\]
:::

<1>2. Integrate by parts.
::: proof
For $R>0$ and $k>0$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-R}^R\sin(kx)f(x)\,dx
&=\left[-\frac{\cos(kx)}k f(x)\right]_{-R}^R
+\frac1k\int_{-R}^R\cos(kx)f'(x)\,dx.
\end{aligned}
\]
By Step 1, the boundary term tends to $0$ as $R\to\infty$. Since $f'\in L^1$, dominated convergence lets us pass to the limit in the remaining integral. Therefore
\[
\int_{-\infty}^\infty\sin(kx)f(x)\,dx
=\frac1k\int_{-\infty}^\infty\cos(kx)f'(x)\,dx.
\]
Consequently
\[
\left|
\int_{-\infty}^\infty\sin(kx)f(x)\,dx
\right|
\le\frac{\|f'\|_1}{k}.
\]
Letting $k\to\infty$ gives
\[
\boxed{
\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\sin(kx)f(x)\,dx=0.}
\]
:::
:::
