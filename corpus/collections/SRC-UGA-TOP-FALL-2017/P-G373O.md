::: problem
A metric space $(X, d)$ is said to be **totally bounded** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a finite cover of $X$ by open balls of radius $\varepsilon$.

(a) Prove that a metric space $X$ is totally bounded if and only if every sequence in $X$ has a Cauchy subsequence.

(b) Exhibit a complete metric space $X$ and a closed subset $A \subseteq X$ that is bounded but not totally bounded.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Prove the sequential characterization of total boundedness in (a), and provide an explicit example of a closed, bounded, non-totally bounded subset of a complete metric space in (b).

<1>1. Part (a) ($\implies$): If $X$ is totally bounded, every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $(x_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ be an arbitrary sequence in $X$.
    <2>2. For $k = 1$, cover $X$ by finitely many open balls of radius $1$: $X = \bigcup_{i=1}^{N_1} B(c_{1, i}, 1)$.
    <2>3. By the Pigeonhole Principle, at least one ball $B(c_1, 1)$ contains $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$. Let $(x_{1, n})_{n=1}^\infty$ be the subsequence of terms lying in $B(c_1, 1)$.
    <2>4. Inductively for each $k \ge 2$, cover $X$ by finitely many open balls of radius $1/k$: $X = \bigcup_{i=1}^{N_k} B(c_{k, i}, 1/k)$.
    <2>5. Since the subsequence $(x_{k-1, n})_{n=1}^\infty$ is infinite, at least one of these finitely many balls, say $B(c_k, 1/k)$, contains infinitely many terms of $(x_{k-1, n})$.
    <2>6. Let $(x_{k, n})_{n=1}^\infty$ be the subsequence of $(x_{k-1, n})$ contained in $B(c_k, 1/k)$.
    <2>7. Construct the diagonal subsequence $y_n = x_{n, n}$.
    <2>8. By construction, for any $k \ge 1$ and all $n, m \ge k$, both $y_n$ and $y_m$ belong to the subsequence $(x_{k, j})$ and therefore lie in $B(c_k, 1/k)$.
    <2>9. By the triangle inequality:
    $$d(y_n, y_m) \le d(y_n, c_k) + d(c_k, y_m) < \frac{1}{k} + \frac{1}{k} = \frac{2}{k} \quad \text{for all } n, m \ge k.$$
    <2>10. For any $\varepsilon > 0$, choosing $k > 2/\varepsilon$ ensures $d(y_n, y_m) < \varepsilon$ for all $n, m \ge k$. Thus $(y_n)$ is a Cauchy subsequence.

<1>2. Part (a) ($\impliedby$): If every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence, $X$ is totally bounded.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. We prove the contrapositive: suppose $X$ is not totally bounded.
    <2>2. Then there exists some $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that $X$ cannot be covered by any finite number of open balls of radius $\varepsilon_0$.
    <2>3. Choose an arbitrary point $x_1 \in X$.
    <2>4. Since $B(x_1, \varepsilon_0) \ne X$, choose $x_2 \in X \setminus B(x_1, \varepsilon_0)$, so $d(x_2, x_1) \ge \varepsilon_0$.
    <2>5. Inductively, given $x_1, \dots, x_k \in X$ such that $d(x_i, x_j) \ge \varepsilon_0$ for all $1 \le i < j \le k$, the union $\bigcup_{i=1}^k B(x_i, \varepsilon_0)$ is a finite union of $\varepsilon_0$-balls, hence cannot cover $X$.
    <2>6. Choose $x_{k+1} \in X \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^k B(x_i, \varepsilon_0)$, so $d(x_{k+1}, x_i) \ge \varepsilon_0$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$.
    <2>7. This recursive construction produces an infinite sequence $(x_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ in $X$ such that $d(x_n, x_m) \ge \varepsilon_0$ for all $n \ne m$.
    <2>8. Any subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ of $(x_n)$ also satisfies $d(x_{n_j}, x_{n_k}) \ge \varepsilon_0$ for all $j \ne k$, and therefore cannot be Cauchy.
    <2>9. Thus there exists a sequence in $X$ with no Cauchy subsequence.

<1>3. Part (b): Construction of a closed, bounded, non-totally bounded subset in a complete metric space.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $X = \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ be the Hilbert space of square-summable real sequences $x = (x_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ equipped with the standard $\ell^2$ norm $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^\infty x_n^2}$.
    <2>2. $X = \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ is a complete metric space.
    <2>3. Let $A = \{e_n : n \ge 1\}$ be the standard orthonormal basis, where $e_n = (0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots)$ has a $1$ in the $n$-th position and zeros elsewhere.
    <2>4. $A$ is bounded: $\|e_n\|_2 = 1$ for all $n$, so $A \subseteq B(0, 2)$.
    <2>5. For any $n \ne m$:
    $$\|e_n - e_m\|_2 = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{2}.$$
    <2>6. The distance between any two distinct points in $A$ is $\sqrt{2}$. Thus every point $e_n$ is an isolated point with $B(e_n, 1) \cap A = \{e_n\}$, so $A$ is discrete, hence closed in $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$.
    <2>7. $A$ is not totally bounded: for $\varepsilon = 1/2$, any open ball of radius $1/2$ can contain at most one point of $A$. Since $A$ is infinite, $A$ cannot be covered by finitely many balls of radius $1/2$.

<1>4. Conclusion:
    *Proof:*
    Total boundedness is equivalent to sequential Cauchy precompactness, and the orthonormal set $A = \{e_n\} \subset \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ is closed and bounded but not totally bounded.
:::
