::: problem
A metric space $(X,d)$ is said to be **totally bounded** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a finite cover of $X$ by open balls of radius $\varepsilon$.

(a) Show that a metric space $X$ is totally bounded if and only if every sequence in $X$ has a Cauchy subsequence.

(b) Exhibit a complete metric space $X$ and a closed subset $A\subseteq X$ that is bounded but not totally bounded.
You are not required to prove that your example satisfies the stated properties.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $X$ is totally bounded, then every sequence in $X$ has a Cauchy subsequence.
::: {.proof}
Let $(x_n)_{n\ge1}$ be a sequence in $X$ and set $I_0=\NN$.
For each $k\ge1$, total boundedness gives a finite cover of $X$ by balls of radius $2^{-k}$.
Since $I_{k-1}$ is infinite, one ball in this cover contains $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n\in I_{k-1}$.
Let $I_k\subseteq I_{k-1}$ be the infinite set of those indices, and denote the chosen ball by
\[
B(c_k,2^{-k}).
\]
Thus
\[
x_n\in B(c_k,2^{-k})
\qquad\text{for every }n\in I_k.
\]

Choose recursively
\[
n_k\in I_k,
\qquad
n_1<n_2<\cdots.
\]
This is possible because every $I_k$ is infinite.
For $j,\ell\ge k$, nestedness gives
\[
n_j,n_\ell\in I_k,
\]
so
\[
d(x_{n_j},x_{n_\ell})
<2^{-k}+2^{-k}
=2^{1-k}.
\]
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $k$ with $2^{1-k}<\varepsilon$.
Then
\[
d(x_{n_j},x_{n_\ell})<\varepsilon
\qquad\text{for all }j,\ell\ge k.
\]
Hence $(x_{n_k})$ is a Cauchy subsequence.
:::

<1>2. If every sequence in $X$ has a Cauchy subsequence, then $X$ is totally bounded.
::: {.proof}
We prove the contrapositive.
Suppose $X$ is not totally bounded.
Then there exists $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that no finite collection of open $\varepsilon_0$-balls covers $X$.
Choose $x_1\in X$.
After choosing $x_1,\ldots,x_k$, the finite union
\[
\bigcup_{i=1}^k B(x_i,\varepsilon_0)
\]
does not cover $X$, so choose
\[
x_{k+1}\notin\bigcup_{i=1}^k B(x_i,\varepsilon_0).
\]
Then
\[
d(x_i,x_j)\ge\varepsilon_0
\qquad\text{whenever }i\ne j.
\]
Every subsequence inherits this separation, so no subsequence can be Cauchy.
Thus failure of total boundedness produces a sequence with no Cauchy subsequence, proving the contrapositive.
:::

<1>3. There is a complete metric space with a closed bounded subset that is not totally bounded.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
X=\NN,
\qquad
d(m,n)=
\begin{cases}
0,&m=n,\\
1,&m\ne n,
\end{cases}
\]
and take $A=X$.

The metric space $X$ is complete: if $(x_n)$ is Cauchy, then for sufficiently large $m,n$ one has
\[
d(x_m,x_n)<\frac12,
\]
which forces $x_m=x_n$.
Thus every Cauchy sequence is eventually constant.

The subset $A=X$ is closed and bounded, with diameter $1$.
However, every open ball of radius $1/2$ is a singleton.
Since $A$ is infinite, no finite collection of such balls covers $A$.
Hence $A$ is not totally bounded.
:::
