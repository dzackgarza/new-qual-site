::: {.problem}
Let $f:(-1,1)\to\mathbb R^2$ be $C^1$, with
\[
f(0)=0,
\qquad
f'(0)\ne0.
\]
Prove that there exists $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ such that $\|f(t)\|$ is increasing on $(0,\varepsilon)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. One has
$$
\frac{f(t)}{t}\longrightarrow f'(0)
\qquad\text{as }t\to0.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since $f(0)=0$ and $f$ is differentiable at $0$,
$$
f'(0)
=
\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(t)-f(0)}{t}
=
\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(t)}{t}.
$$
:::

<1>2. One has
$$
\left\langle\frac{f(t)}{t},f'(t)\right\rangle
\longrightarrow
\norm{f'(0)}^2>0
$$
as $t\to0$ through nonzero values.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1,
$$
\frac{f(t)}{t}\to f'(0),
$$
while continuity of $f'$ gives $f'(t)\to f'(0)$. Continuity of the inner product gives the stated limit. The limit is positive because $f'(0)\ne0$.
:::

<1>3. There exists $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ such that
$$
\left\langle\frac{f(t)}{t},f'(t)\right\rangle>0
$$
for every $t\in(0,\varepsilon)$.

::: {.proof}
This follows from the positive limit in step <1>2.
:::

<1>4. The function
$$
h(t)\coloneqq\norm{f(t)}^2
$$
is strictly increasing on $(0,\varepsilon)$.

::: {.proof}
For $0<t<\varepsilon$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
h'(t)
&=2\langle f(t),f'(t)\rangle\\
&=2t\left\langle\frac{f(t)}{t},f'(t)\right\rangle\\
&>0
\end{aligned}
$$
by step <1>3. Hence $h$ is strictly increasing there.
:::

<1>5. The function $t\mapsto\norm{f(t)}$ is strictly increasing on $(0,\varepsilon)$.

::: {.proof}
The square-root function is strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$. Since
$$
\norm{f(t)}=\sqrt{h(t)},
$$
the conclusion follows from step <1>4.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 proves the assertion.
:::
