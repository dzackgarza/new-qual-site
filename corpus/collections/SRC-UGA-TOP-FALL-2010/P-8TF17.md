::: problem
a. What does it mean to say a topological space is connected?

b. What does it mean to say a topological space is path-connected?

c. Prove that if $X$ is path-connected, then $X$ is connected.

d. Either prove or give a counterexample to the converse of part (c).
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. A topological space $X$ is connected if there do not exist disjoint nonempty open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ with $X=U\cup V$.
::: {.proof}
Equivalently, $X$ admits no separation into two disjoint nonempty open subsets.
This answers part (a).
:::

<1>2. A topological space $X$ is path-connected if for every $x_0,x_1\in X$ there is a continuous map
\[
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
\]
such that $\gamma(0)=x_0$ and $\gamma(1)=x_1$.
::: {.proof}
Such a map $\gamma$ is a path from $x_0$ to $x_1$.
This answers part (b).
:::

<1>3. Every path-connected space is connected.
::: {.proof}
Suppose $X$ is path-connected but not connected.
Then there are disjoint nonempty open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ such that
\[
X=U\cup V.
\]
Choose $u\in U$ and $v\in V$.
By path-connectedness there is a path $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$ from $u$ to $v$.

The sets
\[
\gamma^{-1}(U),\qquad \gamma^{-1}(V)
\]
are open in $[0,1]$, disjoint, and their union is $[0,1]$.
They are both nonempty because $0\in\gamma^{-1}(U)$ and $1\in\gamma^{-1}(V)$.
Thus they separate $[0,1]$, contradicting the connectedness of the interval $[0,1]$.
Therefore $X$ is connected.
:::

<1>4. The converse is false: there are connected spaces that are not path-connected.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
G=\left\{\left(x,\sin\frac1x\right):0<x\le1\right\}\subseteq\RR^2
\]
and let $T=\overline G$.
We show that $T$ is connected but not path-connected.

<2>1. $T$ is connected.
::: {.proof}
The map
\[
(0,1]\to\RR^2,
\qquad
x\longmapsto\left(x,\sin\frac1x\right)
\]
is continuous, so $G$ is connected because $(0,1]$ is connected.
The closure of a connected subspace is connected, hence $T=\overline G$ is connected.
:::

<2>2. The points
\[
p=(0,0),
\qquad
q=\left(\frac1\pi,0\right)
\]
belong to $T$.
::: {.proof}
The point $q$ lies in $G$.
Also
\[
\left(\frac1{k\pi},0\right)\in G
\]
for every positive integer $k$, and these points converge to $p$.
Thus $p\in\overline G=T$.
:::

<2>3. There is no path in $T$ from $p$ to $q$.
::: {.proof}
Assume for contradiction that
\[
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t)):[0,1]\to T
\]
is continuous with $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\gamma(1)=q$.
Let
\[
t_0=\max\{t\in[0,1]:x(t)=0\}.
\]
This maximum exists because $x^{-1}(0)$ is a nonempty closed subset of $[0,1]$, and $t_0<1$ because $x(1)=1/\pi>0$.
By the choice of $t_0$,
\[
x(t)>0\qquad(t_0<t\le1).
\]

Every point of $T$ whose first coordinate is positive lies in $G$: if $(a,b)\in T$ with $a>0$, then continuity of $x\mapsto\sin(1/x)$ near $a$ forces $b=\sin(1/a)$.
Consequently
\[
y(t)=\sin\frac1{x(t)}
\qquad(t_0<t\le1).
\]

Fix a positive integer $m$ with $t_0+1/m\le1$.
Since $x(t_0+1/m)>0$, choose positive numbers $a_m,b_m<x(t_0+1/m)$ such that
\[
\sin\frac1{a_m}=1,
\qquad
\sin\frac1{b_m}=-1.
\]
This is possible with $a_m,b_m$ arbitrarily small, for example by taking reciprocals of sufficiently large numbers of the forms
\[
\frac\pi2+2\pi k,
\qquad
\frac{3\pi}2+2\pi k.
\]
Because $x(t_0)=0$ and $x(t_0+1/m)>a_m,b_m$, the intermediate value theorem gives
\[
r_m,s_m\in(t_0,t_0+1/m)
\]
with $x(r_m)=a_m$ and $x(s_m)=b_m$.
Hence
\[
y(r_m)=1,
\qquad
y(s_m)=-1.
\]
But $r_m,s_m\to t_0$, so continuity of $y$ at $t_0$ would force both sequences $y(r_m)$ and $y(s_m)$ to converge to the same value $y(t_0)$, a contradiction.
Thus no such path exists.
:::

By <2>1 and <2>3, $T$ is connected but not path-connected, disproving the converse in part (d).
