::: {.problem}
Let $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be a nonzero $C^\infty$ function satisfying
\[
f(x)f(y)=f\!\left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\right)
\]
for all $x,y\in\mathbb R$, and suppose
\[
f(x)\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.
\]

1. Prove that $f$ is even and $f(0)=1$.

2. Prove that
   \[
   f'(x)=f''(0)\,x f(x),
   \]
   and find the most general function satisfying the given conditions.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. One has
$$
f(0)\in\{0,1\}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Set $x=y=0$ in the functional equation:
$$
f(0)^2=f(0).
$$
Thus $f(0)$ is an idempotent real number, hence is $0$ or $1$.
:::

<1>2. The case
$$
f(0)=0
$$
would force
$$
f\equiv0.
$$

::: {.proof}
If $f(0)=0$, set $y=0$ in the functional equation. For every real $x$,
$$
0
=
f(x)f(0)
=
f(\abs{x}),
$$
so $f(t)=0$ for every $t\geq0$.

Now for arbitrary $x\in\RR$, set $y=x$. Then
$$
f(x)^2
=
f(\sqrt2\,\abs{x}).
$$
The argument on the right is nonnegative, so the right side is $0$.
Therefore $f(x)=0$ for every $x$, contradicting the assumption that $f$ is
nonzero.
:::

<1>3. One has
$$
\boxed{f(0)=1}
$$
and $f$ is even.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>2 rule out $f(0)=0$, so $f(0)=1$. Setting $y=0$ in the
functional equation now gives
$$
f(x)
=
f(x)f(0)
=
f(\abs{x}).
$$
Hence
$$
f(-x)=f(x)
$$
for every $x$, so $f$ is even.
:::

<1>4. Fix $x>0$ and define
$$
r(y)\coloneqq\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
$$
Then
$$
r'(0)=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
r''(0)=\frac1x.
$$

::: {.proof}
Direct differentiation gives
$$
r'(y)
=
\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},
$$
so $r'(0)=0$. Differentiating once more,
$$
r''(y)
=
\frac{x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}},
$$
and therefore
$$
r''(0)=\frac{x^2}{x^3}=\frac1x.
$$
:::

<1>5. For every $x>0$,
$$
f'(x)=f''(0)\,x f(x).
$$

::: {.proof}
For fixed $x>0$, the functional equation reads
$$
f(x)f(y)=f(r(y)).
$$
Differentiate twice with respect to $y$. At $y=0$, the left side becomes
$$
f(x)f''(0).
$$
For the right side, the chain rule gives
$$
\frac{d^2}{dy^2}f(r(y))
=
f''(r(y))(r'(y))^2
+f'(r(y))r''(y).
$$
Using step <1>4 at $y=0$ gives
$$
f(x)f''(0)
=
\frac{f'(x)}x.
$$
Multiplying by $x$ gives the claim.
:::

<1>6. For every real $x$,
$$
\boxed{
f'(x)=f''(0)\,x f(x)
}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 proves the identity for $x>0$. Since $f$ is even by step <1>3,
$f'$ is odd. Both sides of the displayed identity are therefore odd
functions of $x$, so the identity extends to $x<0$. At $x=0$, evenness
gives $f'(0)=0$, while the right side also vanishes.
:::

<1>7. Set
$$
c\coloneqq f''(0).
$$
Then
$$
f(x)
=
\exp\left(\frac c2x^2\right)
$$
for every real $x$.

::: {.proof}
The differential equation in step <1>6 is
$$
f'=cxf.
$$
The function
$$
F(x)\coloneqq
\exp\left(-\frac c2x^2\right)f(x)
$$
satisfies
$$
F'(x)
=
\exp\left(-\frac c2x^2\right)
\bigl(f'(x)-cxf(x)\bigr)
=0.
$$
Thus $F$ is constant. By step <1>3,
$$
F(0)=f(0)=1,
$$
so $F\equiv1$, which gives the displayed formula.
:::

<1>8. The decay hypothesis is equivalent to
$$
c<0.
$$

::: {.proof}
If $c<0$, then
$$
\exp\left(\frac c2x^2\right)\longrightarrow0
$$
as $\abs{x}\to\infty$. If $c=0$, the function is identically $1$. If
$c>0$, the function tends to $+\infty$. Hence decay occurs exactly when
$c<0$.
:::

<1>9. The most general function satisfying all the hypotheses is
$$
\boxed{
f(x)=e^{-a x^2},
\qquad
a>0
}.
$$

::: {.proof}
By steps <1>7 and <1>8, write
$$
a=-\frac c2>0.
$$
This gives every possible solution. Conversely, if $a>0$, then
$$
f(x)=e^{-ax^2}
$$
is nonzero and smooth, tends to zero at infinity, and satisfies
$$
f(x)f(y)
=
e^{-a(x^2+y^2)}
=
f\left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\right).
$$
:::

<1>10. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>3 proves part 1, step <1>6 proves the requested differential
identity, and step <1>9 gives the complete classification.
