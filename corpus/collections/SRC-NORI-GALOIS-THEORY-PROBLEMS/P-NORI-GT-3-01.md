::: {.problem}
p is an odd prime and $q = p ^ { k }$ . Let E be a splitting field of $X ^ { q } + X \in \mathbb { F } _ { p } [ X ]$ .How many elements does it have?
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
\[
f(X)=X^q+X,
\qquad q=p^k.
\]

<1>1. The polynomial $f$ is separable.

::: {.proof}
In characteristic $p$,
\[
f'(X)=qX^{q-1}+1=1
\]
because $q=p^k=0$ in $\mathbb F_p$. Hence $f$ has no repeated roots.
:::

<1>2. If $\alpha$ is a root of $f$, then
\[
\alpha^q=-\alpha.
\]

::: {.proof}
This is the equation $f(\alpha)=\alpha^q+\alpha=0$.
:::

<1>3. Every root $\alpha$ satisfies
\[
\alpha^{q^2}=\alpha.
\]

::: {.proof}
Raise the identity in step <1>2 to the $q$-th power. Since $q$ is odd because $p$ is odd,
\[
\alpha^{q^2}=(-\alpha)^q=-\alpha^q=\alpha.
\]
:::

<1>4. Therefore all roots of $f$ lie in the finite field $\mathbb F_{q^2}$.

::: {.proof}
Inside an algebraic closure of $\mathbb F_p$, the roots of
\[
X^{q^2}-X
\]
are exactly the elements of the unique field $\mathbb F_{q^2}$. Step <1>3 shows every root of $f$ is among them.
:::

<1>5. The set
\[
V=\{x\in\mathbb F_{q^2}:x^q=-x\}
\]
is a one-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb F_q$ and hence has exactly $q$ elements.

::: {.proof}
The map
\[
T:\mathbb F_{q^2}\to\mathbb F_{q^2},
\qquad T(x)=x^q+x,
\]
is $\mathbb F_q$-linear. Its kernel is $V$. It is not the zero map because for $x\in\mathbb F_q$ one has
\[
T(x)=2x,
\]
and $2\ne0$ since $p$ is odd. The domain is two-dimensional over $\mathbb F_q$, so a nonzero linear map has kernel of dimension at most one. On the other hand $f=X^q+X$ has degree $q$, and by step <1>1 its $q$ roots in a splitting field are distinct; step <1>4 places all of them in $\mathbb F_{q^2}$. Thus $|V|=q$, equivalently $\dim_{\mathbb F_q}V=1$.
:::

<1>6. Choose a nonzero root $\alpha$ of $f$. Then every root is of the form
\[
c\alpha,
\qquad c\in\mathbb F_q.
\]

::: {.proof}
By step <1>5, the roots form the one-dimensional $\mathbb F_q$-vector space $V$. Any nonzero $\alpha\in V$ is therefore a basis of $V$.
:::

<1>7. The splitting field over $\mathbb F_p$ is
\[
E=\mathbb F_q(\alpha).
\]

::: {.proof}
The field generated by all roots contains a nonzero root $\alpha$. It also contains every quotient
\[
\frac{c\alpha}{\alpha}=c
\]
with $c\in\mathbb F_q$, because all $c\alpha$ are roots by step <1>6. Hence it contains $\mathbb F_q$ and equals $\mathbb F_q(\alpha)$. Conversely, step <1>6 shows that $\mathbb F_q(\alpha)$ contains every root.
:::

<1>8. One has
\[
\alpha\notin\mathbb F_q.
\]

::: {.proof}
If $\alpha\in\mathbb F_q$, then $\alpha^q=\alpha$. But step <1>2 gives $\alpha^q=-\alpha$, so $2\alpha=0$. Since $p$ is odd and $\alpha\ne0$, this is impossible.
:::

<1>9. Since $\alpha\in\mathbb F_{q^2}$ but $\alpha\notin\mathbb F_q$, one has
\[
\mathbb F_q(\alpha)=\mathbb F_{q^2}.
\]

::: {.proof}
The extension $\mathbb F_{q^2}/\mathbb F_q$ has prime degree $2$. Hence it has no intermediate fields other than its endpoints. Step <1>8 shows $\mathbb F_q(\alpha)$ is strictly larger than $\mathbb F_q$, so it must be all of $\mathbb F_{q^2}$.
:::

<1>10. Therefore
\[
E=\mathbb F_{q^2}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{|E|=q^2=p^{2k}.}
\]

::: {.proof}
Combine steps <1>7 and <1>9. By definition, $\mathbb F_{q^2}$ has $q^2=p^{2k}$ elements.
:::

<1>11. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>10 gives the requested number of elements.
:::
