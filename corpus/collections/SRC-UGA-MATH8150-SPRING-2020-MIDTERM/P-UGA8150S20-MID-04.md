::: {.problem}
Compute
$$
I(b)=\int_0^{\pi/2}(\tan t)^{ib}\,dt
$$
for $b\in\RR$.

Hint: a simple substitution reduces the integral to one treated in the homework and lectures.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this problem: the collection's provenance is the examination paper alone, so the argument is the card's own. For $b\in\RR$ set
$$
I(b)=\int_0^{\pi/2}(\tan t)^{ib}\,dt,
$$
where for $0<t<\pi/2$ the power is defined by $(\tan t)^{ib}=e^{ib\log(\tan t)}$ with the real logarithm, positive because $\tan t>0$ there.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #converges}
The integrand is continuous on $(0,\pi/2)$ with modulus $1$, so $I(b)$ converges as an improper integral.

::: pf-proof
On $(0,\pi/2)$ the function $\tan$ is positive and continuous, so $\log\tan t$ is continuous and $t\mapsto e^{ib\log\tan t}$ is continuous with $\abs{e^{ib\log\tan t}}=1$ because $b\log\tan t\in\RR$. We split at $\pi/4$: on $(0,\pi/4]$ the integrand extends continuously to any neighbourhood of $0$ since $\tan t\to0$ and the modulus stays $1$; the improper integral of a bounded continuous function over a finite interval converges. On $[\pi/4,\pi/2)$ the same argument applies at $\pi/2$. Hence the integral converges.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #tan}
With $x=\tan t$,
$$
I(b)=\int_0^{\infty}\frac{x^{ib}}{1+x^2}\,dx .
$$

::: pf-proof
$\tan$ maps $(0,\pi/2)$ bijectively onto $(0,\infty)$ with inverse $\arctan$, and $dt=\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$ because $\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x=\frac{1}{1+x^2}$. Also $\tan t=x$ gives $(\tan t)^{ib}=x^{ib}$ on the principal branch, which agrees with $e^{ib\log x}$ for $x>0$. Substituting in the convergent integral of step [](#converges){.pf-ref} gives the display.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #square}
With $u=x^2$ and $a=\dfrac{1+ib}{2}$,
$$
I(b)=\frac12\int_0^{\infty}\frac{u^{a-1}}{1+u}\,du .
$$

::: pf-proof
$x\mapsto u=x^2$ maps $(0,\infty)$ bijectively onto itself with $dx=\frac{du}{2\sqrt u}$ and $x^{ib}=u^{ib/2}$. So the integrand becomes $\frac{u^{ib/2}}{1+u}\cdot\frac{1}{2u^{1/2}}=\frac{u^{ib/2-1/2}}{2(1+u)}$, and $ib/2-1/2=a-1$ with $a=\frac{1+ib}{2}$ by definition of the displayed $a$; its real part is $\operatorname{Re}a=1/2$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #beta}
$\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty}\frac{u^{a-1}}{1+u}\,du=B(a,1-a)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi a)}$ for $0<\operatorname{Re}a<1$.

::: pf-proof
This is Euler's beta integral: substituting $v=u/(1+u)$ turns the integral into $\int_0^1v^{a-1}(1-v)^{-a}dv=B(a,1-a)$, and the beta-gamma relation $B(a,1-a)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)$ together with the reflection formula for $\Gamma$ gives $\pi/\sin(\pi a)$. The hypotheses needed are $\operatorname{Re}a>0$ and $\operatorname{Re}(1-a)>0$, which is $0<\operatorname{Re}a<1$; here $\operatorname{Re}a=1/2$ by step [](#square){.pf-ref}, so it applies.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #trig}
$\sin\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi}{2}+i\dfrac{\pi b}{2}\Bigr)=\cosh\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi b}{2}\Bigr)$.

::: pf-proof
$\sin\bigl(\frac\pi2+z\bigr)=\cos z$ for every complex $z$, and $\cos(iz)=\cosh z$ is the definition of the hyperbolic cosine extended to complex arguments; take $z=i\pi b/2$, so that $iz=-\pi b/2$ and $\cosh(z)=\cos(iz)$ gives the display.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$\displaystyle I(b)=\frac{\pi}{2\cosh(\pi b/2)}$ for every $b\in\RR$.

::: pf-proof
By steps [](#square){.pf-ref} and [](#beta){.pf-ref},
$$
I(b)=\frac12\cdot\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi a)}=\frac{\pi}{2\sin\bigl(\frac{\pi}{2}+i\frac{\pi b}{2}\bigr)}=\frac{\pi}{2\cosh(\pi b/2)},
$$
using $a=\frac{1+ib}{2}$, so $\pi a=\frac{\pi}{2}+i\frac{\pi b}{2}$, and step [](#trig){.pf-ref}. Since $\cosh$ is positive on $\RR$, no sign ambiguity occurs, and the value is real as the integral of a bounded function must be expected to be.
:::
