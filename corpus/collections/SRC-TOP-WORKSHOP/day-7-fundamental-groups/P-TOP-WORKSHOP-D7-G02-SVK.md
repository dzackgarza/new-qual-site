::: {.problem}
State the Seifert–van Kampen Theorem and use it to show that $\pi_1(S^n)=0$ for $n>1$.
Why doesn’t this argument work for $n=1$?
:::

::: {.solution}
A standard form of Seifert--van Kampen says: if \(X=U\cup V\), with \(U,V,U\cap V\) path connected and the basepoint in \(U\cap V\), then
\[
\pi_1(X)\cong
\bigl(\pi_1(U)*\pi_1(V)\bigr)/N,
\]
where \(N\) is the normal subgroup generated by
\[
i_{U*}(\omega)i_{V*}(\omega)^{-1},\qquad \omega\in\pi_1(U\cap V).
\]

For \(S^n\) with \(n>1\), let
\[
U=S^n\setminus\{N\},\qquad V=S^n\setminus\{S\}.
\]
Both \(U\) and \(V\) are homeomorphic to \(\mathbb R^n\), hence simply connected. Their intersection deformation retracts onto \(S^{n-1}\), and for \(n>1\) it is path connected. Van Kampen therefore gives
\[
\pi_1(S^n)=0.
\]
Notice that for \(n=2\) the intersection has fundamental group \(\mathbb Z\), but both maps into the trivial groups \(\pi_1(U)\) and \(\pi_1(V)\) are trivial, so the conclusion is still \(0\).

For \(n=1\), the same cover has
\[
U\cap V=S^1\setminus\{N,S\},
\]
which has two components. Thus the path-connected-intersection hypothesis of this form of van Kampen fails; this is exactly why the argument does not prove \(\pi_1(S^1)=0\).
:::
