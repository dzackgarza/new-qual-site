::: {.problem}
Let
\[
f,g:\mathbb R\longrightarrow\mathbb R,
\qquad
f(x)=-x,
\qquad
g(x)=x+1,
\]
and let
\[
G=\langle f,g\rangle\le\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb R).
\]
Prove carefully that
\[
G\cong\langle a,b\mid b^2=e,\ ba=a^{-1}b\rangle.
\]
This group is the infinite dihedral group $D_\infty$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
\[
P=\langle a,b\mid b^2=e,\ ba=a^{-1}b\rangle.
\]
We use composition of permutations as the group operation.

<1>1. The permutations $g$ and $f$ satisfy the defining relations of $P$.
::: {.proof}
For every $x\in\mathbb R$,
\[
f^2(x)=-(-x)=x,
\]
so $f^2=e$.

Also
\[
(f\circ g)(x)=-(x+1)=-x-1,
\]
while
\[
(g^{-1}\circ f)(x)=g^{-1}(-x)=-x-1.
\]
Hence
\[
f\circ g=g^{-1}\circ f.
\]
Thus the assignment
\[
a\longmapsto g,
\qquad
b\longmapsto f
\]
respects $b^2=e$ and $ba=a^{-1}b$.
:::

<1>2. There is a surjective homomorphism
\[
\Phi:P\longrightarrow G,
\qquad
\Phi(a)=g,
\quad
\Phi(b)=f.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>1, the universal property of the presented group gives a homomorphism $\Phi:P\to\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb R)$ with the displayed values.
Its image contains both $f$ and $g$, and therefore contains
\[
G=\langle f,g\rangle.
\]
Conversely its image is generated by $f$ and $g$, so it is contained in $G$.
Thus $\operatorname{im}\Phi=G$.
:::

<1>3. For every $m\in\mathbb Z$,
\[
ba^m=a^{-m}b
\]
in $P$.
::: {.proof}
For $m\ge0$, this follows by induction from $ba=a^{-1}b$.
The case $m=0$ is trivial.
If $ba^m=a^{-m}b$, then
\[
ba^{m+1}=(ba^m)a=a^{-m}ba=a^{-m}a^{-1}b=a^{-(m+1)}b.
\]

Multiplying $ba=a^{-1}b$ on the right by $b$ gives $bab=a^{-1}$.
Taking inverses yields $ba^{-1}b=a$, and multiplying on the right by $b$ gives
\[
ba^{-1}=ab.
\]
Applying the same induction to $a^{-1}$ gives the formula for negative $m$.
:::

<1>4. Every element of $P$ has a representative of one of the two forms
\[
a^m
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
a^m b,
\qquad m\in\mathbb Z.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $b^2=e$, every occurrence of $b^{-1}$ may be replaced by $b$.
Use <1>3 repeatedly to move each occurrence of $b$ to the right past every power of $a$.
Adjacent pairs $bb$ then cancel.
Thus at most one $b$ remains, at the right end of the word, leaving one of the displayed normal forms.
:::

<1>5. The images of these normal forms under $\Phi$ are
\[
\Phi(a^m)(x)=x+m,
\qquad
\Phi(a^m b)(x)=-x+m.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $\Phi(a)=g$ and $g^m(x)=x+m$ for every $m\in\mathbb Z$,
\[
\Phi(a^m)(x)=x+m.
\]
Also
\[
\Phi(a^m b)=g^m\circ f,
\]
so
\[
\Phi(a^m b)(x)=g^m(-x)=-x+m.
\]
:::

<1>6. Distinct normal forms in <1>4 have distinct images under $\Phi$.
::: {.proof}
If $\Phi(a^m)=\Phi(a^n)$, evaluation at $0$ gives $m=n$.
Likewise,
\[
\Phi(a^m b)(0)=m,
\qquad
\Phi(a^n b)(0)=n,
\]
so equality again implies $m=n$.

Finally, a translation cannot equal a reflected translation.
Indeed, if
\[
\Phi(a^m)=\Phi(a^n b),
\]
then evaluation at $0$ gives $m=n$, but evaluation at $1$ would give
\[
1+m=-1+m,
\]
which is impossible.
:::

<1>7. The homomorphism $\Phi$ is an isomorphism, so
\[
G\cong P.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>4, every element of $P$ has a normal-form representative, and by <1>6 distinct normal forms have distinct images.
Hence $\Phi$ is injective.
It is surjective by <1>2, so it is an isomorphism.
:::
