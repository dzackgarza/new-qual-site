::: {.problem}
Show that the nilpotent elements of a commutative ring form a subring.
:::

::: {.solution}
The collection retains its solution outline as `assets/attachments/Ch13Sltns.pdf`, and entry "# 16" of that outline is this exercise; the seventh edition prints it as Chapter 13 Exercise 14, and the card keeps the outline's number. The two closure computations below are the outline's: a product of nilpotents is nilpotent through $(ab)^{nm}=0$, and a difference through the binomial expansion of $(a-b)^{n+m}$. What the card adds is the subring test itself, which the outline applies without stating, and the hypotheses each computation uses: both need the ring to be commutative, and the second needs the binomial theorem in a commutative ring.

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $N=\theset{x\in R \st x^{k}=0\text{ for some integer }k\ge1}$ its set of nilpotent elements.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #criterion}
A nonempty subset $S\subseteq R$ that is closed under subtraction and under multiplication is a subring of $R$.

::: pf-proof
Closure under subtraction makes $(S,+)$ a subgroup of $(R,+)$: for $a\in S$ one has $0=a-a\in S$, then $-a=0-a\in S$, and then $a+b=a-(-b)\in S$ for $a,b\in S$, which is the subgroup criterion. So $(S,+)$ is an abelian group, being a subgroup of the abelian group $(R,+)$. Closure under multiplication makes the restriction of the product a map $S\times S\to S$. Associativity of addition and of multiplication, and both distributive laws, hold in $S$ because they hold in $R$ and $S$ is closed under the operations. Hence $S$ is a ring under the restrictions of the operations of $R$, that is, a subring. Nothing here requires $S$ to contain a unity of $R$: a subring in this sense is a ring under the inherited operations, whether or not $R$ has a $1$ and whether or not $1$ lies in $S$, which matters below because $1$ is nilpotent only when $1=0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #zero}
$0\in N$, so $N\ne\varnothing$.

::: pf-proof
$0^{1}=0$, so $0$ is nilpotent with exponent $1$. More generally $0^{k}=0$ for every $k\ge1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #product}
If $a,b\in N$ then $ab\in N$.

::: pf-proof
Choose integers $n,m\ge1$ with $a^{n}=0$ and $b^{m}=0$. Commutativity is used twice: $(ab)^{nm}=a^{nm}b^{nm}$, which holds because $ab=ba$, and $a^{nm}b^{nm}=(a^{n})^{m}(b^{m})^{n}$, which holds because integral powers of a single element compose. Therefore $(ab)^{nm}=(a^{n})^{m}(b^{m})^{n}=0\cdot0=0$, so $ab$ is nilpotent. With $l=\operatorname{lcm}(n,m)$ the same conclusion holds in fewer steps, since $l\ge n$ gives $a^{l}=a^{n}a^{l-n}=0$ and likewise $b^{l}=0$, so $(ab)^{l}=a^{l}b^{l}=0$; $nm$ is used in the step because the arithmetic is then immediate.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #difference}
If $a,b\in N$ then $a-b\in N$.

::: pf-proof
Keep $n,m\ge1$ with $a^{n}=0$ and $b^{m}=0$. Since $R$ is commutative, $a$ and $-b$ commute, so the binomial theorem applies in $R$ and
$$
(a-b)^{n+m}=\sum_{k=0}^{n+m}\binom{n+m}{k}a^{k}(-b)^{n+m-k}.
$$
Every summand vanishes. If $k\ge n$ then $a^{k}=a^{n}a^{k-n}=0$. If $k<n$ then $n+m-k\ge m+1>m$, so $b^{n+m-k}=b^{m}b^{n-k}=0$ and hence $(-b)^{n+m-k}=(-1)^{n+m-k}b^{n+m-k}=0$. The two cases cover all $k$ from $0$ to $n+m$, so $(a-b)^{n+m}=0$ and $a-b$ is nilpotent. It is here that commutativity of $R$ is needed a second time: without it $(a-b)^{n+m}$ need not expand as displayed, and a difference of nilpotents in a noncommutative ring need not be nilpotent — in the ring of $2\times2$ matrices over a field, $a=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $b=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ both square to $0$, while $a-b=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has square the identity and is therefore not nilpotent.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #subring}
$N$ is a subring of $R$.

::: pf-proof
$N$ is a subset of $R$ that is nonempty by step [](#zero){.pf-ref}, closed under multiplication by step [](#product){.pf-ref}, and closed under subtraction by step [](#difference){.pf-ref}; by the subring test of step [](#criterion){.pf-ref} it is a subring of $R$. The unity of $R$, if there is one, is nilpotent only in the trivial case: $1\in N$ means $1=1^{k}=0$ for some $k\ge1$, and conversely $1=0$ gives $1=0^{1}\in N$, so for a ring with $1\ne0$ the subring $N$ contains no unity of $R$. The commutativity hypothesis on $R$ is not removable from either closure step.
:::
