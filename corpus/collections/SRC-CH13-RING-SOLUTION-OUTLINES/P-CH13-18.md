::: {.problem}
A ring element is called an idempotent if $a^{2} = a$. Prove that the only idempotents in an integral domain are $0$ and $1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The collection retains its solution outline as `assets/attachments/Ch13Sltns.pdf`, and entry "# 18" of that outline is this exercise; the seventh edition prints it as Chapter 13 Exercise 16, and the card keeps the outline's number. The factorisation $a^{2}-a=a(a-1)$ and the dichotomy that follows from it are the outline's. What the card adds is that $0$ and $1$ are themselves idempotents and are distinct, without which the word "only" is not answered, and the account of which hypotheses of an integral domain each step uses.

Let $D$ be an integral domain: a commutative ring with unity $1\ne0$ in which $xy=0$ implies $x=0$ or $y=0$. Let $a\in D$ be idempotent, that is, $a^{2}=a$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #factor}
$a(a-1)=0$.

::: pf-proof
By distributivity and the unity axiom, $a(a-1)=a\cdot a-a\cdot1=a^{2}-a$. The hypothesis $a^{2}=a$ makes the right-hand side $a-a=0$. This step uses the existence of $1$ in $D$, since $a-1$ is formed with it, and uses no other property of $D$; in particular commutativity is not used, the factorisation being a one-sided distributive identity.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #dichotomy}
$a=0$ or $a=1$.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#factor){.pf-ref} exhibits a product of two elements of $D$ that is $0$, so by the defining property of an integral domain one factor vanishes: $a=0$, or $a-1=0$, and in the second case $a=1$, adding $1$ to both sides in the additive group. This is the only place the absence of zero-divisors is used. Without it the conclusion fails: in $\ZZ_{6}$ one has $3^{2}=9=3$, so $3$ is idempotent, and $3\ne0,1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #exactly}
The set of idempotents of $D$ is exactly $\theset{0,1}$.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#dichotomy){.pf-ref} shows that every idempotent of $D$ is $0$ or $1$. Conversely $0^{2}=0\cdot0=0$, and $1^{2}=1\cdot1=1$ because $1$ is the unity, so $0$ and $1$ are idempotents. They are distinct, $0\ne1$, which is part of the definition of an integral domain. Hence the idempotents of $D$ form the two-element set $\theset{0,1}$, which is the assertion: no idempotent other than these two exists, and these two do occur.
:::
