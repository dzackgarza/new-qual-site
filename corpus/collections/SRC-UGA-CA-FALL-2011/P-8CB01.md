::: {.problem}
Consider $f(x,y)=x^2-2y+y^3$.
Let $P=(1,1)$.

(a) Let $X=\{(x,y)\in\RR^2:f(x,y)=0\}$.
Prove that there is a neighborhood $U$ of $P$ so that $U\cap X$ is given by $y=\phi(x)$ for some $\mathcal{C}^1$ function $\phi$.
Give $\phi'(x)$.

(b) Now consider the same equation in $\CC^2$ (i.e., $Y=\{(x,y)\in\CC^2:f(x,y)=0\}$). Prove that the analogous statement holds.
What is $\phi'(x)$ as an $\RR$-linear map from $\CC$ to $\CC$?
Why is $\phi$ holomorphic?
(Hint: If you consider $g:\CC\to\CC$ as a map $\widetilde{g}:\RR^2\to\RR^2$, how is holomorphicity of $g$ characterized by $D\widetilde{g}$?)
:::

::: {.solution}
The collection's provenance is the examination paper, so no retained solution set covers this problem and the argument is the card's own. Write $f(x,y)=x^2-2y+y^3$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #hypotheses}
$f(1,1)=0$ and $f_y(1,1)=1\ne0$.

::: pf-proof
$f(1,1)=1-2+1=0$. The partial derivative is $f_y(x,y)=-2+3y^2$, so $f_y(1,1)=-2+3=1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #part-a}
(a) There is a neighbourhood $U$ of $P=(1,1)$ with $U\cap X$ the graph of a $\mathcal{C}^1$ function $\phi$ of $x$, and $\phi'(x)=-\dfrac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}$.

::: pf-proof
$f$ is $\mathcal{C}^1$ and $f_y(1,1)=1\ne0$ by step [](#hypotheses){.pf-ref}, so the implicit function theorem gives neighbourhoods of $1$ in $\RR$ and of $1$ in $\RR$ and a unique $\mathcal{C}^1$ function $\phi$ on the first with $\phi(1)=1$ and $f(x,\phi(x))=0$; taking $U$ the product of the two neighbourhoods gives $U\cap X=\{(x,\phi(x))\}$. Differentiating $f(x,\phi(x))=0$ in $x$ gives $2x+(-2+3\phi(x)^2)\phi'(x)=0$, and the coefficient $-2+3\phi(x)^2$ is nonzero near $x=1$, since it equals $1$ there; solving gives the displayed derivative.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #real-derivative}
(b) Regarded as a map $\RR^2\to\RR$, the derivative of $f$ in the $y$-variable at $(1,1)$ is multiplication by $1$, hence an invertible real-linear map.

::: pf-proof
The partial derivative $\partial f/\partial y$ at a point is by definition the real-linear map $h\mapsto f_y(\text{point})\,h$ on $\RR$; at $(1,1)$ the scalar is $1$ by step [](#hypotheses){.pf-ref}, and multiplication by $1$ is invertible.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #part-b}
(b) The analogous statement holds over $\CC$: there is a neighbourhood of $1$ in $\CC$ and a $\mathcal{C}^1$ map $\phi$ with $f(x,\phi(x))=0$ there, whose derivative is $D\phi(x)(h)=-\dfrac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}h$; this is multiplication by a complex scalar, so $\phi$ is holomorphic.

:::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #real-ift}
The real implicit function theorem applies to $f\colon\RR^4\to\RR^2$ at $(1,1)$ in the second variable.

::: pf-proof
Reading $\CC^2$ as $\RR^4$ and $f$ as the pair of real functions $(\operatorname{Re}f,\operatorname{Im}f)$, the map $f$ is $\mathcal{C}^1$; its derivative in the second variable at $(1,1)$ is multiplication by the complex number $f_y(1,1)=1$, which is an invertible real-linear map of $\RR^2$ by step [](#real-derivative){.pf-ref}. The theorem gives neighbourhoods in $\RR^2$ and a $\mathcal{C}^1$ map, which is the required $\phi$ from the corresponding neighbourhood of $1$ in $\CC$ to $\CC$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #complex-linear}
$D\phi(x)$ is multiplication by the complex scalar $-\frac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}$, hence $\CC$-linear.

::: pf-proof
Differentiating the identity $f(x,\phi(x))=0$ at a point of the neighbourhood and applying the chain rule to the real and imaginary parts gives, for each direction $h\in\CC$, $2xh+(-2+3\phi(x)^2)D\phi(x)(h)=0$, so $D\phi(x)(h)=c(x)h$ with $c(x)=-\frac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}$, a complex number independent of $h$; a real-linear map of this form is multiplication by a complex scalar and therefore complex-linear.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
(b) $\phi$ is holomorphic, with complex derivative $\phi'(x)=-\dfrac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}$.

::: pf-proof
A $\mathcal{C}^1$ map $g\colon\CC\to\CC$ is holomorphic exactly when its real derivative $Dg(x)$ is complex-linear at every point, the complex derivative then being the scalar of multiplication; this is the characterization the hint points at, the real derivative being the map $\widetilde g$ carries to $\RR^2$ with the Cauchy--Riemann condition as complex-linearity. $\phi$ is $\mathcal{C}^1$ and step [](#complex-linear){.pf-ref} shows $D\phi(x)$ is complex-linear at every point of its domain, so $\phi$ is holomorphic and its derivative is the displayed scalar, which is part (a)'s formula read over $\CC$.
:::
