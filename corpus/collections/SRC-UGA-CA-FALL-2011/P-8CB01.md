::: {.problem}
Consider $f(x,y)=x^2-2y+y^3$.
Let $P=(1,1)$.

(a) Let $X=\{(x,y)\in\RR^2:f(x,y)=0\}$.
Prove that there is a neighborhood $U$ of $P$ so that $U\cap X$ is given by $y=\phi(x)$ for some $\mathcal{C}^1$ function $\phi$.
Give $\phi'(x)$.

(b) Now consider the same equation in $\CC^2$ (i.e., $Y=\{(x,y)\in\CC^2:f(x,y)=0\}$). Prove that the analogous statement holds.
What is $\phi'(x)$ as an $\RR$-linear map from $\CC$ to $\CC$?
Why is $\phi$ holomorphic?
(Hint: If you consider $g:\CC\to\CC$ as a map $\widetilde{g}:\RR^2\to\RR^2$, how is holomorphicity of $g$ characterized by $D\widetilde{g}$?)
:::

::: {.solution}
We have $f(1,1)=0$ and
$$
f_y(x,y)=-2+3y^2,
\qquad f_y(1,1)=1\ne0.
$$
Thus the real implicit-function theorem gives neighborhoods of $1$ and a
unique $C^1$ function $y=\phi(x)$ with $\phi(1)=1$ and
$f(x,\phi(x))=0$. Differentiating,
$$
2x+(-2+3\phi(x)^2)\phi'(x)=0,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\phi'(x)=-\frac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}}.
$$

For (b), regard $f:\CC^2\to\CC$ as a smooth map
$\RR^4\to\RR^2$. Its derivative in the $y$ variable at $(1,1)$
is multiplication by $f_y(1,1)=1$, hence is an invertible real-linear map.
The real implicit-function theorem therefore gives a $C^1$ map
$\phi$ from a neighborhood of $1$ in $\CC$ to $\CC$ with the
same graph property. Differentiating in a direction $h\in\CC$ gives
$$
D\phi(x)(h)=-\frac{2x}{-2+3\phi(x)^2}\,h.
$$
Thus $D\phi(x)$ is multiplication by a complex scalar, so it is
$\CC$-linear at every point. A $C^1$ map $\CC\to\CC$ is
holomorphic exactly when its real derivative is complex-linear. Hence
$\phi$ is holomorphic, with complex derivative given by the same displayed
scalar.
:::
