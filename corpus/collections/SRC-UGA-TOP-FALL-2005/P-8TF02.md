::: problem
Prove or give a counterexample: If $q:Y\to Z$ is a quotient map and $Y$ is both compact and Hausdorff, then $Z$ must be Hausdorff.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The assertion is false.
::: {.proof}
We construct a quotient of the compact Hausdorff space $Y=[0,1]$ that is not Hausdorff.
:::

<1>2. Let $Z=\{0,1\}$ with topology
\[
\mathcal T_Z=\{\varnothing,\{1\},Z\},
\]
and define
\[
q:[0,1]\longrightarrow Z,
\qquad
q(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x=0,\\
1,&x>0.
\end{cases}
\]
Then $q$ is a quotient map.
::: {.proof}
The map $q$ is surjective.
For the four subsets of $Z$ one has
\[
q^{-1}(\varnothing)=\varnothing,
\qquad
q^{-1}(Z)=[0,1],
\]
\[
q^{-1}(\{1\})=(0,1],
\qquad
q^{-1}(\{0\})=\{0\}.
\]
In the subspace topology on $[0,1]$, the set $(0,1]$ is open, while $\{0\}$ is not open.
Consequently a subset $U\subseteq Z$ is open exactly when $q^{-1}(U)$ is open in $[0,1]$.
This is precisely the quotient-map criterion for the surjection $q$.
:::

<1>3. The space $Y=[0,1]$ is compact and Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
The interval $[0,1]$ is compact by the Heine--Borel theorem, and it is Hausdorff as a subspace of the metric space $\RR$.
:::

<1>4. The quotient space $Z$ is not Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
The only open neighborhood of $0\in Z$ is $Z$ itself.
Hence every open neighborhood of $0$ meets every open neighborhood of $1$, so the two distinct points $0$ and $1$ cannot be separated by disjoint open sets.
Therefore $Z$ is not Hausdorff.
:::

<1>5. Thus compactness and Hausdorffness of the domain do not force a quotient space to be Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
Steps <1>2--<1>4 give a quotient map from a compact Hausdorff space onto a non-Hausdorff space, which is the required counterexample.
:::
:::
