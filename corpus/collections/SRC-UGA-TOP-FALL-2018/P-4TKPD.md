::: problem
Prove that a finite CW complex must be Hausdorff.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $K$ is compact Hausdorff and $R\subseteq K\times K$ is a closed equivalence relation, then the quotient $K/R$ is compact Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
q:K\longrightarrow K/R
\]
be the quotient map.
Compactness is immediate because $q$ is continuous and surjective.

First observe that $q$ is a closed map.
If $C\subseteq K$ is closed, then $C$ is compact.
Its saturation is
\[
q^{-1}(q(C))
=R[C]
=\{y\in K:\text{ there exists }x\in C\text{ with }(x,y)\in R\}.
\]
The set
\[
R\cap(C\times K)
\]
is compact, so its projection to the second factor is compact and therefore closed in the Hausdorff space $K$.
Thus $q^{-1}(q(C))$ is closed.
By the definition of the quotient topology, $q(C)$ is closed in $K/R$.

Now let $A$ and $B$ be two distinct $R$-equivalence classes.
They are closed in $K$ because, for any $a\in A$,
\[
A=\{y\in K:(a,y)\in R\}
\]
is a section of the closed set $R$.
Hence $A$ and $B$ are compact.
Since
\[
A\times B\subseteq(K\times K)\setminus R,
\]
compactness gives open sets $U,V\subseteq K$ with
\[
A\subseteq U,
\qquad
B\subseteq V,
\qquad
(U\times V)\cap R=\varnothing.
\]
For completeness, obtain such $U,V$ by first choosing a product neighborhood avoiding $R$ around each $(a,b)\in A\times B$, then applying compactness first in the $B$ variable and then in the $A$ variable.

Because $q$ is closed, the saturations of closed sets are closed.
Therefore
\[
U'=K\setminus q^{-1}(q(K\setminus U)),
\qquad
V'=K\setminus q^{-1}(q(K\setminus V))
\]
are open saturated sets with
\[
A\subseteq U'\subseteq U,
\qquad
B\subseteq V'\subseteq V.
\]
They are disjoint: if one equivalence class met both, there would be $u\in U'$ and $v\in V'$ with $(u,v)\in R$, contradicting $(U\times V)\cap R=\varnothing$.
Since $U'$ and $V'$ are saturated, $q(U')$ and $q(V')$ are disjoint open neighborhoods of the two quotient points.
Thus $K/R$ is Hausdorff.
:::

<1>2. If $Y$ is compact Hausdorff and
\[
f:S^{m-1}\longrightarrow Y
\]
is continuous, then the adjunction space
\[
Y\cup_fD^m
\]
is compact Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
Set
\[
K=Y\amalg D^m.
\]
This is compact Hausdorff.
The adjunction space is the quotient of $K$ by the equivalence relation generated by
\[
u\sim f(u)
\qquad(u\in S^{m-1}=\partial D^m).
\]
Explicitly, besides diagonal pairs, the relation contains
\[
(u,f(u)),\qquad(f(u),u)
\]
and pairs
\[
(u,v)\in S^{m-1}\times S^{m-1}
\quad\text{with}\quad
f(u)=f(v).
\]
Each of these pieces is closed.
Indeed, $S^{m-1}$ is closed in $D^m$; the graph of $f$ is closed because its domain is compact and $Y$ is Hausdorff; and
\[
\{(u,v):f(u)=f(v)\}
=(f\times f)^{-1}(\Delta_Y)
\]
is closed because the diagonal $\Delta_Y$ is closed in $Y\times Y$.
Hence the whole equivalence relation is closed in $K\times K$.
By <1>1, the quotient $Y\cup_fD^m$ is compact Hausdorff.
:::

<1>3. Every finite CW complex is compact Hausdorff, and hence Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
A finite CW complex has finitely many cells, so order its cells compatibly with dimension and attach them one at a time.
The union of the $0$-cells is a finite discrete space, hence compact Hausdorff.

Assume the space obtained after finitely many preceding attachments is compact Hausdorff.
The next cell is attached by a continuous map
\[
f:S^{m-1}\longrightarrow Y
\]
into that space $Y$.
By <1>2, attaching the cell produces another compact Hausdorff space.
Induction over the finite list of cells therefore shows that the whole CW complex is compact Hausdorff.
In particular, it is Hausdorff.
:::
