::: {.problem}
Let $G = \GL(3, \QQ[x])$ be the group of invertible $3\times 3$ matrices over $\QQ[x]$.
For each $f\in \QQ[x]$, let $S_f$ be the set of $3\times 3$ matrices $A$ over $\QQ[x]$ such that $\det(A) = c f(x)$ for some nonzero constant $c\in \QQ$.

a. Show that for $(P, Q) \in G\cross G$ and $A\in S_f$, the formula
$$
(P, Q)\cdot A \coloneqq PA\inverseof{Q}
$$
gives a well defined map $G\cross G \cross S_f \to S_f$ and show that this map gives a group action of $G\cross G$ on $S_f$.

b. For $f(x) = x^3(x^2+1)^2$, give one representative from each orbit of the group action in (a), and justify your assertion.
:::

::: {.solution}
This is a problem of the University of Georgia algebra qualifying examination of Spring 2014 (`assets/attachments/Algebra_Spring_2014.docx`). No published solution set is transcribed: the argument is the card's own, and part (b) is the Smith normal form classification of matrices over a PID.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #welldefined}
(a) For $(P,Q)\in G\times G$ and $A\in S_{f}$ one has $PAQ^{-1}\in S_{f}$, so the formula defines a map $G\times G\times S_{f}\to S_{f}$.

::: pf-proof
Let $R=\QQ[x]$. Its units are exactly the nonzero rational constants: a polynomial $u$ with a polynomial inverse has $\deg u+\deg u^{-1}=0$, so both degrees vanish. Hence $\det P$ and $\det Q$ are units of $R$ for $P,Q\in G=\GL(3,R)$, that is, nonzero rationals. By multiplicativity of the determinant,
$$\det(PAQ^{-1})=\det P\cdot\det A\cdot(\det Q)^{-1},$$
and if $\det A=cf(x)$ with $c\in\QQ^{\times}$ then this is $c\,\det P\,(\det Q)^{-1}f(x)$, again a nonzero rational constant times $f$. So $PAQ^{-1}\in S_{f}$, and the formula is independent of any choice: it is defined by matrix multiplication and inversion in $G$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #action}
(a) The map is an action of $G\times G$ on $S_{f}$.

::: pf-proof
The identity pair acts trivially: $(I,I)\cdot A=IAI^{-1}=A$. And for two pairs,
$$(P_{1},Q_{1})\cdot\big((P_{2},Q_{2})\cdot A\big)=P_{1}(P_{2}AQ_{2}^{-1})Q_{1}^{-1}=(P_{1}P_{2})A(Q_{1}Q_{2})^{-1}=\big((P_{1},Q_{1})(P_{2},Q_{2})\big)\cdot A,$$
using $(Q_{1}Q_{2})^{-1}=Q_{2}^{-1}Q_{1}^{-1}$ and the definition of the product in $G\times G$. Together with step [](#welldefined){.pf-ref} this is an action.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #snf}
(b) Two matrices of $S_{f}$ lie in the same orbit if and only if they have the same Smith normal form; consequently the orbits are in bijection with the triples $(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})$ of monic polynomials with $d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid d_{3}$ and $d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}=f$.

::: pf-proof
$R=\QQ[x]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a PID, and the Smith normal form theorem says that every matrix over a PID is equivalent, by left and right multiplication by invertible matrices of the same size, to a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})$ with $d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid d_{3}$, and that the $d_{i}$ are unique up to multiplication by units. Left and right multiplication by elements of $G=\GL(3,R)$ is exactly the equivalence relation defining the orbits. Normalizing each $d_{i}$ to be monic fixes the unit ambiguity, and then the product $d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}$ is monic and equals $\det A$ up to a unit; since every $A\in S_{f}$ has $\det A=cf$ with $c$ a nonzero rational and $f$ is monic, the monic normalization forces $d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}=f$. Conversely $\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})$ itself lies in $S_{f}$ when the product is $f$, so every such triple occurs.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #count}
For $f(x)=x^{3}(x^{2}+1)^{2}$ there are exactly six such triples.

::: pf-proof
The polynomials $x$ and $q=x^{2}+1$ are monic irreducibles of $R$ and are coprime, so a monic divisor triple with $d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid d_{3}$ and $d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}=x^{3}q^{2}$ is determined by a nondecreasing triple of nonnegative exponents $(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})$ for $x$ with $\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=3$ and a nondecreasing triple $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})$ for $q$ with $\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=2$, the divisibility being exactly the coordinatewise order. The triples summing to $3$ are $(0,0,3)$, $(0,1,2)$, $(1,1,1)$, and those summing to $2$ are $(0,0,2)$, $(0,1,1)$; there are no others, since a nondecreasing triple of nonnegative integers summing to $s$ is a partition of $s$ into at most three parts. Hence there are $3\cdot2=6$ triples and, by step [](#snf){.pf-ref}, six orbits.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #representatives}
(b) The six matrices $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,x^{3}q^{2})$, $\operatorname{diag}(1,q,x^{3}q)$, $\operatorname{diag}(1,x,x^{2}q^{2})$, $\operatorname{diag}(1,xq,x^{2}q)$, $\operatorname{diag}(x,x,xq^{2})$, $\operatorname{diag}(x,xq,xq)$, with $q=x^{2}+1$, represent the six orbits.

::: pf-proof
Each is diagonal with monic entries satisfying $d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid d_{3}$ and product $x^{3}q^{2}$: for instance $\operatorname{diag}(1,xq,x^{2}q)$ has product $x^{3}q^{2}$ and $1\mid xq\mid x^{2}q$, and $\operatorname{diag}(x,xq,xq)$ has product $x^{3}q^{2}$ and $x\mid xq\mid xq$. Each therefore lies in $S_{f}$ with $c=1$. They are pairwise distinct, and by step [](#count){.pf-ref} there are exactly six orbits, so each of the six is represented exactly once and no two listed matrices are in the same orbit.
:::
