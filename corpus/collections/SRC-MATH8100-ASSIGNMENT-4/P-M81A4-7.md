::: {.problem}
(a) If $f \in L^1(\RR)$ and $x \mapsto x f(x) \in L^1(\RR)$ are Lebesgue integrable on $\RR$, prove that the function:
$$F(t) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \cos(tx) \, dx$$
is differentiable on $\RR$ and find $F'(t)$.

(b) Evaluate the limit:
$$\lim_{t \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \, dx$$
with full justification.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #part-a}
$F$ is differentiable on $\RR$, with
$$F'(t) = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) \sin(tx) \, dx.$$

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Let $t \in \RR$ and let $h_n \to 0$ ($h_n \ne 0$) be any sequence converging to 0.

::: pf-proof
Differentiability of $F$ at $t$ is a statement about the limit of the difference quotients, and a limit exists exactly when every sequence $h_n \to 0$ with $h_n \ne 0$ produces the same limit of the quotients; so it suffices to fix an arbitrary such sequence and compute.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Form the difference quotient:
$$\frac{F(t + h_n) - F(t)}{h_n} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \left( \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} \right) dx.$$

::: pf-proof
The integral is linear and both integrands $\cos((t+h_n)x)f(x)$ and $\cos(tx)f(x)$ are integrable, being $\abs{f}$ times a bounded factor, so the difference of the two integrals is the integral of the difference; dividing by the nonzero real number $h_n$ then brings $1/h_n$ inside the integral, again by linearity.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #pointwise-limit}
For almost every $x \in \RR$, the pointwise limit of the integrand as $n \to \infty$ is:
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x) \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} = f(x) \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \cos(tx) = -x f(x) \sin(tx).$$

::: pf-proof
For fixed $x$ the map $t \mapsto \cos(tx)$ is differentiable with derivative $-x\sin(tx)$, so its difference quotient at $t$ tends to $-x\sin(tx)$ as $h_n \to 0$; multiplying by the number $f(x)$ gives the limit of the integrand, and this holds at every $x$, hence almost every $x$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By the **Mean Value Theorem**, for each $x$ and $h_n$, there exists $\theta_n$ between $t$ and $t + h_n$ such that:
$$\abs{ \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} } = \abs{-x \sin(\theta_n x)} \le \abs{x} \abs{\sin(\theta_n x)} \le \abs{x}.$$

::: pf-proof
The mean value theorem applied to $u \mapsto \cos(ux)$ on the interval with endpoints $t$ and $t+h_n$ gives a point $\theta_n$ between them with $\cos((t+h_n)x) - \cos(tx) = h_n\left(-x\sin(\theta_n x)\right)$; dividing by $h_n \ne 0$ and taking absolute values gives the first equality, and $\abs{\sin} \le 1$ gives the bound $\abs{x}$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #dominating-function}
Therefore, the integrand is dominated by:
$$\abs{ f(x) \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} } \le \abs{x f(x)} \eqqcolon g(x).$$

::: pf-proof
Multiplying the bound of the mean value theorem step by the fixed number $\abs{f(x)}$ bounds the modulus of the difference quotient integrand by $\abs{f(x)}\abs{x}$, which is $g(x)$, and the bound is independent of $n$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By hypothesis, $g(x) = \abs{x f(x)} \in L^1(\RR)$.

::: pf-proof
The hypothesis is that $x \mapsto xf(x)$ is Lebesgue integrable on $\RR$, and $g$ is the modulus of that function; the modulus of an integrable function is integrable, with finite integral, so $g \in L^1(\RR)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By the **Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem (LDCT)**:
$$F'(t) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{F(t + h_n) - F(t)}{h_n} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \lim_{n \to \infty} f(x) \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} \, dx = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) \sin(tx) \, dx.$$

::: pf-proof
The difference quotient integrands are measurable, since $f$ is measurable and the cosine factor is continuous, and they converge pointwise almost everywhere to $-xf(x)\sin(tx)$ by step [](#pointwise-limit){.pf-ref}; they are dominated in modulus by the integrable function $g$ of step [](#dominating-function){.pf-ref}. The dominated convergence theorem then gives that their integrals converge to the integral of the limit, which is the displayed identity, and by the previous steps that limit of quotients is $F'(t)$.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #part-b}
$$\lim_{t \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \, dx = \frac{2}{3}.$$

::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #pointwise-limit-b}
For each fixed $x \in (0, 1]$, the pointwise limit as $t \to 0$ is:
$$\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} = \evaluated{ \frac{d}{dt} e^{t\sqrt{x}} }_{t=0} = \sqrt{x}.$$

::: pf-proof
The expression is the difference quotient at $t = 0$ of the differentiable function $t \mapsto e^{t\sqrt x}$, whose derivative is $\sqrt x\,e^{t\sqrt x}$ and whose value at $0$ is $\sqrt x$; at $x = 0$ the integrand is $0$ for every $t \ne 0$, so the limit is $0 = \sqrt{0}$ there as well.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #domination-b}
We dominate the integrand for all $t \in [-1, 1] \setminus \{0\}$:
- By the Mean Value Theorem applied to $u \mapsto e^u$, there exists $\xi$ between $0$ and $t\sqrt{x}$ such that:
  $$\abs{ \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} } = \abs{ \sqrt{x} e^\xi } \le \sqrt{x} e^{\abs{t}\sqrt{x}} \le 1 \cdot e^{1 \cdot 1} = e.$$

::: pf-proof
The mean value theorem applied to $u \mapsto e^u$ on the interval with endpoints $0$ and $t\sqrt x$ gives $\xi$ between them with $e^{t\sqrt x} - 1 = t\sqrt x\,e^\xi$; dividing by $t \ne 0$ leaves $\sqrt x\,e^\xi$. Since $\abs{\xi} \le \abs{t}\sqrt x \le 1$ for $\abs{t} \le 1$ and $x \in [0,1]$, one has $e^\xi \le e$, and $\sqrt x \le 1$, so the modulus is at most $e$; the bound does not depend on $t$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The constant function $g(x) = e$ is integrable on the finite measure space $[0, 1]$ ($\int_0^1 e \, dx = e < \infty$).

::: pf-proof
The interval $[0,1]$ has finite Lebesgue measure, and a bounded measurable function on a set of finite measure is integrable; the integral of the constant $e$ is $e$ times the measure, which is $e < \infty$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By the **Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem**:
$$\lim_{t \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \, dx = \int_0^1 \lim_{t \to 0} \left( \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \right) dx = \int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx.$$

::: pf-proof
The integrands are measurable and converge pointwise to $\sqrt x$ for every $x \in (0,1]$ by step [](#pointwise-limit-b){.pf-ref}, and for every $t \in [-1,1]\setminus\{0\}$ they are dominated in modulus by the integrable constant $e$ of step [](#domination-b){.pf-ref}; the dominated convergence theorem gives the displayed identity.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
We compute the integral:
$$\int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx = \left[ \frac{2}{3} x^{3/2} \right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3}.$$

::: pf-proof
The derivative of $\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}$ is $x^{1/2}$ for $x > 0$, so the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $\int_0^1 \sqrt x\,dx = \frac{2}{3}\cdot 1^{3/2} - \frac{2}{3}\cdot 0^{3/2} = \frac{2}{3}$.
:::
