::: problem
1. If $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $x \mapsto x f(x) \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ are Lebesgue integrable on $\mathbb{R}$, prove that the function:
$$F(t) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \cos(tx) \, dx$$
is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and find $F'(t)$.

2. Evaluate the limit:
$$\lim_{t \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \, dx$$
with full justification.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Prove differentiability of parametric integrals via the Dominated Convergence Theorem and evaluate the integral limit via monotone/dominated convergence.

<1>1. Part 1: Differentiability of $F(t) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \cos(tx) \, dx$:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $t \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $h_n \to 0$ ($h_n \ne 0$) be any sequence converging to 0.
    <2>2. Form the difference quotient:
        $$\frac{F(t + h_n) - F(t)}{h_n} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) \left( \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} \right) dx.$$
    <2>3. For almost every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the pointwise limit of the integrand as $n \to \infty$ is:
        $$\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x) \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} = f(x) \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \cos(tx) = -x f(x) \sin(tx).$$
    <2>4. By the **Mean Value Theorem**, for each $x$ and $h_n$, there exists $\theta_n$ between $t$ and $t + h_n$ such that:
        $$\left| \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} \right| = |-x \sin(\theta_n x)| \le |x| |\sin(\theta_n x)| \le |x|.$$
    <2>5. Therefore, the integrand is dominated by:
        $$\left| f(x) \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} \right| \le |x f(x)| \eqqcolon g(x).$$
    <2>6. By hypothesis, $g(x) = |x f(x)| \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
    <2>7. By the **Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem (LDCT)**:
        $$F'(t) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{F(t + h_n) - F(t)}{h_n} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \lim_{n \to \infty} f(x) \frac{\cos((t + h_n)x) - \cos(tx)}{h_n} \, dx = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) \sin(tx) \, dx.$$

<1>2. Part 2: Evaluating $\lim_{t \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \, dx$:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. For each fixed $x \in (0, 1]$, the pointwise limit as $t \to 0$ is:
        $$\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} = \left. \frac{d}{dt} e^{t\sqrt{x}} \right|_{t=0} = \sqrt{x}.$$
    <2>2. We dominate the integrand for all $t \in [-1, 1] \setminus \{0\}$:
        - By the Mean Value Theorem applied to $u \mapsto e^u$, there exists $\xi$ between $0$ and $t\sqrt{x}$ such that:
          $$\left| \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \right| = \left| \sqrt{x} e^\xi \right| \le \sqrt{x} e^{|t|\sqrt{x}} \le 1 \cdot e^{1 \cdot 1} = e.$$
    <2>3. The constant function $g(x) = e$ is integrable on the finite measure space $[0, 1]$ ($\int_0^1 e \, dx = e < \infty$).
    <2>4. By the **Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem**:
        $$\lim_{t \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \, dx = \int_0^1 \lim_{t \to 0} \left( \frac{e^{t\sqrt{x}} - 1}{t} \right) dx = \int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx.$$
    <2>5. We compute the integral:
        $$\int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx = \left[ \frac{2}{3} x^{3/2} \right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3}.$$

<1>3. Conclusion:
    1. $F'(t) = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) \sin(tx) \, dx$;
    2. The limit is $\frac{2}{3}$.
    Q.E.D.
:::
