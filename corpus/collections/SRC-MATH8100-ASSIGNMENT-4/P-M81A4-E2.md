::: problem
1. Prove that $L^1$ convergence implies convergence in measure, and give a counterexample to the converse.

2. Suppose $|f_k|\leq g\in L^1$ and $f_k\to f$ in measure.
   Prove that $f\in L^1$ and $f_k\to f$ in $L^1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
**Goal.** (1) $L^1$ convergence implies convergence in measure, with a counterexample to the converse. (2) Dominated convergence in measure implies $L^1$ convergence.

<1>1. (1) $L^1$ convergence implies convergence in measure.
<2>1. For $\eps > 0$, $\mu\theset{|f_k - f| \ge \eps} \le \frac{1}{\eps}\int |f_k - f|\,d\mu$.
::: {.proof}
Chebyshev's (Markov's) inequality.
:::
<2>2. Since $\int |f_k - f| \to 0$, the right side tends to $0$.
::: {.proof}
$L^1$ convergence means $\|f_k - f\|_1 \to 0$.
:::
<2>3. Hence $f_k \to f$ in measure.
::: {.proof}
$\mu\theset{|f_k - f| \ge \eps} \to 0$ for every $\eps > 0$.
:::

<1>2. (1) Counterexample to the converse.
<2>1. On $[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure, let $f_k$ be the indicator of a sliding interval of length $1/k$.
::: {.proof}
e.g. $f_k = \mathbf 1_{[j/k, (j+1)/k]}$ as $k$ ranges over the "typewriter" sequence.
:::
<2>2. $f_k \to 0$ in measure but not in $L^1$.
::: {.proof}
$\mu\theset{f_k \neq 0} = 1/k \to 0$, so $f_k \to 0$ in measure; but $\int |f_k| = 1$ for all $k$, so $f_k \not\to 0$ in $L^1$.
:::

<1>3. (2) Dominated convergence in measure.
<2>1. $f \in L^1$.
::: {.proof}
$|f_k| \le g \in L^1$ and $f_k \to f$ in measure implies $|f| \le g$ a.e. (a subsequence converges a.e. to $f$), so $f \in L^1$.
:::
<2>2. $f_k \to f$ in $L^1$.
::: {.proof}
this is the dominated convergence theorem in the "convergence in measure" form: if $|f_k| \le g \in L^1$ and $f_k \to f$ in measure, then $\int |f_k - f| \to 0$ (every subsequence has a further subsequence converging a.e., and dominated convergence applies).
:::

<1>4. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
<1>1 and <1>2 prove (1); <1>3 proves (2).
:::
:::
