::: {.problem}
(a) Prove that every compact subspace of a Hausdorff space is closed.
Show by example that the Hausdorff hypothesis cannot be removed.

(b) Prove that every compact Hausdorff space is normal.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Let $K$ be a compact subspace of a Hausdorff space $X$ and let $x\in X\setminus K$.
::: {.proof}
We will construct an open neighborhood of $x$ disjoint from $K$.
:::

<1>2. For each $y\in K$, choose disjoint open sets $U_y,V_y\subseteq X$ with $x\in U_y$ and $y\in V_y$.
::: {.proof}
This is possible because $X$ is Hausdorff and $x\neq y$.
:::

<1>3. There exist $y_1,\dots,y_n\in K$ such that $K\subseteq V_{y_1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{y_n}$.
::: {.proof}
The sets $V_y$ cover the compact space $K$, so a finite subcover exists.
:::

<1>4. The set $U=U_{y_1}\cap\cdots\cap U_{y_n}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ disjoint from $K$.
::: {.proof}
It is open and contains $x$. If $z\in U\cap K$, then $z\in V_{y_i}$ for some $i$ by <1>3, while $z\in U\subseteq U_{y_i}$, contradicting $U_{y_i}\cap V_{y_i}=\varnothing$.
:::

<1>5. Hence $K$ is closed in $X$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>4, every point of $X\setminus K$ has an open neighborhood contained in $X\setminus K$, so the complement of $K$ is open.
:::

<1>6. The Hausdorff hypothesis cannot be omitted.
::: {.proof}
Let $X=\{0,1\}$ with the indiscrete topology $\{\varnothing,X\}$. Every subset of a finite space is compact, so $\{0\}$ is compact. But $\{0\}$ is not closed, since its complement $\{1\}$ is not open. The space is not Hausdorff.
:::

<1>7. Now let $X$ be compact Hausdorff, and let $A,B\subseteq X$ be disjoint closed sets.
::: {.proof}
To prove normality, it remains to separate arbitrary disjoint closed subsets by disjoint open neighborhoods.
:::

<1>8. The sets $A$ and $B$ are compact.
::: {.proof}
Closed subsets of a compact space are compact.
:::

<1>9. There exist disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ such that $A\subseteq U$ and $B\subseteq V$.
::: {.proof}
For every $(a,b)\in A\times B$, choose disjoint open neighborhoods $U_{a,b}$ of $a$ and $V_{a,b}$ of $b$. Fix $a\in A$. Compactness of $B$ gives finitely many $b_1,\dots,b_n$ with $B\subseteq\bigcup_iV_{a,b_i}$. Put $U_a=\bigcap_iU_{a,b_i}$ and $V_a=\bigcup_iV_{a,b_i}$; then $a\in U_a$, $B\subseteq V_a$, and $U_a\cap V_a=\varnothing$. Compactness of $A$ gives $a_1,\dots,a_m$ with $A\subseteq\bigcup_jU_{a_j}$. Finally let $U=\bigcup_jU_{a_j}$ and $V=\bigcap_jV_{a_j}$. Then $A\subseteq U$, $B\subseteq V$, and if $x\in U\cap V$, some $j$ has $x\in U_{a_j}\cap V_{a_j}$, a contradiction.
:::

<1>10. Therefore every compact Hausdorff space is normal.
::: {.proof}
Combine <1>7--<1>9.
:::
:::
