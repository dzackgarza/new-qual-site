::: {.problem}
Consider the equation
\[
\int_0^\infty \frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{4\cos(\pi\alpha/2)}.
\]
Find the set of real $\alpha$ for which the integral is absolutely convergent, and prove the equation for all such $\alpha$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The integral is absolutely convergent exactly for
\[
\boxed{-1<\alpha<3}.
\]
For every such $\alpha$,
\[
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty \frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{4\cos(\pi\alpha/2)}.}
\]
At $\alpha=1$ the right-hand side is interpreted by continuity; its value is
$1/2$.

<1>1. The improper integral is absolutely convergent exactly when
$-1<\alpha<3$.
::: {.proof}
The integrand is nonnegative for $x>0$, so absolute convergence is the same
as convergence. Near $0$,
\[
\frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\sim x^\alpha,
\]
and $\int_0^1x^\alpha\,dx$ converges exactly when $\alpha>-1$. Near infinity,
\[
\frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\sim x^{\alpha-4},
\]
and $\int_1^\infty x^{\alpha-4}\,dx$ converges exactly when
$\alpha-4<-1$, equivalently $\alpha<3$. Combining the two conditions gives
$-1<\alpha<3$.
:::

<1>2. Suppose $-1<\alpha<3$ and $\alpha\notin\mathbb Z$. On
$\mathbb C\setminus[0,\infty)$ choose the branch
\[
z^\alpha=\exp(\alpha(\log|z|+i\arg z)),
\qquad 0<\arg z<2\pi,
\]
and set
\[
F(z)=\frac{z^\alpha}{(1+z^2)^2}.
\]
For a keyhole contour around the positive real axis, the two circular arcs
make vanishing contributions as their radii tend to $0$ and $\infty$.
::: {.proof}
On the outer circle $|z|=R$,
\[
|F(z)|=O(R^{\alpha-4}),
\]
so the integral over an arc of length $O(R)$ is $O(R^{\alpha-3})$, which tends
to $0$ because $\alpha<3$. On the inner circle $|z|=\varepsilon$,
\[
|F(z)|=O(\varepsilon^\alpha),
\]
so the integral is $O(\varepsilon^{\alpha+1})$, which tends to $0$ because
$\alpha>-1$.
:::

<1>3. If
\[
I(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty\frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx,
\]
then the two straight sides of the keyhole contour contribute
\[
(1-e^{2\pi i\alpha})I(\alpha).
\]
::: {.proof}
On the upper side of the positive axis, $z=x$ and $z^\alpha=x^\alpha$, so
the contribution tends to $I(\alpha)$. On the lower side,
$\arg z\to2\pi$, hence
\[
z^\alpha=e^{2\pi i\alpha}x^\alpha.
\]
That side is traversed from large $x$ to small $x$, so its contribution tends
to $-e^{2\pi i\alpha}I(\alpha)$. Their sum is the displayed expression.
:::

<1>4. The poles of $F$ inside the keyhole are the double poles at $i$ and
$-i$, and
\[
\operatorname{Res}(F,i)+\operatorname{Res}(F,-i)
=\frac{\alpha-1}{2}e^{\pi i\alpha}\sin\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}.
\]
::: {.proof}
For $a\in\{i,-i\}$,
\[
F(z)=\frac{z^\alpha}{(z-a)^2(z+a)^2},
\]
so
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Res}(F,a)
&=\left.\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{z^\alpha}{(z+a)^2}\right)\right|_{z=a}\\
&=\frac{\alpha-1}{4}a^{\alpha-3}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For the chosen branch, $\arg i=\pi/2$ and $\arg(-i)=3\pi/2$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
i^{\alpha-3}+(-i)^{\alpha-3}
&=e^{\pi i(\alpha-3)/2}+e^{3\pi i(\alpha-3)/2}\\
&=2e^{\pi i\alpha}\sin\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Multiplying by $(\alpha-1)/4$ gives the claimed residue sum.
:::

<1>5. For nonintegral $\alpha\in(-1,3)$,
\[
I(\alpha)=\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{4\cos(\pi\alpha/2)}.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>2--<1>4 and the residue theorem,
\[
(1-e^{2\pi i\alpha})I(\alpha)
=2\pi i\cdot\frac{\alpha-1}{2}e^{\pi i\alpha}
\sin\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}.
\]
Since
\[
1-e^{2\pi i\alpha}
=-2ie^{\pi i\alpha}\sin(\pi\alpha),
\]
we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
I(\alpha)
&=\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)}
{2\sin(\pi\alpha)}\\
&=\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{4\cos(\pi\alpha/2)},
\end{aligned}
\]
using $\sin(\pi\alpha)=2\sin(\pi\alpha/2)\cos(\pi\alpha/2)$.
:::

<1>6. The same identity holds at the integral values
$\alpha=0,1,2$ in the convergence interval.
::: {.proof}
Fix a compact interval $K\subset(-1,3)$. There are exponents
$-1<a<b<3$ with $K\subset[a,b]$. For $0<x\le1$ and $\alpha\in K$,
\[
0\le\frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\le x^a,
\]
while for $x\ge1$,
\[
0\le\frac{x^\alpha}{(1+x^2)^2}\le x^{b-4}.
\]
The two majorants are integrable. Dominated convergence therefore shows
that $I(\alpha)$ is continuous on $(-1,3)$. The right-hand side is continuous
there except for an apparent singularity at $\alpha=1$, where
\[
\lim_{\alpha\to1}
\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{4\cos(\pi\alpha/2)}
=\frac12.
\]
Since the identity from <1>5 holds on the dense set of nonintegral
$\alpha\in(-1,3)$, continuity extends it to $\alpha=0,1,2$ as well.
:::
