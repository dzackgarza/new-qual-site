::: {.problem}
Using the duplication formula for $\Gamma(2z)$, prove that
\[
\int_0^1\log\Gamma(t)\,dt
=\log\sqrt{2\pi}.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
\[
I=\int_0^1\log\Gamma(t)\,dt.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{I=\frac12\log(2\pi)=\log\sqrt{2\pi}}.
\]

<1>1. The integral defining $I$ converges.
::: {.proof}
For $t>0$, the Gamma function is positive, so $\log\Gamma(t)$ is
well-defined and real-valued. The only possible endpoint issue is at $t=0$.
From the functional equation
\[
\Gamma(t+1)=t\Gamma(t)
\]
and $\Gamma(1)=1$, we have
\[
\Gamma(t)=\frac{\Gamma(t+1)}{t}.
\]
As $t\downarrow0$, $\Gamma(t+1)\to1$, so
\[
\log\Gamma(t)=-\log t+O(1).
\]
Since $-\log t$ is integrable at $0$, the improper integral converges.
:::

<1>2. The duplication formula implies, for $0<t<1/2$,
\[
\log\Gamma(t)+\log\Gamma(t+1/2)
=\log\Gamma(2t)+(1-2t)\log2+\frac12\log\pi.
\]
::: {.proof}
McMullen's duplication formula is
\[
2\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(2t)
=2^{2t}\Gamma(t)\Gamma(t+1/2).
\]
Rearranging gives
\[
\Gamma(t)\Gamma(t+1/2)
=2^{1-2t}\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(2t).
\]
All terms are positive for $t>0$, so taking logarithms gives the displayed
identity.
:::

<1>3. Integrating the identity in <1>2 over $0<t<1/2$ gives
\[
I=\frac12I+\frac14\log(2\pi).
\]
::: {.proof}
The left-hand side integrates to
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\int_0^{1/2}\log\Gamma(t)\,dt
+\int_0^{1/2}\log\Gamma(t+1/2)\,dt\\
&\qquad=
\int_0^{1/2}\log\Gamma(t)\,dt
+\int_{1/2}^1\log\Gamma(u)\,du
=I.
\end{aligned}
\]

For the first term on the right-hand side of <1>2, put $u=2t$:
\[
\int_0^{1/2}\log\Gamma(2t)\,dt
=\frac12\int_0^1\log\Gamma(u)\,du
=\frac12I.
\]
Also,
\[
\int_0^{1/2}(1-2t)\,dt=\frac14,
\qquad
\int_0^{1/2}\frac12\log\pi\,dt=\frac14\log\pi.
\]
Therefore
\[
I
=\frac12I+\frac14\log2+\frac14\log\pi
=\frac12I+\frac14\log(2\pi).
\]
:::

<1>4. Solving for $I$ yields the claimed value.
::: {.proof}
From <1>3,
\[
\frac12I=\frac14\log(2\pi),
\]
so
\[
I=\frac12\log(2\pi)=\log\sqrt{2\pi}.
\]
:::
:::
