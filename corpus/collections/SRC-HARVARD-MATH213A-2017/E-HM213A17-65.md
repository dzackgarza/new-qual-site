::: {.problem}
Give an example of a sequence of degree-three rational maps
\[
f_n:\widehat{\mathbb C}\to\widehat{\mathbb C}
\]
such that
\[
f_n(S^1)\subset S^1
\]
and $f_n(z)\to z$ uniformly on $S^1$.
For your example, compute
\[
\int_{S^1}\frac{f_n'(z)}{f_n(z)}\,dz.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
For $c\in(0,1)$, set
\[
B_c(z)=\frac{z-c}{1-cz}.
\]
Fix
\[
a=\frac12,
\qquad
b_n=\frac12+\frac1{4n},
\]
and define
\[
\boxed{f_n(z)=z\frac{B_a(z)}{B_{b_n}(z)}}.
\]

<1>1. Each $f_n$ is a rational map of degree $3$.
::: {.proof}
Writing the function as one quotient gives
\[
f_n(z)
=\frac{z(z-a)(1-b_nz)}{(1-az)(z-b_n)}.
\]
The numerator has degree $3$ and the denominator degree $2$. Their zeros
are respectively
\[
0,\ a,\ 1/b_n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
b_n,\ 1/a.
\]
Since $a\neq b_n$ and $b_n\neq a^{-1}$, there is no common factor. Thus the
induced rational map on $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ has degree $3$.
:::

<1>2. One has $f_n(S^1)\subset S^1$.
::: {.proof}
For $|z|=1$ and real $0<c<1$,
\[
|z-c|=|1-cz|,
\]
so $|B_c(z)|=1$. Hence on $S^1$,
\[
|f_n(z)|
=|z|\frac{|B_a(z)|}{|B_{b_n}(z)|}
=1.
\]
:::

<1>3. The maps $f_n$ converge uniformly to the identity on $S^1$.
::: {.proof}
Since $b_n\to a$ and the denominators $1-b_nz$ stay uniformly away from
zero on $S^1$, the functions $B_{b_n}$ converge uniformly to $B_a$ on
$S^1$. Moreover $|B_{b_n}|=1$ there, so
\[
\frac{B_a}{B_{b_n}}\longrightarrow1
\]
uniformly on $S^1$. Therefore
\[
f_n(z)=z\frac{B_a(z)}{B_{b_n}(z)}\longrightarrow z
\]
uniformly on $S^1$.
:::

<1>4. Inside the unit disk, $f_n$ has two zeros and one pole, all simple.
::: {.proof}
From the factored expression in <1>1, the zeros in $\Delta$ are
\[
z=0,\qquad z=a,
\]
while the only pole in $\Delta$ is
\[
z=b_n.
\]
The remaining zero $1/b_n$ and pole $1/a$ lie outside the closed unit disk.
:::

<1>5. Consequently
\[
\boxed{
\int_{S^1}\frac{f_n'(z)}{f_n(z)}\,dz=2\pi i.}
\]
::: {.proof}
By the argument principle for a meromorphic function,
\[
\frac1{2\pi i}
\int_{S^1}\frac{f_n'(z)}{f_n(z)}\,dz
=N-P,
\]
where $N$ and $P$ are the numbers of zeros and poles in the disk, counted
with multiplicity. By <1>4, $N=2$ and $P=1$. Hence the integral is
$2\pi i$.
:::
