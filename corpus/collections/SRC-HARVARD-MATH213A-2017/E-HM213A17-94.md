::: {.problem}
Use the method of stationary phase to estimate, by hand, the average value of
\[
\sin^{100}(x).
\]
Do not use a calculator.
The exact answer is about $1/12$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The stationary-phase/Laplace estimate for the average over one period is
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{avg}(\sin^{100}x)
\approx\frac1{\sqrt{50\pi}}.}
\]
This is roughly $0.08$, hence close to $1/12$.

<1>1. By symmetry, the average can be written
\[
A
=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{100}x\,dx.
\]
::: {.proof}
The function $\sin^{100}x$ has period $\pi$ and is symmetric in each
quadrant. Hence
\[
\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^{100}x\,dx
=4\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{100}x\,dx.
\]
Dividing by the period length $2\pi$ gives
\[
A
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^{100}x\,dx
=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{100}x\,dx.
\]
:::

<1>2. Center the integral at the maximum $x=\pi/2$ by setting
$t=\pi/2-x$. Then
\[
A=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^{100}t\,dt.
\]
::: {.proof}
Under the substitution $t=\pi/2-x$,
\[
\sin x=\cos t,
\]
and reversing the limits yields the displayed formula.
:::

<1>3. Near $t=0$, the exponent has the quadratic approximation
\[
100\log(\cos t)=-50t^2+O(t^4).
\]
::: {.proof}
The Taylor expansion at the stationary point is
\[
\cos t=1-\frac{t^2}{2}+O(t^4).
\]
Since $\log(1+u)=u+O(u^2)$,
\[
\log(\cos t)
=-\frac{t^2}{2}+O(t^4).
\]
Multiplying by $100$ gives
\[
100\log(\cos t)
=-50t^2+O(100t^4).
\]
Thus the peak has Gaussian width on the scale $t\asymp100^{-1/2}$.
:::

<1>4. Replacing the peak by its Gaussian model gives
\[
\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^{100}t\,dt
\approx\int_0^\infty e^{-50t^2}\,dt
=\frac12\sqrt{\frac\pi{50}}.
\]
::: {.proof}
Away from a small neighborhood of $0$, $|\cos t|<1$ by a fixed amount,
so raising to the $100$-th power suppresses that part of the integral.
Inside the peak, <1>3 replaces $\cos^{100}t$ by $e^{-50t^2}$ to leading
order. Extending the short Gaussian peak to the positive half-line changes
only the lower-order tail. The standard Gaussian integral gives
\[
\int_0^\infty e^{-50t^2}\,dt
=\frac12\sqrt{\frac\pi{50}}.
\]
:::

<1>5. Therefore
\[
A\approx\frac1{\sqrt{50\pi}}.
\]
::: {.proof}
Substituting <1>4 into <1>2,
\[
A
\approx\frac2\pi\cdot\frac12\sqrt{\frac\pi{50}}
=\frac1{\sqrt{50\pi}}.
\]
For a hand estimate, use $3<\pi<22/7$. Then
\[
\frac1{\sqrt{1100/7}}
<\frac1{\sqrt{50\pi}}
<\frac1{\sqrt{150}}.
\]
Both endpoints are about eight hundredths; in particular this predicts a
value close to
\[
\frac1{12}\approx0.083.
\]
This agrees with the scale stated in the problem.
:::
