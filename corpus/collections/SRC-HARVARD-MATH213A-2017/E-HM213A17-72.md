::: {.problem}
Let $f,g\in\mathcal O(\mathbb C)$ have no common zeros.
Show that
\[
(f,g)=(1),
\]
i.e. there exist $r,s\in\mathcal O(\mathbb C)$ such that
\[
fr+gs=1.
\]

Hint: split $1/(fg)$ as $F+G$, with the poles of $F$ supported on the zeros of $f$ and the poles of $G$ supported on the zeros of $g$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. At every zero $a$ of $f$, the meromorphic function
\[
\frac1{f g}
\]
has a well-defined finite principal part.
::: {.proof}
Because $f$ and $g$ have no common zeros, $g(a)\neq0$. Thus near $a$ the
only pole of $1/(fg)$ comes from the zero of $f$, and its order is finite.
Hence its Laurent expansion at $a$ has a finite negative-power tail, which is
the desired principal part.
:::

<1>2. There exists a meromorphic function $F$ whose poles are exactly among
the zeros of $f$ and whose principal part at each such zero agrees with the
principal part of $1/(fg)$.
::: {.proof}
The zeros of a nonzero entire function are discrete. Apply the
Mittag--Leffler theorem to the zero set of $f$, prescribing at each zero the
principal part described in <1>1. If $f$ has no zeros, take $F=0$.
:::

<1>3. Define
\[
G=\frac1{fg}-F.
\]
Then the only poles of $G$ occur at zeros of $g$.
::: {.proof}
At a zero of $f$, the principal part of $F$ was chosen to equal that of
$1/(fg)$, so the difference has a removable singularity there. Away from the
zeros of $f$ and $g$, both terms are holomorphic. Thus the only remaining
possible poles are the zeros of $g$.
:::

<1>4. The functions
\[
s=fF,
\qquad
r=gG
\]
are entire.
::: {.proof}
Every pole of $F$ occurs at a zero of $f$, and its principal part came from
$1/(fg)$. Since $g$ is nonzero there, the pole order of $F$ is at most the
zero order of $f$, so multiplication by $f$ removes it. Hence $fF$ is entire.

Likewise every pole of $G$ occurs at a zero of $g$. From
\[
G=\frac1{fg}-F
\]
and holomorphicity of $F$ at the zeros of $g$, the pole order of $G$ is at
most the corresponding zero order of $g$. Thus $gG$ is entire.
:::

<1>5. These entire functions satisfy
\[
fr+gs=1.
\]
::: {.proof}
From <1>3,
\[
\frac1{fg}=F+G.
\]
Multiplying by $fg$ gives
\[
1=fgF+fgG
=g(fF)+f(gG)
=gs+fr.
\]
Therefore $(f,g)=(1)$ in $\mathcal O(\mathbb C)$.
:::
