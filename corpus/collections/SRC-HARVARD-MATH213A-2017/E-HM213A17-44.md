::: {.problem}
(a) Find all proper holomorphic maps
\[
f:\mathbb C^*\to\mathbb C.
\]

(b) Find all proper holomorphic maps
\[
g:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C^*.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
For part (a), the proper maps are exactly the Laurent polynomials
\[
\boxed{
f(z)=\sum_{k=-m}^{n}a_kz^k,
\qquad
m,n\ge1,
\quad
a_{-m}a_n\neq0.}
\]
Thus a proper map must have a genuine pole at both ends $0$ and $\infty$
of $\mathbb C^*$.

For part (b), there are no proper holomorphic maps
$\mathbb C\to\mathbb C^*$.

<1>1. If $f:\mathbb C^*\to\mathbb C$ is proper, then
\[
|f(z)|\longrightarrow\infty
\quad\text{as }z\to0
\quad\text{and as }z\to\infty.
\]
::: {.proof}
Fix $R>0$. The closed disk
\[
K_R=\{w\in\mathbb C:|w|\le R\}
\]
is compact. Properness implies $f^{-1}(K_R)$ is compact in $\mathbb C^*$.
Hence it is contained in an annulus
\[
\varepsilon_R\le |z|\le M_R
\]
for some $0<\varepsilon_R<M_R<\infty$. Therefore
\[
|z|<\varepsilon_R
\quad\text{or}\quad
|z|>M_R
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|f(z)|>R.
\]
Since $R$ is arbitrary, $|f(z)|$ tends to infinity at both ends.
:::

<1>2. Such an $f$ has a pole at both $0$ and $\infty$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>1, $f$ is nonzero near $0$ and
\[
\frac1{f(z)}\longrightarrow0
\qquad(z\to0).
\]
Thus $1/f$ has a removable singularity at $0$ with value $0$, so $f$
has a pole at $0$.

For the other end, apply the same argument to
\[
F(\zeta)=f(1/\zeta)
\]
near $\zeta=0$. Since $|F(\zeta)|\to\infty$, the point $\infty$ is a
pole of $f$.
:::

<1>3. Therefore every proper $f:\mathbb C^*\to\mathbb C$ is a Laurent
polynomial
\[
f(z)=\sum_{k=-m}^{n}a_kz^k
\]
with $m,n\ge1$ and $a_{-m}a_n\neq0$.
::: {.proof}
A holomorphic function on $\mathbb C^*$ has a Laurent expansion valid on
the annulus $0<|z|<\infty$. Because $0$ is a pole, only finitely many
negative powers occur; because $\infty$ is a pole, only finitely many
positive powers occur. Hence the Laurent series is finite.

The pole at $0$ forces a nonzero negative extreme term $a_{-m}z^{-m}$
with $m\ge1$, and the pole at $\infty$ forces a nonzero positive extreme
term $a_nz^n$ with $n\ge1$.
:::

<1>4. Conversely, every Laurent polynomial of the form in <1>3 is proper
as a map $\mathbb C^*\to\mathbb C$.
::: {.proof}
The extreme terms give
\[
|f(z)|\longrightarrow\infty
\qquad(z\to0)
\]
and
\[
|f(z)|\longrightarrow\infty
\qquad(z\to\infty).
\]
Let $K\subset\mathbb C$ be compact and choose $R$ with
$K\subset\{|w|\le R\}$. The two limits provide
$0<\varepsilon<M<\infty$ such that
\[
f^{-1}(K)\subset\{\varepsilon\le|z|\le M\}.
\]
The set $f^{-1}(K)$ is closed, and the displayed annulus is compact.
Therefore $f^{-1}(K)$ is compact, proving properness.
:::

<1>5. Now suppose $g:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C^*$ is proper. For every
$R>1$, there is $M_R>0$ such that for $|z|>M_R$ one has either
\[
|g(z)|<\frac1R
\]
for all such $z$, or
\[
|g(z)|>R
\]
for all such $z$.
::: {.proof}
The annulus
\[
A_R=\left\{w:\frac1R\le|w|\le R\right\}
\]
is compact in $\mathbb C^*$. Hence $g^{-1}(A_R)$ is compact in
$\mathbb C$, so it lies in some disk $|z|\le M_R$.

Thus, on the connected exterior region $|z|>M_R$, the continuous
function $|g|$ avoids the whole interval $[1/R,R]$. It cannot take values
on both sides of that interval, by the intermediate value theorem on a
path in the exterior region. Therefore exactly one of the two displayed
alternatives holds throughout that region.
:::

<1>6. Consequently, as $z\to\infty$, either
\[
g(z)\longrightarrow0
\]
or
\[
|g(z)|\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
::: {.proof}
Apply <1>5 first with $R=2$. Outside a sufficiently large disk, either
$|g|<1/2$ everywhere or $|g|>2$ everywhere.

In the first case, apply <1>5 with any $R\ge2$. On a sufficiently far
exterior region the alternative $|g|>R$ is incompatible with the already
valid inequality $|g|<1/2$, so necessarily $|g|<1/R$. Thus $g(z)\to0$.

In the second case the alternative $|g|<1/R$ is similarly impossible for
every $R\ge2$, so $|g(z)|>R$ eventually. Thus $|g(z)|\to\infty$.
:::

<1>7. Both alternatives in <1>6 are impossible for an entire nonvanishing
function.
::: {.proof}
If $g(z)\to0$ at infinity, then $g$ is bounded outside a large disk and
is bounded on the remaining compact disk. Hence $g$ is a bounded entire
function. Liouville's theorem makes it constant, and its limit at infinity
would force that constant to be $0$, contradicting $g(\mathbb C)\subset
\mathbb C^*$.

If $|g(z)|\to\infty$, then $1/g$ is entire because $g$ never vanishes,
and
\[
\frac1{g(z)}\longrightarrow0
\qquad(z\to\infty).
\]
The same Liouville argument forces $1/g\equiv0$, impossible.

Therefore no proper holomorphic map $\mathbb C\to\mathbb C^*$ exists.
:::
