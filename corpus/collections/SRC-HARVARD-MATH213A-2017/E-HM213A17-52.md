::: {.problem}
Prove that every proper holomorphic map $f:\mathbb H\to\mathbb H$ can be written in the form
\[
f(z)=a_0z+b_0+\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{a_i}{b_i-z},
\]
with $a_i\ge0$ and $b_i\in\mathbb R$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The map $f$ extends to a rational map on the Riemann sphere and takes
the extended real line $\widehat{\mathbb R}$ to itself.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
\phi(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}
\]
be a Cayley map from $\mathbb H$ to $\Delta$, and let $\psi=\phi^{-1}$.
Then
\[
F=\phi\circ f\circ\psi:\Delta\to\Delta
\]
is proper. By McMullen's Theorem 2.26, $F$ is a finite Blaschke product, so
it extends rationally to $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ and maps $S^1$ to itself.
Conjugating this extension by the Möbius maps $\phi$ and $\psi$ gives a
rational extension of $f$, and since $\psi(S^1)=\widehat{\mathbb R}$, this
extension maps $\widehat{\mathbb R}$ to itself.
:::

<1>2. The rational extension satisfies
\[
f(\overline z)=\overline{f(z)}.
\]
Consequently its finite poles off the real axis would occur in conjugate
pairs.
::: {.proof}
For every real $x$ at which $f$ is finite, <1>1 gives $f(x)\in\mathbb R$.
Thus the two rational functions
\[
f(z)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\overline{f(\overline z)}
\]
agree at infinitely many points, hence agree identically. Poles are therefore
preserved by complex conjugation.
:::

<1>3. Every finite pole of $f$ lies on $\mathbb R$.
::: {.proof}
There are no poles in $\mathbb H$, because $f$ is holomorphic there. If a
pole lay in the lower half-plane, then <1>2 would give its conjugate as a pole
in $\mathbb H$, a contradiction. Hence every finite pole is real.
:::

<1>4. Every finite pole $b$ is simple, and its residue is negative.
::: {.proof}
By <1>2 the Laurent coefficients of $f$ at the real point $b$ are real. If
$b$ were a pole of order $m$, write its leading term as
\[
c(z-b)^{-m},
\qquad c\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}.
\]
For $z=b+re^{i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<\pi$ and $r$ small, the leading
contribution to the imaginary part is
\[
-c\,r^{-m}\sin(m\theta).
\]
If $m\ge2$, then $\sin(m\theta)$ changes sign as $\theta$ ranges over
$(0,\pi)$, contradicting $\operatorname{Im}f(z)>0$ throughout
$\mathbb H$. Therefore $m=1$.

If
\[
f(z)=\frac{c}{z-b}+O(1),
\]
then along $z=b+iy$,
\[
\operatorname{Im}\frac{c}{iy}=-\frac{c}{y}.
\]
Positivity for small $y>0$ forces $c<0$. Writing $a=-c>0$, the polar part is
\[
\frac{a}{b-z}.
\]
:::

<1>5. Subtracting all finite polar parts gives a real polynomial:
\[
P(z)
=f(z)-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{a_i}{b_i-z},
\qquad
a_i>0,\quad b_i\in\mathbb R.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>3--<1>4 these terms remove every finite pole of the rational function
$f$. The remainder is therefore a rational function with no finite poles,
hence a polynomial. The conjugation symmetry in <1>2, shared by each polar
term, implies
\[
P(\overline z)=\overline{P(z)},
\]
so $P$ has real coefficients.
:::

<1>6. The polynomial $P$ has degree at most one.
::: {.proof}
Suppose
\[
P(z)=cz^m+O(z^{m-1}),
\qquad c\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\},
\]
with $m\ge2$. Along a ray $z=Re^{i\theta}$ in the upper half-plane, the
polar terms in <1>5 are $O(R^{-1})$, while
\[
\operatorname{Im}P(Re^{i\theta})
=cR^m\sin(m\theta)+O(R^{m-1}).
\]
As $\theta$ ranges through $(0,\pi)$, $\sin(m\theta)$ takes both signs.
For a suitable fixed $\theta$ and large $R$, this would make
$\operatorname{Im}f(Re^{i\theta})<0$, impossible. Hence $m\le1$.
:::

<1>7. Thus
\[
P(z)=a_0z+b_0
\]
with $a_0\ge0$ and $b_0\in\mathbb R$.
::: {.proof}
Reality of the coefficients follows from <1>5. Along the positive imaginary
axis,
\[
f(iy)=a_0iy+b_0+O(y^{-1})
\qquad(y\to\infty).
\]
Since $\operatorname{Im}f(iy)>0$, division by $y$ and passage to the limit
give $a_0\ge0$.
:::

<1>8. Combining the preceding steps gives
\[
\boxed{
f(z)=a_0z+b_0+\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{a_i}{b_i-z}}
\]
with $a_i\ge0$ and $b_i\in\mathbb R$.
::: {.proof}
This is exactly the decomposition in <1>5 together with <1>7. For finite
poles, <1>4 actually gives $a_i>0$; allowing $a_i=0$ does not change the
stated form.
:::
:::
