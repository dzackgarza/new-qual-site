::: {.problem}
Let $f$ be an entire function such that $f$ is nowhere zero and the set
\[
f^{-1}(1)
\]
is finite.
Prove that $f$ is constant.
:::

::: {.solution}
Suppose for contradiction that $f$ is nonconstant.

<1>1. The function $f$ cannot be a polynomial.
::: {.proof}
A nonconstant complex polynomial has a zero by the fundamental theorem of algebra. This contradicts the hypothesis that
\[
f(z)\ne0
\qquad(z\in\mathbb C).
\]
Hence a nonconstant $f$ would have to be transcendental entire.
:::

<1>2. A transcendental entire $f$ has an essential singularity at $\infty$.
::: {.proof}
Consider
\[
F(w)=f(1/w)
\]
near $w=0$. If $w=0$ were removable, then $f$ would be bounded outside a disk and hence, by the maximum principle applied together with compactness of the disk, bounded on all of $\mathbb C$; Liouville would make $f$ constant. If $w=0$ were a pole, then $f$ would have polynomial growth at infinity and hence would be a polynomial. Both alternatives contradict <1>1 and the assumed nonconstancy. Thus $w=0$, equivalently $\infty$ for $f$, is an essential singularity.
:::

<1>3. Great Picard gives the contradiction.
::: {.proof}
By Great Picard, in every neighborhood of the essential singularity at $\infty$, the function $f$ assumes every complex value with at most one exception.

The value $0$ is omitted everywhere by hypothesis, so $0$ must be the exceptional value. Therefore $f$ takes the value $1$ in every sufficiently small punctured neighborhood of $\infty$; equivalently, for every $R>0$ there exists
\[
z_R,
\qquad |z_R|>R,
\qquad f(z_R)=1.
\]
Choosing $R\to\infty$ produces infinitely many distinct points of $f^{-1}(1)$, contradicting the assumption that this fiber is finite.

Hence $f$ cannot be nonconstant, so $f$ is constant.
:::
:::
