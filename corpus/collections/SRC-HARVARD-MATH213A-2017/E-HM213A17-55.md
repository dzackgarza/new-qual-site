::: {.problem}
Let $T(a,b,c)\subset\mathbb H$ be a hyperbolic triangle with interior angles $a,b,c$.
Prove that
\[
\operatorname{area}T(a,b,c)=\pi-a-b-c
\]
as follows.

(a) Note that $T(0,0,0)$ is an ideal triangle with all vertices at infinity.
Show that all ideal triangles have area $\pi$.

(b) Set
\[
A(a)=\operatorname{area}T(\pi-a,0,0).
\]
Show geometrically that
\[
A(a+a')=A(a)+A(a'),
\]
and conclude that $A(a)=a$.

(c) Extend the sides of $T(a,b,c)$ to rays and use the corresponding ideal vertices to relate the area of $T(a,b,c)$ to the areas of $T(a,0,0)$, $T(b,0,0)$, and $T(c,0,0)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The area is
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{area}T(a,b,c)=\pi-a-b-c}.
\]

<1>1. Every ideal triangle has area $\pi$.
::: {.proof}
Any ordered triple of distinct boundary points of $\mathbb H$ can be sent to
$0,1,\infty$ by an automorphism of $\mathbb H$. Since such automorphisms are
hyperbolic isometries, it is enough to compute the area of the ideal triangle
with vertices $0,1,\infty$.

Its sides from $0$ and $1$ to $\infty$ are the vertical lines $x=0$ and
$x=1$, while the third side is the semicircle
\[
\left(x-\frac12\right)^2+y^2=\frac14,
\qquad
y=\sqrt{x(1-x)}.
\]
For the metric $|dz|/y$, the area element is $dx\,dy/y^2$. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{area}T(0,0,0)
&=\int_0^1\int_{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}^\infty\frac{dy\,dx}{y^2}\\
&=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
With $x=\sin^2 t$, $0\le t\le\pi/2$, this becomes
\[
2\int_0^{\pi/2}dt=\pi.
\]
:::

<1>2. If
\[
A(t)=\operatorname{area}T(\pi-t,0,0),
\qquad 0\le t\le\pi,
\]
then
\[
A(t+t')=A(t)+A(t')
\]
whenever $t,t',t+t'\in[0,\pi]$.
::: {.proof}
The parameter $t$ is the angular defect at the unique finite vertex: its
interior angle is $\pi-t$. The geometric cut indicated in the source is made
by extending a geodesic side to the ideal boundary and inserting the geodesic
through the finite vertex with the prescribed intermediate ideal endpoint.
The two resulting one-finite-vertex triangles have angular defects $t$ and
$t'$, while their union has defect $t+t'$. Their interiors are disjoint, so
hyperbolic area is additive and gives the displayed identity.
:::

<1>3. One has $A(t)=t$ for every $0\le t\le\pi$.
::: {.proof}
First,
\[
A(0)=0
\]
because $T(\pi,0,0)$ is degenerate, while by <1>1,
\[
A(\pi)=\operatorname{area}T(0,0,0)=\pi.
\]
The area varies continuously with the finite angle: after fixing the two
ideal vertices by an isometry, the remaining geodesic side depends
continuously on that angle, and the corresponding regions vary monotonically
on compact subranges. Thus $A$ is continuous.

By <1>2, for every positive integer $m$,
\[
mA(\pi/m)=A(\pi)=\pi,
\]
so $A(\pi/m)=\pi/m$. Additivity then gives
\[
A(k\pi/m)=k\pi/m
\]
for $0\le k\le m$. These rational multiples of $\pi$ are dense in
$[0,\pi]$; continuity therefore yields
\[
A(t)=t
\]
throughout the interval.
:::

<1>4. The source figure decomposes an ideal triangle into $T(a,b,c)$ and
three one-finite-vertex triangles of areas $A(a)$, $A(b)$, and $A(c)$.
::: {.proof}
Extend the three sides of $T(a,b,c)$ along the rays shown in the source
figure until they reach the ideal boundary. The three resulting ideal
vertices bound an ideal triangle containing the original triangle. At the
vertex whose interior angle in the original triangle is $a$, the adjacent
complementary piece has two ideal vertices and finite angle $\pi-a$; hence it
is a triangle of type $T(\pi-a,0,0)$ and has area $A(a)$. The same angle
chase gives complementary pieces of types $T(\pi-b,0,0)$ and
$T(\pi-c,0,0)$ at the other two vertices.

These four regions have disjoint interiors and fill the ideal triangle.
Consequently
\[
\pi
=\operatorname{area}T(a,b,c)+A(a)+A(b)+A(c).
\]
:::

<1>5. Therefore
\[
\operatorname{area}T(a,b,c)=\pi-a-b-c.
\]
::: {.proof}
Substitute $A(t)=t$ from <1>3 into the decomposition in <1>4:
\[
\pi
=\operatorname{area}T(a,b,c)+a+b+c.
\]
Rearranging gives the claimed formula.
:::
