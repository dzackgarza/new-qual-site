::: {.problem}
Given $A,B,C\in\mathbb C$, prove that there is a unique quadratic differential
\[
q=q(z)\,dz^2
\]
on $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ having double poles at $0$, $1$, and $\infty$, residues $A$, $B$, and $C$ there respectively, and no other singularities.

Here, if
\[
q=\left(\frac{a}{z^2}+O\!\left(\frac1z\right)\right)dz^2
\]
near $0$, then $\operatorname{Res}_0(q)=a$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The unique differential is
\[
\boxed{
q=
\left(
\frac{A}{z^2}
+\frac{B}{(z-1)^2}
+\frac{C-A-B}{z(z-1)}
\right)dz^2.
}
\]
Here ``double poles'' must be understood as poles of order at most two: if one of $A,B,C$ vanishes, the corresponding quadratic term may disappear, as is unavoidable for arbitrary prescribed residues.

<1>1. The displayed differential has no finite singularities except possibly at $0$ and $1$, and its residues there are $A$ and $B$.
::: {.proof}
Near $z=0$,
\[
\frac{B}{(z-1)^2}=O(1),
\qquad
\frac{C-A-B}{z(z-1)}=O\!\left(\frac1z\right).
\]
Thus
\[
q=
\left(
\frac{A}{z^2}+O\!\left(\frac1z\right)
\right)dz^2,
\]
so
\[
\operatorname{Res}_0(q)=A.
\]

Similarly, with $u=z-1$,
\[
\frac{A}{z^2}=O(1),
\qquad
\frac{C-A-B}{z(z-1)}=O\!\left(\frac1u\right),
\]
and hence
\[
q=
\left(
\frac{B}{u^2}+O\!\left(\frac1u\right)
\right)du^2.
\]
Therefore
\[
\operatorname{Res}_1(q)=B.
\]
:::

<1>2. The residue at infinity is $C$.
::: {.proof}
As $z\to\infty$,
\[
\frac{1}{(z-1)^2}
=\frac1{z^2}+O(z^{-3}),
\qquad
\frac{1}{z(z-1)}
=\frac1{z^2}+O(z^{-3}).
\]
Consequently
\[
q
=\left(
\frac{A+B+(C-A-B)}{z^2}+O(z^{-3})
\right)dz^2
=\left(
\frac{C}{z^2}+O(z^{-3})
\right)dz^2.
\]

To translate this into the local coordinate $w=1/z$ at infinity, note that
\[
dz^2=w^{-4}dw^2.
\]
Thus
\[
q
=\left(
\frac{C}{w^2}+O\!\left(\frac1w\right)
\right)dw^2,
\]
so
\[
\operatorname{Res}_\infty(q)=C.
\]
:::

<1>3. Any quadratic differential with the stated singularity restrictions has the form
\[
q=\frac{P(z)}{z^2(z-1)^2}\,dz^2,
\qquad
\deg P\le2.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since the only finite poles are at $0$ and $1$, each of order at most two,
\[
z^2(z-1)^2q(z)
\]
is an entire function. Because $q$ has at most a double pole at infinity, its coefficient in the $z$-coordinate satisfies
\[
q(z)=O(z^{-2})
\qquad(z\to\infty).
\]
Therefore
\[
z^2(z-1)^2q(z)=O(z^2).
\]
An entire function of growth $O(|z|^2)$ is a polynomial of degree at most two. Hence the asserted form follows.
:::

<1>4. The three prescribed residues determine $P$ uniquely.
::: {.proof}
Suppose a differential of the form in <1>3 has all three residues equal to zero. Write
\[
P(z)=az^2+bz+c.
\]
Near $0$, the coefficient of $z^{-2}$ is
\[
P(0)=c,
\]
so residue zero at $0$ gives $c=0$.

Near $1$, the coefficient of $(z-1)^{-2}$ is
\[
P(1)=a+b+c,
\]
so residue zero at $1$ gives
\[
a+b+c=0.
\]

At infinity,
\[
\frac{P(z)}{z^2(z-1)^2}
=\frac{a}{z^2}+O(z^{-3}),
\]
so residue zero at infinity gives $a=0$. Hence $c=0$, $a=0$, and then $b=0$. Thus the only differential with all three residues zero is the zero differential.

The difference of any two solutions with residues $A,B,C$ has zero residues, so it must vanish. This proves uniqueness, while <1>1--<1>2 prove existence.
