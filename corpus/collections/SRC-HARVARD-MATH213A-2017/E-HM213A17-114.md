::: {.problem}
Let $f:\Delta\to\mathbb C$ be univalent with
\[
f(0)=0,
\qquad
f'(0)=1,
\]
and suppose
\[
f(iz)=if(z)
\]
for every $z\in\Delta$.
Show that
\[
B\left(0,\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)\subseteq f(\Delta).
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
It is enough to prove that every omitted point $p\notin f(\Delta)$ satisfies
\[
|p|\ge\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

<1>1. The exterior transform
\[
F(\zeta)=\frac1{f(1/\zeta)}
\]
belongs to $\Sigma$ and satisfies
\[
F(i\zeta)=iF(\zeta).
\]
::: {.proof}
The symmetry $f(iz)=if(z)$ implies that the Taylor expansion of $f$ has only powers congruent to $1$ modulo $4$:
\[
f(z)=z+a_5z^5+a_9z^9+\cdots.
\]
Hence
\[
F(\zeta)
=\zeta+\frac{b_3}{\zeta^3}+\frac{b_7}{\zeta^7}+\cdots,
\]
so $F$ has the required normalization at infinity. It is univalent because $f$ is univalent. Also
\[
F(i\zeta)
=\frac1{f(1/(i\zeta))}
=\frac1{f(-i/\zeta)}
=\frac1{-i f(1/\zeta)}
=iF(\zeta).
\]
In particular $F(-\zeta)=-F(\zeta)$.
:::

<1>2. If $p\notin f(\Delta)$ and
\[
w=\frac1p,
\]
then both $w$ and $-w$ are omitted by $F$.
::: {.proof}
Since $0=f(0)$, an omitted point $p$ is nonzero. If $F(\zeta)=w=1/p$, then
\[
f(1/\zeta)=p,
\]
contrary to the choice of $p$. Thus $w$ is omitted. Since $F$ is odd, $-w$ is omitted as well.
:::

<1>3. The function
\[
H(\zeta)=\sqrt{F(\zeta)^2-w^2},
\qquad
H(\zeta)\sim\zeta\quad(\zeta\to\infty),
\]
is a normalized exterior univalent map, and its first area-theorem coefficient is
\[
c_1=-\frac{w^2}{2}.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>2, the function $F^2-w^2$ has no zero on the exterior disk. More precisely,
\[
q(\zeta)=\frac{F(\zeta)^2-w^2}{\zeta^2}
\]
is zero-free and, in the coordinate $u=1/\zeta$, extends holomorphically across $u=0$ with value $q(\infty)=1$. Thus $q$ is a zero-free holomorphic function on the simply connected disk $|u|<1$, so it has a holomorphic square root $Q$ normalized by $Q(\infty)=1$. Setting
\[
H(\zeta)=\zeta Q(\zeta)
\]
gives the asserted branch and $H(\zeta)/\zeta\to1$.

To check univalence, suppose $H(\zeta_1)=H(\zeta_2)$. Squaring gives
\[
F(\zeta_1)^2=F(\zeta_2)^2,
\]
so either $F(\zeta_1)=F(\zeta_2)$ or $F(\zeta_1)=-F(\zeta_2)$. In the first case univalence of $F$ gives $\zeta_1=\zeta_2$. In the second, oddness of $F$ gives $\zeta_1=-\zeta_2$.

The normalized square root is also odd: both $H(-\zeta)$ and $-H(\zeta)$ square to the same function and are asymptotic to $-\zeta$ at infinity. Hence if $\zeta_1=-\zeta_2$ and $H(\zeta_1)=H(\zeta_2)$, then $H(\zeta_1)=0$, impossible because $F^2-w^2$ is zero-free. Thus $H$ is univalent.

Finally, since
\[
F(\zeta)=\zeta+O(\zeta^{-3}),
\]
we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
H(\zeta)
&=\sqrt{\zeta^2-w^2+O(\zeta^{-2})}\\
&=\zeta-\frac{w^2}{2\zeta}+O(\zeta^{-3}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $H\in\Sigma$ and its coefficient of $1/\zeta$ is $c_1=-w^2/2$.
:::

<1>4. The area theorem gives the required disk.
::: {.proof}
For every map in $\Sigma$, the area theorem implies
\[
|c_1|\le1.
\]
Applying this to $H$ gives
\[
\frac{|w|^2}{2}\le1,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
|w|\le\sqrt2.
\]
Since $w=1/p$,
\[
|p|\ge\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
Thus no point of modulus less than $1/\sqrt2$ can be omitted by $f$, and therefore
\[
B\left(0,\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)\subseteq f(\Delta).
\]
:::
