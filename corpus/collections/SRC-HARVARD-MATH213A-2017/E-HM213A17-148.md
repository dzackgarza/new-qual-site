::: {.problem}
Determine the orbits of
\[
\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)
\]
acting by fractional linear transformations on
\[
\mathbb Q\cup\{\infty\},
\]
and determine the orbits of the principal congruence subgroup $\Gamma(2)$ on the same set.
:::

::: {.solution}
There is one $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$-orbit and three $\Gamma(2)$-orbits:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal O_\infty
&=\left\{\frac pq:\gcd(p,q)=1,\ p\text{ odd},\ q\text{ even}\right\},\\
\mathcal O_0
&=\left\{\frac pq:\gcd(p,q)=1,\ p\text{ even},\ q\text{ odd}\right\},\\
\mathcal O_1
&=\left\{\frac pq:\gcd(p,q)=1,\ p\text{ odd},\ q\text{ odd}\right\}.
\end{aligned}
}
\]
Here $\infty=1/0$ belongs to $\mathcal O_\infty$; the representatives of the three orbits are $\infty,0,1$.

<1>1. The action of $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ on $\mathbb Q\cup\{\infty\}$ is transitive.
::: {.proof}
Let $p/q\in\mathbb Q$ be in lowest terms. By Bezout's identity there are integers $r,s$ such that
\[
ps-qr=1.
\]
Then
\[
g=
\begin{pmatrix}
p&r\\
q&s
\end{pmatrix}
\in\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z),
\]
and
\[
g\cdot\infty=\frac pq.
\]
Thus every rational boundary point lies in the orbit of $\infty$, and so does $\infty$ itself. Hence there is exactly one orbit.
:::

<1>2. Reduction modulo $2$ gives three invariants for the $\Gamma(2)$ action.
::: {.proof}
Represent a boundary point by a primitive column vector
\[
\binom pq,
\qquad \gcd(p,q)=1,
\]
with $p/q$ as its projective class. Since the vector is primitive, its reduction modulo $2$ is one of the three nonzero vectors
\[
\binom10,
\qquad
\binom01,
\qquad
\binom11
\]
in $(\mathbb F_2)^2$.

Every matrix in
\[
\Gamma(2)=\{g\in\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z):g\equiv I\pmod2\}
\]
acts trivially on $(\mathbb F_2)^2$. Therefore the parity type of $(p,q)$ is constant on each $\Gamma(2)$-orbit. The three displayed parity classes are thus distinct orbits or unions of orbits.
:::

<1>3. Every primitive pair $(p,q)$ with $p$ odd and $q$ even lies in the orbit of $\infty$.
::: {.proof}
Choose integers $b,d$ with
\[
pd-qb=1.
\]
Because $p$ is odd and $q$ is even, reduction modulo $2$ forces $d$ to be odd. All solutions are
\[
(b+kp,d+kq),
\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Since $p$ is odd, choose the parity of $k$ so that $b+kp$ is even; the parity of $d+kq$ remains odd. Renaming this solution $(b,d)$ gives
\[
g=
\begin{pmatrix}
p&b\\
q&d
\end{pmatrix}
\equiv
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\pmod2.
\]
Thus $g\in\Gamma(2)$ and
\[
g\cdot\infty=\frac pq.
\]
:::

<1>4. Every primitive pair $(p,q)$ with $p$ even and $q$ odd lies in the orbit of $0$.
::: {.proof}
Choose integers $a,c$ with
\[
aq-cp=1.
\]
Modulo $2$, this forces $a$ to be odd. Replacing a solution by
\[
(a+kp,c+kq)
\]
preserves the equation; because $q$ is odd and $p$ is even, choose $k$ so that $c+kq$ is even while $a+kp$ remains odd. Then
\[
g=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&p\\
c&q
\end{pmatrix}
\in\Gamma(2),
\]
and
\[
g\cdot0=\frac pq.
\]
:::

<1>5. Every primitive pair $(p,q)$ with both $p$ and $q$ odd lies in the orbit of $1$.
::: {.proof}
Choose integers $a,c$ with
\[
aq-cp=1.
\]
Modulo $2$, since $p$ and $q$ are both odd,
\[
a-c\equiv1\pmod2,
\]
so $a$ and $c$ have opposite parity. Replacing $(a,c)$ by
\[
(a+p,c+q)
\]
flips both parities without changing $aq-cp$. Hence we may arrange
\[
a\text{ odd},
\qquad
c\text{ even}.
\]
Set
\[
b=p-a,
\qquad
d=q-c.
\]
Then $b$ is even, $d$ is odd, and
\[
ad-bc
=a(q-c)-(p-a)c
=aq-cp
=1.
\]
Thus
\[
g=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&b\\
c&d
\end{pmatrix}
\in\Gamma(2).
\]
Finally,
\[
g\cdot1
=\frac{a+b}{c+d}
=\frac pq.
\]
:::

<1>6. Hence these are exactly the three $\Gamma(2)$-orbits.
::: {.proof}
By <1>2 no two different parity classes can lie in the same $\Gamma(2)$-orbit. By <1>3--<1>5 each parity class is itself one orbit, represented respectively by
\[
\infty=\frac10,
\qquad
0=\frac01,
\qquad
1=\frac11.
\]
This proves the classification.
:::
