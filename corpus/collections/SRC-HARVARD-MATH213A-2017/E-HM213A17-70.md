::: {.problem}
Give an example of a canonical product
\[
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z}{a_n}\right)
\]
that has order exactly $1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Take
\[
\boxed{a_n=n(\log(n+1))^2}
\]
and set
\[
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z}{a_n}\right).
\]

<1>1. The series
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{a_n}
\]
converges.
::: {.proof}
For large $n$, $\log(n+1)$ is comparable to $\log n$, so
\[
\frac1{a_n}
=\frac1{n(\log(n+1))^2}
\asymp\frac1{n(\log n)^2}.
\]
The latter series converges by the integral test, since
\[
\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x(\log x)^2}<\infty.
\]
:::

<1>2. Consequently the displayed product is a genus-zero canonical product
and defines an entire function.
::: {.proof}
For genus zero the Weierstrass factor is $E_0(w)=1-w$. Convergence of
\(\sum 1/|a_n|\) is exactly the criterion ensuring that
\[
\prod_n E_0(z/a_n)
=\prod_n\left(1-\frac z{a_n}\right)
\]
converges uniformly on compact subsets to an entire function with zero set
$(a_n)$.
:::

<1>3. The critical exponent of the zero sequence $(a_n)$ is exactly $1$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>1, the series
\[
\sum_n|a_n|^{-1}
\]
converges, so the critical exponent is at most $1$.

Now fix $0<s<1$. Since logarithms grow more slowly than every positive
power, for all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
(\log(n+1))^{2s}\le n^{(1-s)/2}.
\]
Hence
\[
a_n^s
=n^s(\log(n+1))^{2s}
\le n^{(1+s)/2},
\]
and therefore
\[
\frac1{a_n^s}\ge n^{-(1+s)/2}.
\]
Because $(1+s)/2<1$, the series on the right diverges. Thus
\[
\sum_n|a_n|^{-s}=\infty
\]
for every $s<1$. The critical exponent is therefore $1$.
:::

<1>4. The entire function $f$ has order exactly $1$.
::: {.proof}
McMullen's Theorem 3.13 states that the canonical product associated to a
zero sequence has order equal to the critical exponent of that sequence.
By <1>3,
\[
\rho(f)=\alpha(a_n)=1.
\]
Thus the genus-zero product above is an explicit example of exact order one.
:::
:::
