::: {.problem}
Let $\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a discrete subgroup.
Prove that
\[
\Lambda\cong\mathbb Z^a
\]
for some $a\le n$, and that $a=n$ if and only if $\mathbb R^n/\Lambda$ is compact.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Let
\[
V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}(\Lambda),
\qquad
a=\dim_{\mathbb R}V\le n.
\]
Choose $v_1,\ldots,v_a\in\Lambda$ which form a real basis of $V$, and set
\[
L=\mathbb Zv_1+\cdots+\mathbb Zv_a\subseteq\Lambda.
\]
Then $L\cong\mathbb Z^a$.
::: {.proof}
Because the $v_i$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb R$, they are also linearly independent over $\mathbb Z$. Hence the homomorphism
\[
\mathbb Z^a\longrightarrow L,
\qquad
(m_1,\ldots,m_a)\longmapsto\sum_i m_iv_i
\]
is an isomorphism.
:::

<1>2. The subgroup $L$ has finite index in $\Lambda$.
::: {.proof}
Consider the compact parallelepiped
\[
P=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^a t_iv_i:0\le t_i\le1\right\}\subset V.
\]
Every $\lambda\in\Lambda$ can be written $\lambda=\sum_i x_iv_i$. Subtracting
\[
\sum_i\lfloor x_i\rfloor v_i\in L
\]
produces a representative of the coset $\lambda+L$ lying in $P\cap\Lambda$.

The set $P\cap\Lambda$ is finite. Indeed, if it were infinite, compactness of $P$ would give distinct $\lambda_j\in P\cap\Lambda$ with a convergent subsequence. Then
\[
\lambda_j-\lambda_k\longrightarrow0.
\]
Since $\Lambda$ is discrete, some neighborhood of $0$ contains no nonzero element of $\Lambda$, so for sufficiently large $j,k$ one would have $\lambda_j-\lambda_k=0$, a contradiction. Thus only finitely many cosets of $L$ occur in $\Lambda$, i.e.
\[
[\Lambda:L]<\infty.
\]
:::

<1>3. One has
\[
\Lambda\cong\mathbb Z^a.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>2, $\Lambda$ is finitely generated: adjoining finitely many representatives for $\Lambda/L$ to the basis $v_1,\ldots,v_a$ generates it. It is torsion-free because it is a subgroup of the additive group $\mathbb R^n$. Hence the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups gives
\[
\Lambda\cong\mathbb Z^r
\]
for some $r$.

Since $L\subseteq\Lambda$ has finite index, tensoring the inclusion with $\mathbb Q$ gives an isomorphism
\[
L\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Q
\cong
\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Q.
\]
The left side has dimension $a$ over $\mathbb Q$, while the right side has dimension $r$. Therefore $r=a$.
:::

<1>4. If $a=n$, then $\mathbb R^n/\Lambda$ is compact.
::: {.proof}
When $a=n$, the subgroup $L$ from <1>1 is a full-rank lattice in $\mathbb R^n$. Its compact parallelepiped $P$ maps surjectively onto $\mathbb R^n/L$, so $\mathbb R^n/L$ is compact. Since $L\subseteq\Lambda$, the natural map
\[
\mathbb R^n/L\longrightarrow\mathbb R^n/\Lambda
\]
is continuous and surjective. Therefore $\mathbb R^n/\Lambda$ is compact.
:::

<1>5. If $\mathbb R^n/\Lambda$ is compact, then $a=n$.
::: {.proof}
Because $\Lambda\subseteq V$, there is a well-defined continuous surjection
\[
\mathbb R^n/\Lambda\longrightarrow\mathbb R^n/V,
\qquad
x+\Lambda\longmapsto x+V.
\]
If $a<n$, then
\[
\mathbb R^n/V\cong\mathbb R^{n-a}
\]
is noncompact. It cannot be the continuous image of the compact space $\mathbb R^n/\Lambda$. Hence $a=n$.
:::
