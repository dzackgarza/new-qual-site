::: {.problem}
Let $U\subset\mathbb C$ be simply connected, with
\[
0\in U,
\qquad
1\notin U.
\]

(a) Suppose the conformal radius of $(U,0)$ is $2$.
Is $U$ uniquely determined?

(b) What if the conformal radius is $4$?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) No. For example, both
\[
U_1=\{w:\operatorname{Re}w<1\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
U_2=\mathbb C\setminus[1/2,\infty)
\]
contain $0$, omit $1$, and have conformal radius $2$ at $0$.

(b) Yes. If the conformal radius is $4$, then necessarily
\[
\boxed{U=\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)}.
\]

<1>1. Let $R$ be the conformal radius of $(U,0)$. Then $R\le4$; if $R=4$, the normalized Riemann map is forced to be the Koebe extremal.
::: {.proof}
Choose the Riemann map
\[
F:(\Delta,0)\longrightarrow(U,0)
\]
with $F'(0)=R>0$, and put
\[
f(z)=\frac{F(z)}R=z+a_2z^2+\cdots.
\]
Then $f\in\mathcal S$. Since $1\notin U$, the point
\[
p=\frac1R
\]
is omitted by $f$.

McMullen's coefficient argument in the proof of the Koebe one-quarter theorem applies to any omitted point $p$: the map
\[
A_p(w)=\frac{w}{1-w/p}
\]
satisfies $A_p(0)=0$, $A_p'(0)=1$, and $A_p\circ f\in\mathcal S$. Its second coefficient is
\[
a_2+\frac1p=a_2+R.
\]
The elementary coefficient bound $|a_2|\le2$ therefore gives
\[
|a_2|\le2,
\qquad
|a_2+R|\le2.
\]
Consequently
\[
R=|(a_2+R)-a_2|
\le |a_2+R|+|a_2|
\le4.
\]

If $R=4$, the closed disks
\[
\{|a_2|\le2\},
\qquad
\{|a_2+4|\le2\}
\]
are tangent, so necessarily
\[
a_2=-2.
\]
We now identify the equality case. Define the odd univalent function
\[
h(z)=\sqrt{f(z^2)}
=z+\frac{a_2}{2}z^3+\cdots
=z-z^3+\cdots,
\]
where the branch is chosen with $h'(0)=1$. Its exterior transform
\[
H(\zeta)=\frac1{h(1/\zeta)}
=\zeta+\frac1\zeta+\frac{b_2}{\zeta^2}+\cdots
\]
lies in $\Sigma$. By the area theorem,
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}n|b_n|^2\le1.
\]
Here $b_1=-a_2/2=1$, so equality is already exhausted by the first term and hence
\[
b_n=0\qquad(n\ge2).
\]
Thus
\[
H(\zeta)=\zeta+\frac1\zeta.
\]
Replacing $\zeta$ by $1/z$ gives
\[
h(z)=\frac{z}{1+z^2},
\]
and squaring yields
\[
f(t)=\frac{t}{(1+t)^2}
\qquad(t\in\Delta).
\]
Therefore the Riemann map itself is forced to be
\[
F(t)=\frac{4t}{(1+t)^2}.
\]

For completeness, the square root above is well-defined analytically: since $f$ is univalent and $f(0)=0$, the function $f(w)/w$ is holomorphic and zero-free on $\Delta$, hence has a holomorphic square root. Thus
\[
h(z)=z\sqrt{\frac{f(z^2)}{z^2}}.
\]
It is univalent as well. Indeed, $h(z_1)=h(z_2)$ implies $f(z_1^2)=f(z_2^2)$, hence $z_1^2=z_2^2$. If $z_2=-z_1$, oddness gives $h(z_1)=-h(z_1)$, hence $h(z_1)=0$, and then $z_1=0$ because $f$ has no zero except at $0$. Thus $z_1=z_2$.
:::

<1>2. The map
\[
k(z)=\frac{z}{(1+z)^2}
\]
is a conformal bijection
\[
\Delta\longrightarrow\mathbb C\setminus[1/4,\infty).
\]
::: {.proof}
If $k(z_1)=k(z_2)$, then
\[
z_1(1+z_2)^2=z_2(1+z_1)^2,
\]
so
\[
(z_1-z_2)(1-z_1z_2)=0.
\]
For $z_1,z_2\in\Delta$ one has $|z_1z_2|<1$, hence $z_1=z_2$; thus $k$ is univalent.

For $z=e^{i\theta}\ne-1$,
\[
k(e^{i\theta})
=\frac{1}{4\cos^2(\theta/2)}
\in[1/4,\infty).
\]
It remains to identify the image. Solving $k(z)=w$ gives
\[
wz^2+(2w-1)z+w=0.
\]
For $w\ne0$, the two roots have product $1$. A root lies on $S^1$ exactly when $w=k(e^{i\theta})\in[1/4,\infty)$. Hence, if
\[
w\notin[1/4,\infty),
\]
neither root lies on $S^1$, and because their moduli have product $1$, exactly one root lies in $\Delta$. Conversely, for $w\in[1/4,\infty)$ both roots lie on $S^1$, so there is no root in $\Delta$. The case $w=0$ has the unique solution $z=0$. Therefore
\[
 k(\Delta)=\mathbb C\setminus[1/4,\infty).
\]
:::

<1>3. The two radius-$2$ domains in part (a) are distinct and satisfy all hypotheses.
::: {.proof}
For
\[
U_1=\{w:\operatorname{Re}w<1\},
\]
the map
\[
F_1(z)=\frac{2z}{1+z}
\]
is conformal from $\Delta$ onto $U_1$: its inverse is $z=w/(2-w)$, and
\[
\left|\frac{w}{2-w}\right|<1
\iff \operatorname{Re}w<1.
\]
Since $F_1(0)=0$ and $F_1'(0)=2$, the conformal radius is $2$.

By <1>2,
\[
F_2(z)=2k(z)=\frac{2z}{(1+z)^2}
\]
maps $\Delta$ conformally onto
\[
U_2=\mathbb C\setminus[1/2,\infty).
\]
Again $F_2(0)=0$ and $F_2'(0)=2$. Both domains omit $1$, but $U_1\ne U_2$, so radius $2$ does not determine $U$.
:::

<1>4. Radius $4$ determines the domain uniquely.
::: {.proof}
By <1>1, the normalized Riemann map must be
\[
F(z)=4k(z)=\frac{4z}{(1+z)^2}.
\]
By <1>2 its image is
\[
4\bigl(\mathbb C\setminus[1/4,\infty)\bigr)
=\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty).
\]
Hence this is the only possible $U$ when the conformal radius equals $4$.
:::
