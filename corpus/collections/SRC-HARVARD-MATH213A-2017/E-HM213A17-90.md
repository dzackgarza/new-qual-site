::: {.problem}
Give an example of an entire function of order $1/3$.
:::

::: {.solution}
An explicit example is
\[
\boxed{
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left(1-\frac{z}{n^3}\right).}
\]

<1>1. The displayed infinite product defines an entire function.
::: {.proof}
The proposed zero sequence is
\[
a_n=n^3.
\]
Since
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{|a_n|}
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^3}<\infty,
\]
the genus-zero canonical product
\[
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac z{a_n}\right)
\]
converges uniformly on compact subsets of $\mathbb C$. Hence it defines an
entire function whose zeros are exactly the points $n^3$, all simple.
:::

<1>2. The critical exponent of the zero sequence $a_n=n^3$ is $1/3$.
::: {.proof}
For $s>0$,
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{|a_n|^s}
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^{3s}}.
\]
This series converges exactly when $3s>1$, i.e. when $s>1/3$, and diverges
when $s\le1/3$. Therefore
\[
\alpha(a_n)=\frac13.
\]
:::

<1>3. The entire function $f$ has order exactly $1/3$.
::: {.proof}
McMullen's Theorem 3.13 states that the canonical product associated to a
zero sequence has order equal to the critical exponent of that sequence.
Applying it to $a_n=n^3$ and using <1>2 gives
\[
\rho(f)=\alpha(a_n)=\frac13.
\]
Thus the displayed product is an entire function of the required order.
:::
:::
