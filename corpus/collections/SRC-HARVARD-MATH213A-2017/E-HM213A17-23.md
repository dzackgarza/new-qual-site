::: {.problem}
Let $p(n)$ be the number of partitions of $n$ into unequal parts.

(a) Show that
\[
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+z^n)
\]
defines an analytic function on the unit disk $\Delta$.

(b) Show that
\[
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)z^n.
\]

(c) Show that $f$ cannot be analytically continued beyond the unit disk: if $g$ is analytic on a connected domain $U\supset\Delta$ and $g=f$ on $\Delta$, then $U=\Delta$.
:::

::: {.solution}
We use the convention $p(0)=1$, corresponding to the empty partition.

<1>1. The product
\[
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+z^n)
\]
converges locally uniformly on $\Delta$ and defines an analytic function
there.
::: {.proof}
Fix $0<\rho<1$. On $|z|\le\rho$ we have
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|z|^n
\le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\rho^n<\infty.
\]
Choose $N_0$ so that $\rho^n\le1/2$ for $n\ge N_0$. For $|w|\le1/2$,
the branch of $\log(1+w)$ with value $0$ at $w=0$ satisfies
\[
|\log(1+w)|\le 2|w|.
\]
Hence
\[
\sum_{n=N_0}^{\infty}\log(1+z^n)
\]
converges uniformly on $|z|\le\rho$ by the Weierstrass $M$-test. Therefore
\[
\prod_{n=N_0}^{\infty}(1+z^n)
=\exp\!\left(\sum_{n=N_0}^{\infty}\log(1+z^n)\right)
\]
is analytic there. Multiplying by the finite product over $n<N_0$ proves
that $f$ is analytic on every compact subdisk, hence on $\Delta$.
:::

<1>2. For every $z\in\Delta$,
\[
f(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p(m)z^m.
\]
::: {.proof}
For a finite product,
\[
\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1+z^n)
=\sum_{A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}}z^{\sum_{a\in A}a}.
\]
Choosing the term $z^n$ from a factor means choosing the part $n$, and
each factor can be chosen at most once. Thus the coefficient of $z^m$ is
the number of partitions of $m$ into unequal parts whose parts are at most
$N$.

For fixed $m$, this coefficient stabilizes to $p(m)$ once $N\ge m$.
Moreover, for $|z|<1$ the sum over all finite subsets is absolutely
convergent, since
\[
\sum_{A\subset\mathbb N\,\text{finite}}|z|^{\sum_{a\in A}a}
=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+|z|^n)<\infty
\]
by <1>1 applied to the positive real number $|z|$. We may therefore group
terms according to their exponent and obtain
\[
f(z)
=\sum_{A\subset\mathbb N\,\text{finite}}z^{\sum_{a\in A}a}
=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p(m)z^m.
\]
:::

<1>3. If $\zeta$ is a root of unity of odd order $q$, then
\[
|f(r\zeta)|\longrightarrow\infty
\qquad (r\to1^-).
\]
::: {.proof}
Since the product in <1>1 has no zeros in $\Delta$, choose the analytic
logarithm determined by $\log f(0)=0$. Expanding $\log(1+w)$ and using
absolute convergence for $|z|<1$ gives
\[
\log f(z)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log(1+z^n)
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\frac{z^k}{1-z^k}.
\]
Write $r=e^{-s}$ with $s>0$ and set $z=r\zeta$. Split this series into
terms with $q\mid k$ and terms with $q\nmid k$.

For $k=q\ell$, since $q$ is odd and $\zeta^q=1$, the divisible part is
\[
M(s)
=\frac1q\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{\ell}\frac1{e^{q\ell s}-1}.
\]
Furthermore
\[
0<\frac{s}{e^{q\ell s}-1}\le\frac1{q\ell}.
\]
Dominated convergence therefore yields
\[
\lim_{s\to0^+}sM(s)
=\frac1{q^2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}rac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{\ell^2}
=\frac{\pi^2}{12q^2}>0.
\]

For the remaining terms, the finitely many numbers
$\zeta^j$, $1\le j<q$, stay a positive distance from the radial segment
ending at $1$. Hence there is a constant $c_q>0$ such that
\[
|1-r^k\zeta^k|\ge c_q
\qquad(q\nmid k,\ 0<r<1).
\]
Thus the nondivisible part $N(s)$ satisfies
\[
|N(s)|
\le \frac1{c_q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^k}{k}
=\frac1{c_q}\log\frac1{1-r}
=O(\log(1/s)).
\]
Consequently $sN(s)\to0$. We conclude that
\[
\operatorname{Re}\log f(r\zeta)
=\frac{\pi^2}{12q^2s}+o(1/s)\longrightarrow+\infty,
\]
and hence $|f(r\zeta)|\to\infty$.
:::

<1>4. Roots of unity of odd order are dense in $S^1$.
::: {.proof}
They are the points $e^{2\pi i a/q}$ with $q$ odd. Rationals with odd
denominator are dense in $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$: given any real number and
any $\varepsilon>0$, choose an odd $q$ so large that $1/q<\varepsilon$
and then choose an integer $a$ with $|a/q-x|\le1/(2q)$. Passing through
$t\mapsto e^{2\pi it}$ proves the claim.
:::

<1>5. The unit circle is a natural boundary for $f$. In particular, if
$g$ is analytic on a connected domain $U\supset\Delta$ and $g=f$ on
$\Delta$, then $U=\Delta$.
::: {.proof}
Suppose instead that $U$ properly contains $\Delta$. Since an open
connected subset of $\mathbb C$ is path connected, there is a path in $U$
from a point of $\Delta$ to a point outside $\overline\Delta$. The path
meets $S^1$ at some point $\xi\in U$. Because $U$ is open, some disk
$D(\xi,\varepsilon)$ lies in $U$; its intersection with $S^1$ contains an
open arc.

By <1>4 choose an odd-order root of unity $\zeta$ in that arc. Then
$\zeta\in U$, so analyticity of $g$ makes $g$ bounded on a sufficiently
small closed disk about $\zeta$. But for $r<1$ sufficiently close to $1$,
$r\zeta$ lies in that disk and also in $\Delta$, hence
\[
g(r\zeta)=f(r\zeta).
\]
This contradicts <1>3, which says $|f(r\zeta)|\to\infty$. Therefore no
such proper $U$ exists, and $U=\Delta$.
:::
