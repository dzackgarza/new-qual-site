::: {.problem}
Let
\[
S=\{z=x+iy:|x|<1,\ y>0\}.
\]
Give an explicit conformal map from $S$ to the upper half-plane $\mathbb H$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Take
\[
\boxed{F(z)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{2}\right)}.
\]

<1>1. The map $F$ sends $S$ into $\mathbb H$.
::: {.proof}
Write $z=x+iy$ with $|x|<1$ and $y>0$. Using
\[
\sin(a+ib)=\sin a\cosh b+i\cos a\sinh b,
\]
we obtain
\[
\operatorname{Im}F(z)
=\cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{2}\right)
\sinh\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}\right).
\]
Because $|x|<1$,
\[
\cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{2}\right)>0,
\]
and because $y>0$ the hyperbolic sine factor is also positive. Hence
$\operatorname{Im}F(z)>0$.
:::

<1>2. The derivative of $F$ never vanishes on $S$.
::: {.proof}
We have
\[
F'(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi z}{2}\right).
\]
The zeros of $\cos(\pi z/2)$ are the odd integers. They lie on the real
axis, and none lies in $S$. Thus $F'(z)\neq0$ throughout $S$.
:::

<1>3. The map $F$ is injective on $S$.
::: {.proof}
Suppose $F(z_1)=F(z_2)$ and put
\[
u_j=\frac{\pi z_j}{2}.
\]
Then
\[
\sin u_1=\sin u_2.
\]
Hence either
\[
u_1-u_2=2\pi k
\]
or
\[
u_1+u_2=(2k+1)\pi
\]
for some $k\in\mathbb Z$. Since
\[
-\frac\pi2<\operatorname{Re}u_j<\frac\pi2,
\]
the first equation forces $k=0$, hence $u_1=u_2$. The second equation is
impossible because $\operatorname{Im}u_1,\operatorname{Im}u_2>0$, whereas its
right-hand side is real. Therefore $z_1=z_2$.
:::

<1>4. The map $F:S\to\mathbb H$ is proper.
::: {.proof}
For $z=x+iy$,
\[
|F(z)|^2
=\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi x}{2}\right)
+\sinh^2\left(\frac{\pi y}{2}\right).
\]
Thus $|F(z)|\to\infty$ as $y\to\infty$.

Now let $K\subset\mathbb H$ be compact. Then there are constants
$M<\infty$ and $\delta>0$ such that
\[
|w|\le M,
\qquad
\operatorname{Im}w\ge\delta
\]
for all $w\in K$. The preceding formula bounds $y$ on $F^{-1}(K)$. Also,
if a sequence in $F^{-1}(K)$ approached either the bottom edge $y=0$ or one
of the vertical edges $x=\pm1$, then the formula in <1>1 would give
\[
\operatorname{Im}F(z)\longrightarrow0,
\]
contradicting the lower bound $\delta$. Hence $F^{-1}(K)$ stays a positive
distance from every boundary component of $S$ and is bounded. Being closed,
it is compact.
:::

<1>5. The map $F$ is a conformal homeomorphism from $S$ onto
$\mathbb H$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>4, $F$ is a nonconstant proper holomorphic map between connected
Riemann surfaces, so it is surjective. By <1>3 it is injective, and by <1>2
its derivative never vanishes. Therefore $F$ is an analytic homeomorphism
with analytic inverse, hence a conformal map from $S$ to $\mathbb H$.
:::
