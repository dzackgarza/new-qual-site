::: {.problem}
(a) Estimate $\log 2$ by evaluating
\[
S=\sum_{n=0}^5 a_n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
E=\sum_{n=0}^5 b_n(1/2)^n,
\]
where
\[
f(x)=\log(1+x)=\sum a_nx^n
\]
and
\[
g(y)=f\!\left(\frac{y}{1-y}\right)=\sum b_ny^n.
\]
How do these approximations compare to $\log 2$?

(b) What is the radius of convergence of the power series for $g(y)$?
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The first five nonzero coefficients of $f(x)=\log(1+x)$ are
\[
a_n=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}
\qquad(1\le n\le5),
\]
with $a_0=0$. Hence
\[
S=1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15
=\frac{47}{60}
\approx0.783333.
\]
::: {.proof}
For $|x|<1$,
\[
\log(1+x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}x^n.
\]
Substituting $x=1$ in the first five terms gives the displayed value of $S$.
:::

<1>2. The transformed function is
\[
g(y)=-\log(1-y)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^n}{n},
\]
so $b_0=0$ and $b_n=1/n$ for $n\ge1$. Therefore
\[
E
=\frac12+\frac18+\frac1{24}+\frac1{64}+\frac1{160}
=\frac{661}{960}
\approx0.688542.
\]
::: {.proof}
Directly,
\[
g(y)
=f\!\left(\frac{y}{1-y}\right)
=\log\!\left(1+\frac{y}{1-y}\right)
=\log\!\left(\frac1{1-y}\right)
=-\log(1-y).
\]
Its Taylor expansion at $0$ is therefore $\sum_{n\ge1}y^n/n$. Evaluating its degree-five truncation at $y=1/2$ gives the displayed value of $E$.
:::

<1>3. The transformed approximation is much more accurate:
\[
S-\log2\approx0.090186,
\qquad
\log2-E\approx0.004606.
\]
In particular $S$ is an overestimate, while $E$ is an underestimate.
::: {.proof}
The alternating harmonic series gives
\[
\log2=1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15-\cdots.
\]
Its odd partial sums decrease to $\log2$, so $S>\log2$. On the other hand,
\[
\log2=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n2^n},
\]
whose terms are positive, so $E<\log2$. Using $\log2\approx0.693147$ gives the stated numerical errors.
:::

<1>4. The radius of convergence of the power series for $g$ is
\[
\boxed{1}.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $b_n=1/n$,
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|b_n|^{1/n}
=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1/n}
=1.
\]
The Cauchy-Hadamard formula therefore gives radius $R=1$. Equivalently, $g(y)=-\log(1-y)$ has its nearest singularity to $0$ at $y=1$.
:::
