::: {.problem}
A subgroup $L$ of $\QQ^n$ is called a lattice if it is finitely generated and its $\QQ$-span is $\QQ^n$.

(a) Prove that for every lattice $L$ of $\QQ^n$, there exists $x \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$ such that $L = x\ZZ^n$.

(b) Suppose $L_1, \ldots, L_m$ are lattices in $\QQ^n$.
Prove that $L_1 + \cdots + L_m$ is also a lattice in $\QQ^n$.

(c) Prove that for every finite subgroup $G$ of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$, there exists $x \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$ such that $xGx^{-1} \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\ZZ)$.
(Hint.
Think about a lattice which is $G$-invariant.)
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step

Every lattice $L\le\QQ^n$ has the form $L=x\ZZ^n$ for some $x\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$.

::: pf-proof

The additive group $L$ is finitely generated and torsion-free, because it is a subgroup of the torsion-free group $\QQ^n$.
By the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups, $L$ is free abelian of some finite rank $r$.
Choose a $\ZZ$-basis $v_1,\ldots,v_r$ of $L$.

Tensoring the inclusion $L\subseteq\QQ^n$ with $\QQ$, or equivalently taking rational linear combinations of this basis, shows that
$$
\operatorname{span}_{\QQ}(L)
=\operatorname{span}_{\QQ}\{v_1,\ldots,v_r\}.
$$
The vectors $v_i$ are $\QQ$-linearly independent: a rational relation can be cleared of denominators to give an integral relation, which must be trivial because the $v_i$ are a $\ZZ$-basis.
Since the rational span is all of $\QQ^n$, it follows that $r=n$ and that $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ form a $\QQ$-basis of $\QQ^n$.

Let $x$ be the matrix whose columns are $v_1,\ldots,v_n$.
Its entries are rational and its columns are a basis, so $x\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$.
Moreover,
$$
x\ZZ^n
=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i:a_i\in\ZZ\right\}
=L.
$$
This proves part (a).

:::

:::

::: pf-step

A finite sum of lattices in $\QQ^n$ is again a lattice.

::: pf-proof

For each $i$, choose a finite generating set $S_i$ for $L_i$.
Then
$$
L_1+\cdots+L_m
$$
is generated as an abelian group by the finite set $S_1\cup\cdots\cup S_m$.
It is therefore finitely generated.

Its rational span is $\QQ^n$, because it contains $L_1$ and $L_1$ already has rational span $\QQ^n$.
Thus $L_1+\cdots+L_m$ is a lattice, proving part (b).

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Every finite subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$ preserves a lattice.

::: pf-proof

Let $G\le\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$ be finite and define
$$
L=\sum_{g\in G}g\ZZ^n.
$$
For each $g\in G$, the subgroup $g\ZZ^n$ is a lattice: it is generated by the columns of $g$, and those columns form a $\QQ$-basis of $\QQ^n$.
Because $G$ is finite, part (b) shows that $L$ is a lattice.

For every $h\in G$,
$$
hL
=\sum_{g\in G}hg\ZZ^n
=\sum_{k\in G}k\ZZ^n
=L,
$$
since left multiplication by $h$ permutes the elements of $G$.
Thus $L$ is $G$-invariant.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The invariant lattice gives the required integral conjugate of $G$.

::: pf-proof

By part (a), write
$$
L=y\ZZ^n
$$
for some $y\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$.
For each $g\in G$, invariance of $L$ gives
$$
g(y\ZZ^n)=y\ZZ^n,
$$
and hence
$$
(y^{-1}gy)\ZZ^n=\ZZ^n.
$$
A rational invertible matrix carrying $\ZZ^n$ onto itself lies in $\mathrm{GL}_n(\ZZ)$: its columns are integral, and the same argument applied to its inverse shows that its inverse has integral entries.
Therefore
$$
y^{-1}Gy\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_n(\ZZ).
$$
Taking $x=y^{-1}\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\QQ)$ yields
$$
xGx^{-1}\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_n(\ZZ),
$$
which proves part (c).

:::
