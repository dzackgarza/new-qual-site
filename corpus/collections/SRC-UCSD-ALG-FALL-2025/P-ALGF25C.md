::: problem
A subgroup $L$ of $\mathbb{Q}^n$ is called a lattice if it is finitely generated and its $\mathbb{Q}$-span is $\mathbb{Q}^n$.

(a) Prove that for every lattice $L$ of $\mathbb{Q}^n$, there exists $x \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Q})$ such that $L = x\mathbb{Z}^n$.

(b) Suppose $L_1, \ldots, L_m$ are lattices in $\mathbb{Q}^n$.
Prove that $L_1 + \cdots + L_m$ is also a lattice in $\mathbb{Q}^n$.

(c) Prove that for every finite subgroup $G$ of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Q})$, there exists $x \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Q})$ such that $xGx^{-1} \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$.
(Hint.
Think about a lattice which is $G$-invariant.)
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Every lattice $L\le\mathbb Q^n$ has the form $L=x\mathbb Z^n$ for some $x\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Q)$.
::: {.proof}
The additive group $L$ is finitely generated and torsion-free, because it is a subgroup of the torsion-free group $\mathbb Q^n$.
By the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups, $L$ is free abelian of some finite rank $r$.
Choose a $\mathbb Z$-basis $v_1,\ldots,v_r$ of $L$.

Tensoring the inclusion $L\subseteq\mathbb Q^n$ with $\mathbb Q$, or equivalently taking rational linear combinations of this basis, shows that
\[
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}(L)
=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{v_1,\ldots,v_r\}.
\]
The vectors $v_i$ are $\mathbb Q$-linearly independent: a rational relation can be cleared of denominators to give an integral relation, which must be trivial because the $v_i$ are a $\mathbb Z$-basis.
Since the rational span is all of $\mathbb Q^n$, it follows that $r=n$ and that $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ form a $\mathbb Q$-basis of $\mathbb Q^n$.

Let $x$ be the matrix whose columns are $v_1,\ldots,v_n$.
Its entries are rational and its columns are a basis, so $x\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Q)$.
Moreover,
\[
x\mathbb Z^n
=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i:a_i\in\mathbb Z\right\}
=L.
\]
This proves part (a).
:::

<1>2. A finite sum of lattices in $\mathbb Q^n$ is again a lattice.
::: {.proof}
For each $i$, choose a finite generating set $S_i$ for $L_i$.
Then
\[
L_1+\cdots+L_m
\]
is generated as an abelian group by the finite set $S_1\cup\cdots\cup S_m$.
It is therefore finitely generated.

Its rational span is $\mathbb Q^n$, because it contains $L_1$ and $L_1$ already has rational span $\mathbb Q^n$.
Thus $L_1+\cdots+L_m$ is a lattice, proving part (b).
:::

<1>3. Every finite subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Q)$ preserves a lattice.
::: {.proof}
Let $G\le\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Q)$ be finite and define
\[
L=\sum_{g\in G}g\mathbb Z^n.
\]
For each $g\in G$, the subgroup $g\mathbb Z^n$ is a lattice: it is generated by the columns of $g$, and those columns form a $\mathbb Q$-basis of $\mathbb Q^n$.
Because $G$ is finite, part (b) shows that $L$ is a lattice.

For every $h\in G$,
\[
hL
=\sum_{g\in G}hg\mathbb Z^n
=\sum_{k\in G}k\mathbb Z^n
=L,
\]
since left multiplication by $h$ permutes the elements of $G$.
Thus $L$ is $G$-invariant.
:::

<1>4. The invariant lattice gives the required integral conjugate of $G$.
::: {.proof}
By part (a), write
\[
L=y\mathbb Z^n
\]
for some $y\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Q)$.
For each $g\in G$, invariance of $L$ gives
\[
g(y\mathbb Z^n)=y\mathbb Z^n,
\]
and hence
\[
(y^{-1}gy)\mathbb Z^n=\mathbb Z^n.
\]
A rational invertible matrix carrying $\mathbb Z^n$ onto itself lies in $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Z)$: its columns are integral, and the same argument applied to its inverse shows that its inverse has integral entries.
Therefore
\[
y^{-1}Gy\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Z).
\]
Taking $x=y^{-1}\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Q)$ yields
\[
xGx^{-1}\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Z),
\]
which proves part (c).
:::
