::: problem
Give an example of a topological space that is connected but not path-connected.
Make sure you prove that your example satisfies the property.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Let
\[
G=
\left\{
\left(x,\sin\frac1x\right):0<x\le1
\right\}
\subseteq\mathbb R^2
\]
and define
\[
X=G\cup\{(0,0)\}.
\]
Then $G$ is connected.
::: {.proof}
The interval $(0,1]$ is connected, and the map
\[
(0,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb R^2,
\qquad
x\longmapsto\left(x,\sin\frac1x\right)
\]
is continuous.
Since a continuous image of a connected space is connected, $G$ is connected.
:::

<1>2. The point $(0,0)$ lies in $\overline G$, and therefore $X$ is connected.
::: {.proof}
For every positive integer $k$,
\[
\left(\frac1{k\pi},0\right)\in G,
\]
and these points converge to $(0,0)$.
Hence $(0,0)\in\overline G$.

To verify connectedness directly, suppose that
\[
X=U\cup V
\]
were a separation of $X$ into disjoint nonempty open subsets in the subspace topology.
Because $G$ is connected, it must lie entirely in one member of the separation; after relabeling, say $G\subseteq U$.
Then the remaining point $(0,0)$ lies in $V$.
But $V$ is an open neighborhood of $(0,0)$ in $X$, while $(0,0)\in\overline G$, so $V\cap G\ne\varnothing$.
This contradicts $G\subseteq U$ and $U\cap V=\varnothing$.
Thus $X$ is connected.
:::

<1>3. There is no path in $X$ from $(0,0)$ to
\[
q=\left(\frac1\pi,0\right)\in G.
\]
::: {.proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that
\[
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t)):[0,1]\to X
\]
is continuous with
\[
\gamma(0)=(0,0),
\qquad
\gamma(1)=q.
\]
The set
\[
Z=\{t\in[0,1]:x(t)=0\}
\]
is nonempty and closed, hence compact.
Let
\[
t_0=\max Z.
\]
Since $x(1)=1/\pi>0$, one has $t_0<1$.
By maximality of $t_0$,
\[
x(t)>0
\qquad(t_0<t\le1).
\]
Every point of $X$ with positive first coordinate lies in $G$, so
\[
y(t)=\sin\frac1{x(t)}
\qquad(t_0<t\le1).
\]

Choose any sufficiently large integer $m$ for which $t_0+1/m\le1$.
Because $x(t_0+1/m)>0$, choose numbers
\[
0<a_m,b_m<x(t_0+1/m)
\]
so small that
\[
\sin\frac1{a_m}=1,
\qquad
\sin\frac1{b_m}=-1.
\]
Such choices exist because the values $1$ and $-1$ occur for reciprocals of arbitrarily large numbers of the forms
\[
\frac\pi2+2\pi k,
\qquad
\frac{3\pi}2+2\pi k.
\]
Since
\[
x(t_0)=0
\]
and $x$ is continuous, the intermediate value theorem gives
\[
r_m,s_m\in(t_0,t_0+1/m)
\]
with
\[
x(r_m)=a_m,
\qquad
x(s_m)=b_m.
\]
Therefore
\[
y(r_m)=1,
\qquad
y(s_m)=-1.
\]
But
\[
r_m\to t_0,
\qquad
s_m\to t_0,
\]
so continuity of $y$ at $t_0$ would force both sequences $y(r_m)$ and $y(s_m)$ to converge to the same value $y(t_0)$.
This is impossible.
Hence no such path exists.
:::

<1>4. Thus $X$ is connected but not path-connected.
::: {.proof}
Connectedness is <1>2, and <1>3 exhibits two points of $X$ that cannot be joined by a path.
:::
