::: {.problem}
Find the least-squares solution of
$$
x+y=1,
\qquad
x-y=2,
\qquad
2x+y=1.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step

Write the system as $Au=b$ with
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&-1\\
2&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
u=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
b=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The three rows of $A$ are exactly the coefficient pairs in the three displayed equations, and the entries of $b$ are their right-hand sides.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The normal equations are
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
6&2\\
2&3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}5\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Direct multiplication gives
$$
A^TA=
\begin{pmatrix}6&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A^Tb=
\begin{pmatrix}5\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A least-squares solution satisfies $A^T(Au-b)=0$, which is precisely the displayed system.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The unique least-squares solution is
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=\left(\frac{15}{14},-\frac57\right).}
$$


::: pf-proof

The determinant of the normal matrix is
$$
6\cdot3-2\cdot2=14\ne0,
$$
so the solution is unique. Its inverse is
$$
(A^TA)^{-1}
=\frac1{14}
\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\-2&6\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
=(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb
=\frac1{14}
\begin{pmatrix}15\\-10\end{pmatrix},
$$
which is the displayed pair.

:::

Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} gives the unique least-squares minimizer.
