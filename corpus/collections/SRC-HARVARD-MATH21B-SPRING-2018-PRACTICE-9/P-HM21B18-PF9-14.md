::: {.problem}
Use the Fourier expansion
$$
x=\sum_{n=1}^\infty 2(-1)^{n+1}\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}
\qquad(-\pi\le x\le\pi)
$$
to verify
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}.
$$

(a) Write the Fourier-coefficient formula that gives the displayed sine coefficients and explain why no cosine coefficients occur.

(b) Integrate the series term-by-term to obtain a cosine series for $g(x)=x^2/2$ and determine its zeroth Fourier coefficient.

(c) Compute $\norm{g}^2$ and use Parseval's identity to deduce the value of $\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-4}$.
:::

::: {.solution}

Use the orthonormal Fourier basis
$$
\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad \cos(nx),\qquad \sin(nx)\quad(n\ge1)
$$
for the inner product
$$
\langle h,k\rangle=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}h(x)k(x)\,dx.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

For $f(x)=x$, every cosine coefficient is zero and
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx
=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n.
$$


::: pf-proof

The function $f$ is odd, whereas $1/\sqrt2$ and every $\cos(nx)$ are even. Hence their products with $f$ are odd, so the corresponding Fourier coefficients vanish. Since $x\sin(nx)$ is even,
$$
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin(nx)\,dx
=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx.
$$
Integration by parts gives
$$
\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx
=\left[-\frac{x\cos(nx)}n+\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi
=-\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n},
$$
which yields the displayed coefficient formula.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

Term-by-term integration gives
$$
g(x)=\frac{x^2}{2}
=\frac{a_0}{\sqrt2}
+\sum_{n=1}^\infty 2(-1)^n\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2},
\qquad
\boxed{a_0=\frac{\pi^2}{3\sqrt2}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

By the term-by-term integration theorem for Fourier series, integrating the expansion from step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} introduces only an undetermined constant and changes each sine term to
$$
2(-1)^n\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}.
$$
In the chosen orthonormal basis the constant term is $a_0/\sqrt2$, where
$$
a_0
=\left\langle g,\frac1{\sqrt2}\right\rangle
=\frac1{\pi\sqrt2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{x^2}{2}\,dx
=\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt2}\int_0^\pi\frac{x^2}{2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi^2}{3\sqrt2}.
$$
Thus the integrated series has exactly the stated constant term.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The norm and the required zeta value are
$$
\boxed{\norm{g}^2=\frac{\pi^4}{10}},
\qquad
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Directly from the definition of the norm,
$$
\norm{g}^2
=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(\frac{x^2}{2}\right)^2\,dx
=\frac{2}{4\pi}\int_0^\pi x^4\,dx
=\frac{\pi^4}{10}.
$$
The cosine coefficients in step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} are $2(-1)^n/n^2$. Parseval's identity therefore gives
$$
\frac{\pi^4}{10}
=a_0^2+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left(2(-1)^n\frac1{n^2}\right)^2
=\frac{\pi^4}{18}+4\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}.
$$
Hence
$$
4\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}
=\pi^4\left(\frac1{10}-\frac1{18}\right)
=\frac{2\pi^4}{45},
$$
and division by $4$ yields the stated value.

:::
