::: {.problem}
Let $\Omega$ be a star-shaped domain of $\CC$.
Let $\{f_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be a sequence of injective analytic functions on $\Omega$ that converges uniformly on every compact subset of $\Omega$ to a function $f$.
Prove that:

(a) $f$ is analytic on $\Omega$, and

(b) $f$ is either injective or a constant.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step

(a) Since $f_n\to f$ uniformly on compact subsets, the locally uniform limit of holomorphic functions is holomorphic, so $f$ is analytic on $\Omega$.

::: pf-proof

Each $f_n$ is analytic on the open set $\Omega$, and the convergence is uniform on every compact subset of $\Omega$; that is precisely the hypothesis of the locally uniform limit theorem for holomorphic functions, whose conclusion is that the limit is holomorphic on $\Omega$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(a) For a triangle $T\Subset\Omega$,
$$
\int_{\partial T}f(z)\,dz
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\partial T}f_n(z)\,dz=0,
$$
so Morera's theorem gives holomorphicity.

::: pf-proof

$\partial T$ is compact and lies in $\Omega$, so $f_n\to f$ uniformly on it; uniform convergence on a curve permits the interchange of limit and integral, since $\abs{\int(f_n-f)}\le\ell(\partial T)\sup_{\partial T}\abs{f_n-f}$. Each $\int_{\partial T}f_n=0$ because $f_n$ is holomorphic on the simply connected region bounded by $T$ (Cauchy's theorem for a triangle). Hence the integral of $f$ over every triangle in $\Omega$ vanishes, and Morera's theorem gives that $f$ is holomorphic.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) Assume $f$ is not constant and suppose $f(a)=f(b)$ for distinct $a,b\in\Omega$. Then
$$
g(z)=f(z)-f(a)
$$
is holomorphic and not constant, and its zeros are isolated.

::: pf-proof

$g$ is holomorphic because $f$ is, by part (a), and it is not constant because $f$ is not. The zeros of a holomorphic function that is not identically zero are isolated: if they accumulated at a point of $\Omega$, the identity theorem would force $g$ to vanish on a neighbourhood and then, $\Omega$ being connected (star-shaped), on all of $\Omega$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) Choose a closed disk $D$ around $b$ whose closure lies in $\Omega$, which does not contain $a$, and such that $b$ is the only zero of $g$ in $D$.

::: pf-proof

$\Omega$ is open, so a small closed disk about $b$ lies in $\Omega$ and excludes $a$; shrinking it if necessary, and using that the zeros of $g$ are isolated, it contains no zero of $g$ other than $b$. On the boundary circle $\partial D$ then $g\ne0$, which is what the zero-counting theorem below needs.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) With $g_n(z)=f_n(z)-f_n(a)$, one has $g_n\to g$ uniformly on $D$, so by Hurwitz's theorem $g_n$ has a zero $b_n$ in $D$ for all sufficiently large $n$.

::: pf-proof

$f_n\to f$ uniformly on the compact set $D$, and $f_n(a)\to f(a)$ because $a\in\Omega$; so $g_n\to g$ uniformly on $D$. $g$ has exactly one zero in $D$, at $b$, and none on $\partial D$; Hurwitz's theorem in its zero-counting form then says that for all large $n$ the function $g_n$ has, counted with multiplicity, as many zeros in $D$ as $g$, hence at least one.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) But $g_n(b_n)=0$ means $f_n(b_n)=f_n(a)$, and injectivity of $f_n$ forces $b_n=a$, impossible because $a\notin D$. Hence $f$ is injective.

::: pf-proof

$b_n\in D\subseteq\Omega$ and $a\in\Omega$ both lie in the domain of $f_n$, and $f_n(b_n)=f_n(a)$ with $f_n$ injective gives $b_n=a$; but $a\notin D$ while $b_n\in D$. So the supposition $f(a)=f(b)$ with $a\ne b$ is impossible for a nonconstant limit, and $f$ is injective.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) Therefore the limit is either injective or constant, and both parts are proved.

::: pf-proof

If $f$ is constant there is nothing to show; if it is not, step 6 shows it is injective.

:::
