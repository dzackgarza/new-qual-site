::: {.problem}
Let

$$
g ( x ) = \sum _ { n = 0 } ^ { \infty } { \frac { 1 } { 1 + n ^ { 2 } x } } .
$$

(a) Prove that the series defining g does not converge uniformly on $( 0 , \infty )$

(b) Prove that g is however a continuous function on $( 0 , \infty )$

(c) Is g differentiable?
If so, is the derivative function $g ^ { \prime }$ continuous?
:::

::: {.solution}
Write
$$
u_n(x)=\frac1{1+n^2x},
\qquad
x>0.
$$

<1>1. For every $n\geq1$,
$$
\sup_{x>0}u_n(x)=1.
$$

::: {.proof}
For every $x>0$,
$$
0<u_n(x)<1.
$$
As $x\to0^+$,
$$
u_n(x)
=
\frac1{1+n^2x}
\longrightarrow1.
$$
Thus the supremum over $(0,\infty)$ is $1$.
:::

<1>2. The sequence $(u_n)$ does not converge uniformly to $0$ on
$(0,\infty)$.

::: {.proof}
Uniform convergence to $0$ would require
$$
\sup_{x>0}|u_n(x)|
\longrightarrow0.
$$
But step <1>1 shows that this supremum equals $1$ for every $n\geq1$.
:::

<1>3. The series defining $g$ does not converge uniformly on
$(0,\infty)$.

::: {.proof}
If
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_n(x)
$$
converged uniformly, then its terms $u_n$ would converge uniformly to $0$.
This contradicts step <1>2.
:::

<1>4. Part (a) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>3.
:::

<1>5. Fix $a>0$. For every $x\geq a$ and every $n\geq1$,
$$
0<u_n(x)
\leq
\frac1{n^2a}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since $x\geq a$,
$$
1+n^2x
\geq
n^2a.
$$
Taking reciprocals gives the inequality.
:::

<1>6. The series defining $g$ converges uniformly on every interval
$[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$.

::: {.proof}
The $n=0$ term is the constant function $1$. For $n\geq1$, step <1>5 gives
the uniform bound
$$
|u_n(x)|
\leq
\frac1{a n^2}.
$$
The numerical series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{a n^2}
$$
converges, so the Weierstrass $M$-test gives uniform convergence.
:::

<1>7. The function $g$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.

::: {.proof}
Fix $x_0>0$ and choose
$$
a=\frac{x_0}{2}.
$$
By step <1>6, the series converges uniformly on $[a,\infty)$, which contains
a neighborhood of $x_0$. Every summand is continuous there, so the uniform
limit is continuous at $x_0$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $g$ is continuous
on $(0,\infty)$.
:::

<1>8. Part (b) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>7.
:::

<1>9. For $n\geq1$,
$$
u_n'(x)
=
-\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Differentiate $(1+n^2x)^{-1}$ by the chain rule.
:::

<1>10. Fix $a>0$. For every $x\geq a$ and every $n\geq1$,
$$
|u_n'(x)|
\leq
\frac1{a^2n^2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since
$$
1+n^2x
\geq
n^2a,
$$
one has
$$
|u_n'(x)|
=
\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}
\leq
\frac{n^2}{n^4a^2}
=
\frac1{a^2n^2}.
$$
:::

<1>11. The derivative series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_n'(x)
$$
converges uniformly on every interval $[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>10 gives the uniform majorant
$$
\frac1{a^2n^2},
$$
and the corresponding numerical series converges. Apply the Weierstrass
$M$-test.
:::

<1>12. The function $g$ is differentiable on $(0,\infty)$, with
$$
\boxed{
g'(x)
=
-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Fix $x_0>0$ and choose $a=x_0/2$. On $[a,\infty)$, the original series
converges at every point by step <1>6, and the derivative series converges
uniformly by step <1>11. Hence the standard theorem on termwise
differentiation applies on this interval. In particular, $g$ is differentiable
at $x_0$ with the displayed derivative. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, the formula
holds throughout $(0,\infty)$.
:::

<1>13. The derivative $g'$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.

::: {.proof}
Fix $x_0>0$ and again choose $a=x_0/2$. Each derivative term is continuous on
$[a,\infty)$, and step <1>11 gives uniform convergence of their series.
Therefore $g'$ is continuous at $x_0$ as a uniform limit of continuous
functions. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $g'$ is continuous on the whole domain.
:::

<1>14. Part (c) follows.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>12 proves differentiability and step <1>13 proves continuity of the
derivative.
:::

<1>15. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>4, <1>8, and <1>14 establish parts (a), (b), and (c), respectively.
:::
