::: {.problem}
Include brief justification for your answers to parts (b) and (c).

(a) Complete the definition: $f:\RR^n\to\RR^m$ is (real) differentiable at a point $a\in\RR^n$ if there is a linear transformation $\ldots$

(b) Give an example of a function $g:\RR^2\to\RR$ whose first order partial derivatives exist everywhere, such that $g$ is not differentiable at $(0,0)$.

(c) Give an example of a function $h:\RR^2\to\RR^2$ which is differentiable everywhere, but not complex differentiable anywhere.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

For part (a), $f:\RR^n\to\RR^m$ is differentiable at
$a\in\RR^n$ if there is a linear map
$L:\RR^n\to\RR^m$ such that
$$
\lim_{x\to a}
\frac{\norm{f(x)-f(a)-L(x-a)}}{\norm{x-a}}
=0.
$$


::: pf-proof

This is the definition of Fréchet differentiability in finite-dimensional
real normed spaces. The linear map is necessarily unique and is denoted
$Df(a)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

For part (b), define
$$
g(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^3}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\
0,&(x,y)=(0,0).
\end{cases}
$$
Then both first partial derivatives exist everywhere, but $g$ is not
differentiable at $(0,0)$.


::: pf-proof

Away from the origin the function is rational with nonvanishing
denominator, hence smooth. At the origin,
$$
g_x(0,0)
=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{g(t,0)-g(0,0)}{t}
=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{t}{t}=1,
$$
whereas
$$
g_y(0,0)
=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{g(0,t)-g(0,0)}{t}
=0.
$$
Thus both first partials exist everywhere.

If $g$ were differentiable at the origin, its derivative would have to be
the linear map
$$
L(x,y)=x
$$
determined by these two partial derivatives. Along the line $y=x$,
$$
g(x,x)=\frac{x}{2},
$$
so
$$
\frac{\abs{g(x,x)-L(x,x)}}{\norm{(x,x)}}
=\frac{\abs{-x/2}}{\sqrt2\abs{x}}
=\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}
$$
for every $x\ne0$. This does not tend to zero. Hence the differentiability
criterion in step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} fails at the origin.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

For part (c), take
$$
h(x,y)=(x,-y).
$$
This map is real differentiable everywhere and complex differentiable
nowhere.


::: pf-proof

The map is real-linear, so it is differentiable at every point. Under
$\RR^2\cong\CC$ it is the map
$$
z\longmapsto\overline z.
$$
Writing $h=(u,v)$ with $u(x,y)=x$ and $v(x,y)=-y$, one has
$$
u_x=1,
\qquad
v_y=-1.
$$
The first Cauchy--Riemann equation $u_x=v_y$ fails at every point.
Therefore $h$ is complex differentiable nowhere.

:::
