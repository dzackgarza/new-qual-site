::: problem
Include brief justification for your answers to parts (b) and (c).

(a) Complete the definition: $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ is (real) differentiable at a point $a\in\mathbb{R}^n$ if there is a linear transformation $\ldots$

(b) Give an example of a function $g:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ whose first order partial derivatives exist everywhere, such that $g$ is not differentiable at $(0,0)$.

(c) Give an example of a function $h:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ which is differentiable everywhere, but not complex differentiable anywhere.
:::

::: solution
(a) The map $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if there is a linear map
$L:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ such that
\[
\lim_{x\to a}\frac{\|f(x)-f(a)-L(x-a)\|}{\|x-a\|}=0.
\]
The map $L$, necessarily unique, is $Df(a)$.

(b) Define
\[
g(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^3}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\
0,&(x,y)=(0,0).
\end{cases}
\]
Away from the origin it is smooth. At the origin both partial derivatives
exist: $g_x(0,0)=1$ and $g_y(0,0)=0$. If $g$ were differentiable there, its
derivative would be $L(x,y)=x$. But along $y=x$,
\[
g(x,x)-L(x,x)=\frac{x}{2}-x=-\frac{x}{2},
\]
whose ratio to $\sqrt{2}|x|$ does not tend to $0$. Thus $g$ is not
differentiable at the origin.

(c) Take
\[
h(x,y)=(x,-y),
\]
which corresponds to the complex map $z\mapsto\bar z$. It is real-linear,
hence differentiable everywhere. Its real components $u=x$, $v=-y$ satisfy
$u_x=1$ and $v_y=-1$, so the Cauchy--Riemann equations fail at every point.
Thus it is nowhere complex differentiable.
:::
