::: problem
(a) What is a principal ideal domain?

(b) Consider the four rings $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2)$, $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-4)$, and $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$.
Which of these rings are principal ideal domains, and which are not?
Explain.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. A principal ideal domain is a commutative ring $R$ with
$1\ne0$, no zero divisors, and every ideal generated by one element:
for each ideal $I$ there is $a\in R$ such that $I=(a)=aR$.
The zero ideal is allowed and is generated by zero [@DF04].

<1>2. The ring $\mathbb Q[x]$ is a principal ideal domain.

::: proof
The product of two nonzero polynomials is nonzero, because its
leading coefficient is the product of two nonzero rational numbers.
Thus $\mathbb Q[x]$ is an integral domain.

Let $I$ be a nonzero ideal, and choose a nonzero $f\in I$ of
smallest degree. For any $g\in I$, polynomial division over the
field $\mathbb Q$ gives $g=qf+r$, with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg f$.
Since $r=g-qf\in I$, the latter alternative contradicts the
choice of $f$. Therefore every $g\in I$ belongs to $(f)$, while
$(f)\subseteq I$ because $f\in I$. Hence $I=(f)$.
Together with $(0)$, this accounts for every ideal.
:::

<1>3. The ring $\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2-2)$ is a field and hence a
principal ideal domain.

::: proof
The quadratic $f=x^2-2$ has no rational root. Indeed, if
$(a/b)^2=2$ for relatively prime integers $a,b$ with $b\ne0$,
then $a^2=2b^2$ makes $a$ even, and substitution then makes $b$
even, a contradiction. A reducible quadratic over a field has a
linear factor, hence a root, so $f$ is irreducible.

For any nonzero residue class $\bar g$ modulo $(f)$, the polynomial
$f$ does not divide $g$. Irreducibility gives $\gcd(f,g)=1$.
The Euclidean algorithm in $\mathbb Q[x]$ supplies polynomials
$a,b$ with $ag+bf=1$, so $\bar a\bar g=1$. Thus every nonzero
element of the quotient is invertible. The quotient is nonzero
because a polynomial of positive degree cannot divide $1$.
It is therefore a field. A field has only the ideals $(0)$ and
$(1)$: any ideal containing a nonzero element contains its product
with its inverse, namely $1$.
:::

<1>4. The ring $\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2-4)$ is not a principal ideal domain.

::: proof
The residue classes of $x-2$ and $x+2$ are nonzero: neither
degree-one polynomial is a multiple of $x^2-4$. Their product
is zero, since $(x-2)(x+2)=x^2-4$. The quotient has zero divisors
and is not an integral domain, so it cannot be a principal ideal
domain.
:::

<1>5. The ring $\mathbb Q[x,y]$ is not a principal ideal domain.

::: proof
Consider $I=(x,y)$. Evaluation at $(0,0)$ sends every element of
$I$ to zero but sends $1$ to $1$, so $I$ is proper.

Suppose $I=(f)$. Since $x\in I$, there is a nonzero polynomial
$g$ with $fg=x$. Regard this identity as one in $\mathbb Q[x][y]$.
The coefficient ring $\mathbb Q[x]$ is a domain by step <1>2,
so degrees in $y$ add for nonzero products. The right side has
$y$-degree zero, hence $f,g\in\mathbb Q[x]$.
Their $x$-degrees add to one. If $f$ has degree zero, it is a
nonzero rational unit, contradicting that $I$ is proper.
Otherwise $g$ is a nonzero rational constant, and $f=cx$ for
some $c\in\mathbb Q^\times$.

But $y\in(f)$ would then give $y=cxh$ for a polynomial $h$.
Setting $x=0$ yields the false identity $y=0$ in $\mathbb Q[y]$.
Thus $I$ is not principal. This rules out the principal ideal
domain property.
:::
