::: problem
Let $F$ be a field of order 1024.

(a) Find the prime field of $F$.

(b) Let $K$ be a subfield of $F$.
Find all possible orders for $K$.
:::

::: solution
The prime field is $\mathbb F_2$. The possible subfield orders are
$$
\boxed{2,\quad4,\quad32,\quad1024},
$$
with exactly one subfield of each order.

<1>1. The characteristic is $2$ and $[F:\mathbb F_2]=10$.

::: proof
The additive order of $1$ is a positive divisor of $1024$.
It is greater than $1$, since a field has $1\ne0$, and is prime:
a composite characteristic $ab$ with $1<a,b<ab$ would give
nonzero elements $a\cdot1,b\cdot1$ with product zero.
The only prime divisor of $1024=2^{10}$ is $2$.
The subfield generated by $1$ is therefore $\{0,1\}\cong\mathbb F_2$.
An $n$-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb F_2$ has $2^n$
elements, so $[F:\mathbb F_2]=10$.
:::

<1>2. Any subfield has one of the four displayed orders.

::: proof
Every subfield $K$ contains $\mathbb F_2$. Put
$d=[K:\mathbb F_2]$ and $e=[F:K]$. These are finite positive
integers, and the tower law gives $de=10$. Thus
$d\in\{1,2,5,10\}$, and $|K|=2^d$ belongs to the displayed list.
:::

<1>3. Each possible order occurs.

::: proof
Fix a positive divisor $d$ of $10$ and put $q=2^d$.
The polynomial $X^q-X$ divides $X^{1024}-X$ in $\mathbb F_2[X]$.
Indeed, in the quotient by $(X^q-X)$ the residue class $x$ satisfies
$x^q=x$. Induction gives $x^{q^j}=x$ for every $j\geq1$;
taking $j=10/d$ proves the divisibility.

Every nonzero $a\in F$ satisfies $a^{1023}=1$, by Lagrange's
theorem in $F^\times$ [@DF04]. Including zero, all $1024$
elements of $F$ are roots of $X^{1024}-X$, so
$$
X^{1024}-X=\prod_{a\in F}(X-a).
$$
A monic divisor of this product is itself a product of distinct
linear factors in $F[X]$, by unique factorization for polynomials
over a field [@DF04]. Consequently $X^q-X$ has exactly $q$
distinct roots in $F$.

Let $K_d=\{a\in F:a^q=a\}$. In characteristic $2$, iterating
$(a+b)^2=a^2+b^2$ gives $(a+b)^q=a^q+b^q$.
Thus $K_d$ is closed under addition; additive inverses coincide
with the original elements. It is closed under multiplication
because $(ab)^q=a^qb^q$, and under nonzero inversion because
$(a^{-1})^q=(a^q)^{-1}$. It contains $0,1$, so it is a subfield,
and the root count gives $|K_d|=q=2^d$.
:::

<1>4. There is no second subfield of any listed order.

::: proof
If $K\subseteq F$ is a subfield of order $2^d$, then Lagrange's
theorem in $K^\times$ gives $a^{2^d}=a$ for each nonzero $a\in K$,
and the equation holds at zero as well. Hence $K\subseteq K_d$.
Both fields have $2^d$ elements, so they are equal.
:::
:::
