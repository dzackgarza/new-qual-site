::: {.problem}
a. List all isomorphism classes of groups of size 120.

b. Find all isomorphism classes of $\mathbf{Q}[x]$ modules $V$ such that $\dim_{\mathbf{Q}} V=6$ and $V$ is annihilated by $(x-3)^2(x+5)$.

c. Find all possible rational canonical forms for a 6 by 6 matrix over $\mathbf{Q}$ that has minimal polynomial $(x-3)^2(x+5)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The $47$ groups of order $120$ in part (a) are the $39$ semidirect products
specified in the table in step [](#s5){.pf-ref}, the five groups in step [](#s6){.pf-ref}, and the
three groups in step [](#s7){.pf-ref}.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}
An extension with finite abelian kernel $N$ and quotient $Q$ splits
when $\gcd(|N|,|Q|)=1$.

::: pf-proof
Write $N$ additively and choose representatives $s(q)$ with $s(1)=1$.
Conjugation defines an action of $Q$ on $N$, independent of the representatives
because $N$ is abelian. Put
$f(p,q)=s(p)s(q)s(pq)^{-1}\in N$. Associativity gives
$$
f(p,q)+f(pq,r)=p\cdot f(q,r)+f(p,qr).
$$
Let $S(p)=\sum_{r\in Q}f(p,r)$ and $m=|Q|$. Summing the identity over $r$
gives
$$
m f(p,q)=S(p)+p\cdot S(q)-S(pq).
$$
Multiplication by $m$ is an automorphism of $N$. Thus, with
$h(p)=m^{-1}S(p)$, replacing $s(p)$ by $(-h(p))s(p)$ makes its factor set
zero. The new representatives form a subgroup isomorphic to $Q$, so the
extension is $N\rtimes Q$.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}
A central extension of $A_4$ by $C_2$ is either $C_2\times A_4$ or
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_3)$. A central extension of $A_5$ by $C_2$ is
either $C_2\times A_5$ or $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_5)$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
We use the presentations
$$
T_m=\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3=(ab)^m=1\rangle,
\qquad T_3\cong A_4,\quad T_5\cong A_5.
$$
Coset enumeration gives the orders of the two presented groups.

```gap
F := FreeGroup("a", "b");;
a := F.1;; b := F.2;;
orders := List([3,5], m -> Size(F / [a^2,b^3,(a*b)^m]));;
if orders <> [12,60] then Error("unexpected triangle-group order"); fi;
Print(orders, "\n");
# [ 12, 60 ]
```

For $m=3$, the permutations $(12)(34)$ and $(123)$ generate $A_4$:
the conjugates of the first generate its Klein four subgroup, and the
second supplies the quotient of order $3$. For $m=5$, use $(12)(34)$ and
$(135)$. Their product has order $5$, so their subgroup has order divisible
by $2$, $3$, and $5$. Its order is therefore $30$ or $60$; order $30$ would
give a normal subgroup of index $2$ in the simple group $A_5$. Consequently
the subgroup is $A_5$. These surjections and the computed orders establish
the two presentations. We use the simplicity of $A_n$ for $n\geq5$ here
and below [@DF04].
:::

::: pf-step
Let $z$ generate the central kernel and lift $a,b$ to $\alpha,\beta$.
Since $3$ is odd, replacing $\beta$ by $z\beta$ when necessary makes
$\beta^3=1$. Since $m$ is odd, replacing $\alpha$ by $z\alpha$ when
necessary makes $(\alpha\beta)^m=1$. This replacement does not change
$\alpha^2$, which is either $1$ or $z$.

If $\alpha^2=1$, the subgroup generated by $\alpha,\beta$ is a quotient
of $T_m$ that surjects onto $T_m$, hence maps isomorphically to the quotient.
It is a complement to the central kernel, giving the direct product.
:::

::: pf-step
If $\alpha^2=z$, the extension is a quotient of
$$
\widetilde T_m=
\langle a,b,z\mid z^2=1,\ [z,a]=[z,b]=1,
 a^2=z,\ b^3=1,\ (ab)^m=1\rangle.
$$
Its central subgroup $\langle z\rangle$ has order at most $2$, and its
quotient is $T_m$. Thus $|\widetilde T_m|\leq 2|T_m|$.
For $p=m\in\{3,5\}$, the matrices
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{in }\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)
$$
satisfy $A^2=-I$, $B^3=I$, and
$AB=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ of order
$p$. They generate $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_p)$: powers of $AB$ give all
upper elementary matrices, conjugation by $A$ gives the lower ones, and
these perform Gaussian elimination. Diagonal matrices are also generated,
as witnessed, for $t\ne0$, by
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-t^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}A
=\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
There are $p^2-1$ choices for a nonzero first column and $p$ choices for
a second column having determinant $1$, so this matrix group has order
$p(p^2-1)=2|T_m|$. It is therefore isomorphic to $\widetilde T_m$ and to
the extension in this case.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}
Every group $H$ of order $24$ is solvable. If its Sylow $3$-subgroup
is not normal, then
$$
H\cong S_4,\qquad C_2\times A_4,\qquad
\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_3).
$$

::: pf-proof
If the Sylow $3$-subgroup is normal, step [](#s1){.pf-ref} gives
$H\cong C_3\rtimes P$ for a group $P$ of order $8$, so $H$ is solvable.
Otherwise there are four Sylow $3$-subgroups. Conjugation on them defines
$H\to S_4$ with kernel $K$ contained in a normalizer of order $6$.
If $3$ divided $|K|$, normality would put all four Sylow $3$-subgroups
inside $K$, requiring at least $1+4(3-1)=9$ elements. Thus $|K|=1$ or $2$.
In the first case $H\cong S_4$. In the second, the image is $A_4$, and
$K\cong C_2$ is central. Step [](#s2){.pf-ref} gives the other two possibilities.
The series $1\lhd V_4\lhd A_4\lhd S_4$ has abelian factors, and a central
extension of a solvable group is solvable, proving the assertion in all cases.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}
A group $G$ of order $120$ is solvable if and only if its Sylow
$5$-subgroup is normal. In that case $G\cong C_5\rtimes H$ with $|H|=24$.

::: pf-proof
We first prove by induction that every solvable group of order dividing
$120$ and divisible by $5$ has a normal Sylow $5$-subgroup. A minimal
nontrivial normal subgroup $N$ of a finite solvable group is elementary
abelian: its commutator subgroup must be trivial by minimality and
solvability, and its primary subgroups and subgroup of $p$-th powers are
characteristic. Here $N$ is therefore $C_5$, $C_3$, or $C_2^r$ with
$1\leq r\leq3$.

If $N=C_5$, the conclusion follows. Otherwise the induction hypothesis in
$G/N$ gives a normal subgroup $L/N$ of order $5$. A Sylow $5$-subgroup
$P$ of $L$ acts trivially on $N$, because
$$
|\operatorname{Aut}(C_3)|=2,\qquad
|\operatorname{GL}_r(\mathbf F_2)|\in\{1,6,168\}
$$
are not divisible by $5$. Hence $L=N\times P$, and $P$ is its unique
Sylow $5$-subgroup. It is characteristic in $L\lhd G$, so normal in $G$.
The displayed linear-group orders follow by choosing successive independent
columns; for example $(8-1)(8-2)(8-4)=168$.

For $|G|=120$, a normal Sylow $5$-subgroup is cyclic and step [](#s1){.pf-ref} splits
the extension. Its quotient of order $24$ is solvable by step [](#s3){.pf-ref}, so
$G$ is solvable as well.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}
Exactly $39$ isomorphism classes have a normal cyclic subgroup of
order $15$. They are the following explicitly specified semidirect products.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Put $N=C_3\times C_5=\langle c\rangle\times\langle d\rangle$.
Identify its automorphism group with the additive group
$\mathcal A=C_2\oplus C_4$ by letting $(i,j)$ act as
$$
c\longmapsto c^{(-1)^i},\qquad d\longmapsto d^{2^j}.
$$
Write $u=(1,0)$, $v=(0,1)$, and $t=2v=(0,2)$. Thus
$\mathcal A[2]=\{0,u,t,u+t\}$. For each row below form
$N\rtimes_\phi P$, with $\phi$ given on the displayed ordered generators.
Each choice in a set or each explicitly listed tuple is one group.

| $P$ and ordered generators | Values of $\phi$ | Number |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| $C_8=\langle r\rangle$ | $\phi(r)\in\{0,u,t,u+t,v,u+v\}$ | 6 |
| $C_4\times C_2=\langle r\rangle\times\langle s\rangle$ | $(\phi(r),\phi(s))=(R,0)$, $R\in\{0,u,t,u+t\}$ | 4 |
| Same $P$ | $(R,u)$, $R\in\{0,t\}$ | 2 |
| Same $P$ | $(R,t)$, $R\in\{0,u\}$ | 2 |
| Same $P$ | $(R,u+t)$, $R\in\{0,u\}$ | 2 |
| Same $P$ | $(v,0),(u+v,0),(v,u)$ | 3 |
| $C_2^3=\langle r,s,w\rangle$ | $(0,0,0)$; $(R,0,0)$ for $R\in\{u,t,u+t\}$; $(u,t,0)$ | 5 |
| $D_8=\langle r,s\mid r^4=s^2=1,\ srs^{-1}=r^{-1}\rangle$ | $(0,S)$, $S\in\{0,u,t,u+t\}$ | 4 |
| Same $P$ | $(u,S)$, $S\in\{0,t\}$ | 2 |
| Same $P$ | $(t,S)$, $S\in\{0,u\}$ | 2 |
| Same $P$ | $(u+t,S)$, $S\in\{0,u\}$ | 2 |
| $Q_8=\langle r,s\mid r^4=1,\ s^2=r^2,\ srs^{-1}=r^{-1}\rangle$ | $(0,0)$; $(R,0)$ for $R\in\{u,t,u+t\}$; $(u,t)$ | 5 |

The entries sum to $6+13+5+10+5=39$.
:::

::: pf-step
These are all possibilities for $P$. An abelian group of order $8$
is $C_8$, $C_4\times C_2$, or $C_2^3$, according as its exponent is $8$,
$4$, or $2$. In the exponent-$4$ case, choose $r$ of order $4$ and an
element outside $\langle r\rangle$; multiplying the latter by $r^{-1}$
when its square is $r^2$ produces an outside involution and hence the
direct product. A nonabelian group of order $8$ has an element $r$ of
order $4$, since a group in which every element squares to $1$ is abelian.
For $s\notin\langle r\rangle$, conjugation must invert $r$, and
$s^2\in\{1,r^2\}$. These give exactly the displayed presentations of
$D_8$ and $Q_8$.
:::

::: pf-step
A normal subgroup $N\cong C_{15}$ is precisely the set of all
odd-order elements of $G$: the quotient is a $2$-group, and every element
of $N$ has odd order. Thus $N$ is characteristic, and step [](#s1){.pf-ref} splits the
extension. Two actions on the same $P$ give isomorphic groups precisely
when they differ by precomposition with an automorphism of $P$.
Indeed an isomorphism induces automorphisms of $N$ and the quotient $P$;
the former conjugates the action, but $\operatorname{Aut}(N)$ is abelian,
so this conjugation does nothing. Conversely, a change of generators in
$P$ realizing the precomposition gives an isomorphism of semidirect
products. Different types of $P$ cannot give isomorphic quotients.
:::

::: pf-step
Each row of the table lists one representative of each
$\operatorname{Aut}(P)$-orbit of actions. For $C_8$, changing a generator multiplies its image
by an odd integer, hence identifies $R$ with $-R$; this gives its six
listed images. For $C_4\times C_2$, every automorphism has the form
$$
r\longmapsto r^{\varepsilon}s^e,\qquad
s\longmapsto r^{2f}s,
\quad \varepsilon\in\{1,3\},\ e,f\in\{0,1\}.
$$
Consequently $(R,S)$ changes to $(\varepsilon R+eS,2fR+S)$.
If $2R=0$, $S$ stays fixed and $R$ is taken modulo $\langle S\rangle$;
these are the first ten tuples for this $P$. If $R$ has order $4$, then
$2R=t$. When $S\in\{0,t\}$, one can set $S=0$ and reduce $R$ to either
$v$ or $u+v$. When $S\in\{u,u+t\}$, the same operations reduce the
pair to $(v,u)$. These are three further, distinct orbits.

For $C_2^3$, all actions are linear maps to $\mathcal A[2]$. Changing a
basis of the domain identifies two maps exactly when they have the same
image; there are the zero subspace, three lines, and the whole plane.
For $D_8$, the relations force $R,S\in\mathcal A[2]$. Its automorphisms
send $r$ to $r^{\pm1}$ and $s$ to $r^k s$, so $R$ stays fixed and $S$
is taken modulo $\langle R\rangle$, giving the ten listed pairs.
Finally, every homomorphism from $Q_8$ factors through its abelianization
$C_2^2$. Automorphisms permuting the three cyclic subgroups of order $4$
induce all of $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf F_2)$. Again the image subspace
classifies the action, giving five pairs. This proves both exhaustiveness
and irredundancy of the table.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}
The remaining solvable groups of order $120$ are exactly
$$
C_5\times\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_3),\quad
C_5\times S_4,\quad
C_5\rtimes_{\mathrm{sgn}}S_4,\quad
C_{10}\times A_4,\quad
D_{10}\times A_4.
$$
In the third group, even permutations act trivially on $C_5$ and odd
permutations act by inversion. Here $D_{10}$ has order $10$.

::: pf-proof
Write $G=C_5\rtimes H$ using step [](#s4){.pf-ref}. If $H$ has a normal Sylow
$3$-subgroup, its inverse image in $G$ has order $15$ and is cyclic:
the action of $C_3$ on $C_5$ is trivial because $3\nmid4$.
This is step [](#s5){.pf-ref}. Otherwise step [](#s3){.pf-ref} gives the three possibilities for
$H$. Their abelianizations are respectively $C_3$, $C_2$, and
$C_3\times C_2$ for $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_3)$, $S_4$, and
$A_4\times C_2$.

For the first assertion, abelianize the presentation in step [](#s2){.pf-ref} with
$m=3$: $a^4=b^3=(ab)^3=1$ implies $a^3=a^4=1$, hence $a=1$;
the quotient onto $A_4/V_4=C_3$ shows that the abelianization is exactly
$C_3$. Also $[S_4,S_4]=A_4$ and $[A_4,A_4]=V_4$: the respective
quotients are abelian, commutators of transpositions give $3$-cycles, and
a commutator of two $3$-cycles in $A_4$ is a nonidentity double
transposition whose conjugates generate $V_4$.

A homomorphism from these abelianizations to
$\operatorname{Aut}(C_5)=C_4$ is trivial in the first case and is either
trivial or the unique nontrivial map from the $C_2$ factor in each of the
other two cases. This gives exactly the five displayed groups. The
quotient by the unique Sylow $5$-subgroup distinguishes the three types
of $H$; within each type, a trivial action cannot become nontrivial
under an automorphism. None has a normal subgroup of order $15$, since
its image in $H$ would be a normal subgroup of order $3$.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s7}
The nonsolvable groups of order $120$ are exactly
$$
S_5,\qquad C_2\times A_5,\qquad
\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_5).
$$
Thus part (a) has $39+5+3=47$ isomorphism classes. Its abelian members
are $C_{120}$, $C_{60}\times C_2$, and $C_{30}\times C_2^2$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
The only nonabelian simple group whose order divides $120$ is $A_5$.
Here are the order exclusions. Prime-power orders have nontrivial center.
Orders $6,10,15,20,40$ have a normal Sylow $3$- or $5$-subgroup.
For orders $12$ and $24$, a nonnormal Sylow $3$-subgroup gives an action
on four such subgroups. A simple group would embed in $A_4$, which is
impossible for order $24$ and would identify the order-$12$ group with
the nonsimple group $A_4$. For order $30$, absence of normal Sylow
$3$- and $5$-subgroups would require $10\cdot2+6\cdot4=44$
nonidentity elements of orders $3$ and $5$.

For a simple group of order $60$, Sylow theory gives $n_5=6$ and
$n_3\in\{4,10\}$. The value $n_3=4$ would embed it in $A_4$, so
$n_3=10$. These account for $24+20=44$ nonidentity elements.
The possibilities for $n_2$ are $3,5,15$; $3$ is excluded by the
degree-$3$ conjugation action, and $5$ embeds the group in $A_5$, proving
the required isomorphism. If $n_2=15$ and the Sylow $2$-subgroups were
cyclic, their $30$ distinct generators of order $4$ would exceed the
$15$ remaining nonidentity elements. Thus they are Klein four groups.
For an involution $t$, its centralizer has order $4,12$, or $20$;
order $60$ is excluded by the trivial center. Order $20$ gives an
impossible faithful action of degree $3$, while order $12$ gives an
embedding in $A_5$. If all these centralizers have order $4$, each
involution belongs to a unique Sylow $2$-subgroup, so $15$ Klein four
subgroups require $45$ distinct involutions, again impossible.
This proves the order-$60$ assertion.

Finally, a simple group of order $120$ would have six Sylow
$5$-subgroups and hence embed in $A_6$. Its image would have index $3$.
The action of the simple group $A_6$ on those three cosets is nontrivial
but cannot be injective, since $360>6$, a contradiction. These cases
exhaust all divisors of $120$. In each use of simplicity, the nontrivial
permutation action is faithful and its composite with the sign
homomorphism is trivial, so the image lies in the alternating group.
:::

::: pf-step
A composition series of a nonsolvable group of order $120$ must
therefore have factors $A_5$ and $C_2$. The group has either a normal
subgroup $C_2$ with quotient $A_5$, or a normal subgroup $A_5$ with
quotient $C_2$. In the first case the kernel is central, so step [](#s2){.pf-ref}
applies.

In the second case, conjugation gives
$G\to\operatorname{Aut}(A_5)\cong S_5$. To justify the last isomorphism,
the five Sylow $2$-subgroups of $A_5$ are the Klein four groups fixing
each of the five letters. An automorphism permutes these groups.
If it fixes all five, then for each $g\in A_5$, $g$ and its image induce
the same permutation of them; the faithful conjugation action implies
that the automorphism fixes $g$. Hence $\operatorname{Aut}(A_5)$ embeds
in $S_5$. Conversely conjugation by $S_5$ induces all natural
permutations of these five groups, so embeds $S_5$ in the automorphism
group. This proves equality.

The kernel of $G\to\operatorname{Aut}(A_5)$ is the centralizer of its
normal $A_5$ and has trivial intersection with that subgroup. Its order
is therefore $1$ or $2$. In the first case $G\cong S_5$; in the second
the commuting subgroups give $G\cong A_5\times C_2$.
:::

::: pf-step
The three groups exist and have a section isomorphic to $A_5$, so
are nonsolvable. They are distinct: $S_5$ has trivial center, whereas
the other two have a central involution, and
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbf F_5)$ is perfect while $C_2\times A_5$ has
abelianization $C_2$. For perfectness, abelianize its presentation from
step [](#s2){.pf-ref}: $4a=0$, $3b=0$, and $5a+5b=0$ imply $a+2b=0$, then
$b=0$ and $a=0$; also $z=2a=0$.

Finally an abelian group of order $120$ is the product of its Sylow
subgroups; its odd part is $C_{15}$ and its $2$-part is one of the three
abelian groups of order $8$ from step [](#s5){.pf-ref}. This gives the stated three
abelian groups.
:::

::: {.pf-step #s8}
For part (b), put $R=\mathbf Q[x]$, $p=x-3$, and $q=x+5$.
The isomorphism classes are
$$
V_{a,b,c}=(R/(p^2))^a\oplus(R/(p))^b\oplus(R/(q))^c,
\qquad a,b,c\geq0,\quad 2a+b+c=6.
$$
There are $7+5+3+1=16$ classes, corresponding to $a=0,1,2,3$.

::: pf-proof
Let $T$ be multiplication by $x$. Since $p^2$ and $q$ are relatively
prime and $p(T)^2q(T)=0$, a Bezout identity decomposes
$$
V=\ker(T-3I)^2\oplus\ker(T+5I).
$$
Indeed, if $h p^2+kq=1$, then
$w=h(T)p(T)^2w+k(T)q(T)w$, with the first term in $\ker q(T)$ and the
second in $\ker p(T)^2$. Their intersection is zero by the same identity.
On the first summand
put $U=T-3I$, so $U^2=0$. Choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_a$ of
$\operatorname{im}U$, extend it by $f_1,\ldots,f_b$ to a basis of
$\ker U$, and choose $w_i$ with $Uw_i=e_i$.
The vectors $w_i,e_i,f_j$ form a basis: applying $U$ proves independence
of the $w_i$ modulo the kernel, and subtracting the appropriate $w_i$
from any vector puts it in the kernel. Each pair $w_i,e_i$ gives
$R/(p^2)$ and each $f_j$ gives $R/(p)$.
On the other summand $T=-5I$, giving $c$ copies of $R/(q)$.

The dimension condition is exactly $2a+b+c=6$. Conversely every displayed
module has that dimension and is annihilated by $p^2q$. The dimensions
of the two primary summands and the rank of $U$ recover $a,b,c$, so
distinct triples give nonisomorphic modules.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
For part (c), let $C(f)$ denote the companion matrix of a monic
polynomial $f$: it is the matrix of multiplication by $x$ on $R/(f)$
in the ordered basis $1,x,\ldots,x^{\deg f-1}$. The six possible
rational canonical forms are the block diagonal matrices with the
following ordered companion blocks.

| $(a,b,c)$ | Invariant factors, in divisibility order |
| --- | --- |
| $(1,0,4)$ | $q,\ q,\ q,\ p^2q$ |
| $(1,1,3)$ | $q,\ pq,\ p^2q$ |
| $(1,2,2)$ | $p,\ pq,\ p^2q$ |
| $(1,3,1)$ | $p,\ p,\ p,\ p^2q$ |
| $(2,0,2)$ | $p^2q,\ p^2q$ |
| $(2,1,1)$ | $p,\ p^2,\ p^2q$ |

::: pf-proof
In step [](#s8){.pf-ref} the minimal polynomial is exactly $p^2q$ precisely when
$a\geq1$ and $c\geq1$. The nonnegative solutions of $2a+b+c=6$ with
these inequalities are exactly the six triples displayed. For each,
combine the primary cyclic factors by
$R/(p^i)\oplus R/(q)\cong R/(p^iq)$, arranging the $p$-powers in
increasing order and pairing the $q$-factors with the rightmost ones.
This gives each listed divisibility chain. Its degrees sum to $6$ and
its largest factor is $p^2q$, so its companion blocks have the required
size and minimal polynomial. Distinct triples were proved nonisomorphic
in step [](#s8){.pf-ref}, and therefore give distinct similarity classes. Every
matrix in question yields one of those modules, proving completeness.
:::
