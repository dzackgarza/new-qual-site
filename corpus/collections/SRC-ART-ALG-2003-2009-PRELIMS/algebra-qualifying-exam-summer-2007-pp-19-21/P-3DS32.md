::: {.problem}
(a) List all abelian groups of size 90.
(b) List all $\RR[x]$ modules $M$ such that $\dim_{\RR} M=4$ and $M$ is annihilated by $x^4-1$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

Up to isomorphism, there are exactly two abelian groups of order $90$:
$$
\boxed{C_{90},\qquad C_3\oplus C_{30}}.
$$

::: pf-proof

Since
$$
90=2\cdot3^2\cdot5,
$$
the $2$-primary and $5$-primary parts are forced to be $C_2$ and $C_5$. The $3$-primary part is either
$$
C_9
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
C_3\oplus C_3.
$$
Thus the two groups are
$$
C_2\oplus C_9\oplus C_5\cong C_{90}
$$
and
$$
C_2\oplus C_3\oplus C_3\oplus C_5
\cong C_3\oplus C_{30}.
$$
The fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups shows that these are exhaustive and nonisomorphic.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

Over $\RR$,
$$
x^4-1=(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)
$$
is a product of three distinct irreducible factors.

::: pf-proof

The factorization is immediate from
$$
x^4-1=(x^2-1)(x^2+1)=(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1).
$$
The quadratic $x^2+1$ has no real root and is therefore irreducible over $\RR$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

Every finite-dimensional $\RR[x]$-module annihilated by $x^4-1$ is of the form
$$
M\cong
\bigl(\RR[x]/(x-1)\bigr)^{\oplus a}
\oplus
\bigl(\RR[x]/(x+1)\bigr)^{\oplus b}
\oplus
\bigl(\RR[x]/(x^2+1)\bigr)^{\oplus c}
$$
for uniquely determined integers $a,b,c\ge0$.

::: pf-proof

Because $M$ is finite-dimensional over $\RR$, it is finitely generated over the PID $\RR[x]$. The structure theorem gives a direct sum of cyclic primary modules
$$
\RR[x]/(p^e)
$$
for irreducible polynomials $p$.

Since $(x^4-1)M=0$, every elementary divisor $p^e$ divides $x^4-1$. By step [](#s2){.pf-ref}, $x^4-1$ is squarefree, so only the three first powers
$$
x-1,
\qquad
x+1,
\qquad
x^2+1
$$
can occur. Their multiplicities $a,b,c$ are uniquely determined by the structure theorem.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

The condition $\dim_{\RR}M=4$ is equivalent to
$$
a+b+2c=4.
$$

::: pf-proof

The real dimensions of the three simple summands in step [](#s3){.pf-ref} are respectively
$$
1,\qquad1,\qquad2.
$$
Dimensions add under direct sums, giving the displayed equation.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

Therefore the nine isomorphism classes in part (b) are obtained from
$$
\boxed{(a,b,c)\in
\{(4,0,0),(3,1,0),(2,2,0),(1,3,0),(0,4,0),
(2,0,1),(1,1,1),(0,2,1),(0,0,2)\}}.
$$

::: pf-proof

If $c=0$, step [](#s4){.pf-ref} gives $a+b=4$, yielding the five pairs
$$
(4,0),(3,1),(2,2),(1,3),(0,4).
$$
If $c=1$, then $a+b=2$, yielding three pairs. If $c=2$, then $a=b=0$. No larger $c$ is possible. These nine triples are distinct, and uniqueness in step [](#s3){.pf-ref} makes the corresponding modules pairwise nonisomorphic.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1){.pf-ref} answers part (a), and steps [](#s3){.pf-ref}, [](#s4){.pf-ref} and [](#s5){.pf-ref} give the complete classification in part (b).

:::
