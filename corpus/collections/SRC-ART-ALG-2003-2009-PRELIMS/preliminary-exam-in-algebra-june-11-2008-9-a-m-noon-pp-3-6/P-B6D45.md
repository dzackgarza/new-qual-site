::: {.problem}
a. State Sylow's Theorem.

b. Prove that no group of order 6545 is simple.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Sylow's theorem has the following form [@DF04]. If $H$ is finite
and $|H|=p^a m$ with $p$ prime and $p\nmid m$, then:

- There is a subgroup of order $p^a$, called a Sylow $p$-subgroup.
- Every $p$-subgroup lies in a Sylow $p$-subgroup, and all Sylow
  $p$-subgroups are conjugate.
- Their number $n_p$ satisfies $n_p\mid m$ and $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$.
  For any Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$, it is also
  $n_p=[H:N_H(P)]$.

<1>2. If $|G|=6545$, the number of Sylow $5$-subgroups is $1$ or $11$.

::: {.proof}
The factorization is $6545=5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot17$. Hence Sylow's theorem
requires $n_5\mid1309=7\cdot11\cdot17$ and $n_5\equiv1\pmod5$.
The positive divisors of $1309$ are
$$
1,\ 7,\ 11,\ 17,\ 77,\ 119,\ 187,\ 1309.
$$
Among these, only $1$ and $11$ are congruent to $1$ modulo $5$.
:::

<1>3. In either case, $G$ has a nontrivial proper normal subgroup.

::: {.proof}
If $n_5=1$, the unique Sylow $5$-subgroup is normal, and its order $5$
makes it nontrivial and proper.

If $n_5=11$, conjugation on the eleven Sylow $5$-subgroups defines
$\rho:G\to S_{11}$. The action is transitive by Sylow conjugacy and
has more than one point, so $\rho$ is nontrivial and $\ker\rho\ne G$.
It cannot be injective: otherwise Lagrange's theorem would give
$6545\mid11!$, whereas $17\mid6545$ and $17\nmid11!$.
Thus $1\ne\ker\rho\ne G$, and the kernel is normal.
In both cases $G$ is not simple.
:::
:::
