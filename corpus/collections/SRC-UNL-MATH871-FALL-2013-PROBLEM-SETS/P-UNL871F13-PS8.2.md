::: {.problem}
For each space below, identify a familiar space homeomorphic to the quotient and prove your answer.

1. Let $X=[0,1]\times[0,1]$, and let $\sim$ be the smallest equivalence relation such that
   $$
   (x,0)\sim(x,1),\qquad (0,y)\sim(1,y)
   $$
   for all $x,y\in[0,1]$.

2. Let $X=D_1\sqcup D_2$ be the disjoint union of two closed disks.
   Identify boundary points with the same Euclidean coordinates: for $(x,y)\in D_1$ and $(r,s)\in D_2$ with $x^2+y^2=1$, impose
   $$
   (x,y)\sim(r,s)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(x,y)=(r,s).
   $$

3. Let $X=D^2$ and collapse the entire boundary circle to one point; equivalently, let $\sim$ be the smallest equivalence relation satisfying
   $$
   (x,y)\sim(1,0)
   $$
   whenever $x^2+y^2=1$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step

For the square, define
$$
q_1:[0,1]^2\to S^1\times S^1
$$
by
$$
q_1(x,y)
=
\bigl(
e^{2\pi i x},
e^{2\pi i y}
\bigr).
$$


::: pf-proof

The map is continuous.
It satisfies
$$
q_1(x,0)
=
q_1(x,1)
$$
and
$$
q_1(0,y)
=
q_1(1,y),
$$
so it is constant on the prescribed equivalence classes.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Two points of the square have the same $q_1$-image exactly when they
are equivalent under the relation generated by the opposite-edge
identifications.


::: pf-proof

Suppose
$$
q_1(x_0,y_0)
=
q_1(x_1,y_1).
$$
Then
$$
e^{2\pi i x_0}
=
e^{2\pi i x_1},
\qquad
e^{2\pi i y_0}
=
e^{2\pi i y_1}.
$$
Since all coordinates lie in $[0,1]$, each equality means either the two
coordinates are equal or they are the pair
$$
0,1.
$$
Thus the points differ only by the stated opposite-edge identifications.

Conversely, each generating identification preserves $q_1$, so equivalent
points have equal images.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
[0,1]^2/{\sim}
\cong
S^1\times S^1=T^2.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

The map $q_1$ is surjective, so it induces a continuous bijection
$$
\bar q_1:
[0,1]^2/{\sim}
\to
S^1\times S^1.
$$
The quotient is compact because it is the continuous image of the compact
square under the quotient map.
The torus is Hausdorff.
Hence the continuous bijection $\bar q_1$ is a homeomorphism.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

For part (2), write
$$
D^2
=
\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}.
$$
Define
$$
\phi_+:
D_1\to S^2
$$
by
$$
\phi_+(x,y)
=
\left(
x,
y,
\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}
\right),
$$
and define
$$
\phi_-:
D_2\to S^2
$$
by
$$
\phi_-(x,y)
=
\left(
x,
y,
-\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}
\right).
$$


::: pf-proof

The first map identifies $D_1$ with the closed upper hemisphere, and the
second identifies $D_2$ with the closed lower hemisphere.
Both maps are continuous.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

On the boundary circle, the two maps agree:
$$
\phi_+(x,y)
=
\phi_-(x,y)
=
(x,y,0)
\qquad
(x^2+y^2=1).
$$


::: pf-proof

On the boundary,
$$
1-x^2-y^2=0.
$$
Hence the third coordinate in both formulas is zero.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The map
$$
q_2:D_1\sqcup D_2\to S^2
$$
defined by $\phi_+$ on $D_1$ and $\phi_-$ on $D_2$ has fibers exactly
the stated equivalence classes.


::: pf-proof

Interior points of $D_1$ map to the open upper hemisphere, while interior
points of $D_2$ map to the open lower hemisphere, so no interior point from
one disk is identified with a point from the other.

Each $\phi_\pm$ is injective on its own disk.
The only common image points occur on the equator, and step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} shows that
the two boundary points have the same image exactly when their Euclidean
coordinates are equal.
Thus the fibers are exactly the prescribed classes.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Consequently
$$
\boxed{
(D_1\sqcup D_2)/{\sim}
\cong
S^2.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

The map $q_2$ is continuous and surjective and factors through a continuous
bijection from the quotient onto $S^2$.

The disjoint union
$$
D_1\sqcup D_2
$$
is compact, so its quotient is compact.
The sphere is Hausdorff.
Hence the induced continuous bijection is a homeomorphism.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

For part (3), define
$$
q_3:D^2\to S^2
$$
in polar coordinates by
$$
\boxed{
q_3(r,\theta)
=
\bigl(
\sin(\pi r)\cos\theta,
\sin(\pi r)\sin\theta,
\cos(\pi r)
\bigr).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

At
$$
r=0,
$$
the formula gives the north pole
$$
(0,0,1),
$$
independent of $\theta$, so it is well defined at the origin.

At
$$
r=1,
$$
the formula gives the south pole
$$
(0,0,-1),
$$
again independent of $\theta$.
Thus the entire boundary circle is collapsed to one point.
The formula is continuous.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

On the open disk
$$
0\le r<1,
$$
the map $q_3$ is one-to-one onto
$$
S^2\setminus\{(0,0,-1)\}.
$$


::: pf-proof

For
$$
0<r<1,
$$
the polar angle $\theta$ is recovered from the first two coordinates, and
the third coordinate
$$
\cos(\pi r)
$$
determines $r$ uniquely because cosine is strictly decreasing on
$$
[0,\pi].
$$
At
$$
r=0,
$$
there is only the single center point of the disk.

Every sphere point except the south pole has polar angle
$$
\pi r
$$
for a unique
$$
0\le r<1,
$$
so the image is exactly the punctured sphere.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore the fibers of $q_3$ are exactly the equivalence classes
obtained by collapsing
$$
\partial D^2
$$
to one point.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}, every interior point is in a singleton fiber.
By step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}, every boundary point maps to the same south pole.
Thus the only nontrivial fiber is the whole boundary circle.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

Consequently
$$
\boxed{
D^2/\partial D^2
\cong
S^2.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

The map $q_3$ factors through a continuous bijection
$$
\bar q_3:
D^2/\partial D^2
\to
S^2.
$$
The quotient is compact and $S^2$ is Hausdorff.
Therefore $\bar q_3$ is a homeomorphism.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

The three quotient spaces are
$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c|c}
1&T^2\\
2&S^2\\
3&S^2.
\end{array}
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Collect steps [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}, and [](#s1-11){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} gives the requested identifications, with explicit quotient maps
constructed in the preceding steps.
