::: {.problem}
(Degree Rule) Let $D$ be an integral domain and $f(x), g(x) \in D[x]$. Prove that $\deg(f(x)g(x)) = \deg f(x) + \deg g(x)$. Show, by example, that for a commutative ring $R$ it is possible that $\deg f(x)g(x) < \deg f(x) + \deg g(x)$, where $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are nonzero elements in $R[x]$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The argument is the retained solution outline for this chapter, **item 19, PDF p. 2** (`assets/attachments/Ch16Sltns.pdf`), restructured into steps.

Throughout, $D$ is an integral domain, $f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}$ and $g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}x^{j}$ with $a_{n}\neq0$ and $b_{m}\neq0$, so that $\deg f=n$ and $\deg g=m$; coefficients outside the ranges shown are taken to be $0$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #coefficient}
The coefficient of $x^{k}$ in $f(x)g(x)$ is $c_{k}=\sum_{i+j=k}a_{i}b_{j}$, and $c_{k}=0$ for $k>n+m$.

::: pf-proof
Multiplying out, $f(x)g(x)=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}b_{j}x^{i+j}$, so the coefficient of $x^{k}$ is the sum of $a_{i}b_{j}$ over all pairs with $i+j=k$. If $k>n+m$ and $i+j=k$, then $i>n$ or $j>m$ (otherwise $i+j\le n+m$), so each summand has a zero factor; hence $c_{k}=0$ and $f(x)g(x)$ has degree at most $n+m$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #domain}
$a_{n}b_{m}\neq0$ because $D$ is an integral domain and $a_{n},b_{m}\neq0$.

::: pf-proof
An integral domain has no zero divisors, so a product of two nonzero elements is nonzero. Both $a_{n}$ and $b_{m}$ are nonzero by the choice of $n$ and $m$ as the degrees.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #top}
$c_{n+m}=a_{n}b_{m}$.

::: pf-proof
Among the pairs with $i+j=n+m$: if $i>n$ then $a_{i}=0$; if $i<n$ then $j=n+m-i>m$, so $b_{j}=0$. The only pair left is $i=n$, $j=m$, and its summand is $a_{n}b_{m}$, which is nonzero by step [](#domain){.pf-ref}. So the whole sum collapses to $a_{n}b_{m}$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #degree}
$\deg\big(f(x)g(x)\big)=n+m=\deg f+\deg g$.

::: pf-proof
By step [](#coefficient){.pf-ref} the product has no term of degree above $n+m$, and by steps [](#top){.pf-ref} and [](#domain){.pf-ref} its coefficient of $x^{n+m}$ is nonzero. So the highest degree with a nonzero coefficient is exactly $n+m$, as claimed.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #failure}
Over the commutative ring $\ZZ_{4}$ the rule fails: $f(x)=g(x)=2x+1$ are nonzero of degree $1$, while $f(x)g(x)=1$ has degree $0<1+1$.

::: pf-proof
In $\ZZ_{4}[x]$ the element $2x+1$ is nonzero and has degree $1$, since its coefficient $2$ of $x$ is nonzero and no higher power appears. Multiplying, $(2x+1)^{2}=4x^{2}+4x+1$, and $4=0$ in $\ZZ_{4}$, so $(2x+1)^{2}=1$, a nonzero constant of degree $0$. Hence $\deg(fg)=0<2=\deg f+\deg g$. The ring $\ZZ_{4}$ is commutative but not a domain, since $2\cdot2=0$ with $2\neq0$, and that is exactly what step [](#domain){.pf-ref} used: the product of the two leading coefficients $2\cdot2$ vanishes, so the coefficient of $x^{2}$ need not survive.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #conclusion}
The degree rule holds over every integral domain and can fail over a commutative ring, so the hypothesis "$D$ is an integral domain" is not removable.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#degree){.pf-ref} and [](#failure){.pf-ref} together give both halves of the exercise: the equality for $D$ an integral domain, and a commutative ring $R=\ZZ_{4}$ with nonzero $f,g\in R[x]$ and $\deg(fg)<\deg f+\deg g$.
:::
