::: {.problem}
Prove that if $f:X\to Y$ is continuous and surjective, $X$ is compact, and $Y$ is Hausdorff, then $f$ is a quotient map.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The map $f:X\to Y$ is closed.
::: {.proof}
Let $F\subseteq X$ be closed.
Since $X$ is compact, the closed subspace $F$ is compact.
Continuity of $f$ implies that $f(F)$ is compact in $Y$.
Because $Y$ is Hausdorff, every compact subset of $Y$ is closed.
Hence $f(F)$ is closed in $Y$.
Thus $f$ is a closed map.
:::

<1>2. For every subset $A\subseteq Y$,
\[
A\text{ is closed in }Y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f^{-1}(A)\text{ is closed in }X.
\]
::: {.proof}
If $A$ is closed in $Y$, then $f^{-1}(A)$ is closed in $X$ because $f$ is continuous.

Conversely, suppose $f^{-1}(A)$ is closed in $X$.
By <1>1,
\[
f\bigl(f^{-1}(A)\bigr)
\]
is closed in $Y$.
Since $f$ is surjective,
\[
f\bigl(f^{-1}(A)\bigr)=A.
\]
Therefore $A$ is closed in $Y$.
:::

<1>3. The map $f$ is a quotient map.
::: {.proof}
Taking complements in the equivalence of <1>2 gives, for every $U\subseteq Y$,
\[
U\text{ is open in }Y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f^{-1}(U)\text{ is open in }X.
\]
Together with the assumed surjectivity of $f$, this is exactly the definition of a quotient map.
:::
:::
