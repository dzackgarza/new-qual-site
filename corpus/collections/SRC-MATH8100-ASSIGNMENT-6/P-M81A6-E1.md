::: problem
Use the specified repeated integral involving $xe^{-x^2(1+y^2)}$ to prove that
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2},
$$
and hence that $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\pi x^2}\,dx=1$.
:::

::: solution
Let
\[
J=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx
\]
and consider the nonnegative function
\[
(x,y)\longmapsto x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}
\]
on $(0,\infty)^2$. Tonelli's theorem permits either order of integration.

First integrate in $x$. For fixed $y\ge0$,
\[
\int_0^\infty x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}\,dx
=\frac1{2(1+y^2)}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}\,dx\,dy
&=\frac12\int_0^\infty\frac{dy}{1+y^2}\\
&=\frac\pi4.
\end{aligned}
\]

Now integrate in $y$. For fixed $x>0$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}\,dy
&=x e^{-x^2}\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2y^2}\,dy\\
&=e^{-x^2}\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du\\
&=J e^{-x^2},
\end{aligned}
\]
where $u=xy$. Hence
\[
\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}\,dy\,dx
=J^2.
\]
Comparing the two evaluations gives
\[
J^2=\frac\pi4.
\]
Since $J>0$,
\[
\boxed{J=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}}.
\]

Finally, with $u=\sqrt\pi\,x$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\pi x^2}\,dx
&=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-u^2}\,du\\
&=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}J
=1.
\end{aligned}
\]
:::
