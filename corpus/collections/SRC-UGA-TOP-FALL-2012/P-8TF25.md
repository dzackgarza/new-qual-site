::: problem
Give an example of a map $f:X\to Y$ such that both spaces are connected and the map is a continuous bijection but not a homeomorphism.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Take
\[
X=[0,1),
\qquad
Y=S^1,
\]
and define
\[
f:[0,1)\longrightarrow S^1,
\qquad
f(t)=e^{2\pi i t}.
\]
Then both $X$ and $Y$ are connected.
::: {.proof}
The half-open interval $[0,1)$ is an interval in $\RR$, hence is connected.
The circle $S^1$ is the continuous image of the connected interval $[0,1]$ under
\[
t\longmapsto e^{2\pi i t},
\]
so it is connected as well.
:::

<1>2. The map $f$ is continuous and bijective.
::: {.proof}
Continuity follows from continuity of the complex exponential.

For surjectivity, every $z\in S^1$ can be written uniquely in the form
\[
z=e^{2\pi i t}
\]
with $t\in[0,1)$.
The same uniqueness proves injectivity: if
\[
e^{2\pi i s}=e^{2\pi i t}
\]
for $s,t\in[0,1)$, then $s-t\in\ZZ$, while $-1<s-t<1$, hence $s=t$.
:::

<1>3. The map $f$ is not a homeomorphism.
::: {.proof}
The circle $S^1$ is compact, since it is the continuous image of the compact interval $[0,1]$.

The interval $[0,1)$ is not compact. Indeed, the family
\[
U_n=[0,1-1/n)
\qquad(n\ge2)
\]
consists of open subsets of $[0,1)$ and covers $[0,1)$, but every finite subfamily is contained in one $U_N$ and therefore misses all points sufficiently close to $1$.

Compactness is preserved by homeomorphisms.
Thus $[0,1)$ and $S^1$ cannot be homeomorphic, so the continuous bijection $f$ is not a homeomorphism.
:::
:::
