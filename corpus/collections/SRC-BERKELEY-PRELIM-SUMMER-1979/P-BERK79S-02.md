::: {.problem}
Let $F\subseteq K$ be fields and let $p,q\in F[x]$.
Prove that the greatest common divisor of $p$ and $q$ computed in $F[x]$ is the same, up to multiplication by a unit, as their greatest common divisor computed in $K[x]$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
d=\gcd_{F[x]}(p,q).
$$

<1>1. The polynomial $d$ is a common divisor of $p$ and $q$ in $K[x]$.

::: {.proof}
Since $d$ is a common divisor in $F[x]$, there are polynomials
$$
p_1,q_1\in F[x]
$$
such that
$$
p=dp_1,
\qquad
q=dq_1.
$$
Because
$$
F[x]\subseteq K[x],
$$
the same factorizations hold in $K[x]$.
:::

<1>2. There are polynomials $a,b\in F[x]$ such that
$$
d=ap+bq.
$$

::: {.proof}
The ring $F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain and hence a principal ideal domain.
The ideal generated by $p$ and $q$ is therefore generated by their gcd:
$$
(p,q)=(d).
$$
In particular,
$$
d\in(p,q),
$$
which gives the displayed Bezout identity.
:::

<1>3. Every common divisor of $p$ and $q$ in $K[x]$ divides $d$ in
$K[x]$.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
c\in K[x]
$$
divide both $p$ and $q$. Then $c$ divides every $K[x]$-linear combination
of $p$ and $q$. Since the coefficients $a,b$ in step <1>2 belong to
$F[x]\subseteq K[x]$, the identity
$$
d=ap+bq
$$
shows that
$$
c\mid d
$$
in $K[x]$.
:::

<1>4. The polynomial $d$ is a greatest common divisor of $p$ and $q$ in
$K[x]$.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 says that $d$ is a common divisor in $K[x]$. Step <1>3 says that
every common divisor in $K[x]$ divides $d$. These are exactly the defining
properties of a greatest common divisor.
:::

<1>5. The gcd computed in $F[x]$ and the gcd computed in $K[x]$ differ by
a unit of $K[x]$:
$$
\boxed{
\gcd_{K[x]}(p,q)
\sim
\gcd_{F[x]}(p,q).
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Greatest common divisors in an integral domain are unique up to
multiplication by a unit. Step <1>4 shows that the $F[x]$ gcd is also a
$K[x]$ gcd. The units of $K[x]$ are the nonzero constants in $K$.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 is the required conclusion.
:::
