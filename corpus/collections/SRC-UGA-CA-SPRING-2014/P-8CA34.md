::: {.problem}
Suppose $\ell$ is a line and $C$ is a circle in the plane.
Under what conditions is there a Möbius map which simultaneously sends $\ell\cup\{\infty\}$ and $C$ to concentric circles?
Justify your conclusions.
:::

::: {.solution}
The answer: such a map exists if and only if $\ell$ and $C$ are disjoint.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #nec}
If a Möbius map $T$ sends $\ell\cup\{\infty\}$ and $C$ to concentric circles, then $\ell\cap C=\varnothing$.

::: pf-proof
$T$ is a bijection of the Riemann sphere, and $\ell\cup\{\infty\}\neq C$ because $\infty\notin C$. So the two images are distinct concentric circles, which have no common point. Hence $(\ell\cup\{\infty\})\cap C=\varnothing$, and so $\ell\cap C=\varnothing$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #normal}
If $\ell\cap C=\varnothing$, then after a rigid motion of the plane, $\ell=\RR$ and $C=\{z:\abs{z-ic}=r\}$ with $c>r>0$.

::: pf-proof
Rotate and translate so that $\ell$ is the real axis and the center of $C$ lies in the closed upper half-plane, at $ic$ with $c\ge0$. The circle $C$ misses $\RR$ exactly when the distance $c$ from its center to $\RR$ exceeds its radius $r$. A rigid motion is a Möbius map, and a composition of Möbius maps is a Möbius map.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #suff}
In the normal form of step [](#normal){.pf-ref}, put $s=\sqrt{c^2-r^2}>0$ and
$$
T(z)=\frac{z-is}{z+is}.
$$
Then $T$ sends $\RR\cup\{\infty\}$ to the unit circle and $C$ to a circle centered at $0$.

::: pf-proof
For real $x$, $\abs{x-is}=\abs{x+is}$, so $\abs{T(x)}=1$; also $T(\infty)=1$. So $T(\RR\cup\{\infty\})$ is the unit circle.

The points $p=is$ and $q=-is$ are symmetric with respect to $C$, because $q-ic$ is a positive multiple of $p-ic$ (both are negative multiples of $i$) and
$$
\abs{p-ic}\,\abs{q-ic}=(c-s)(c+s)=c^2-s^2=r^2.
$$
Möbius maps preserve symmetry with respect to generalized circles (the symmetry principle). Since $T(p)=0$ and $T(q)=\infty$, the points $0$ and $\infty$ are symmetric with respect to $T(C)$. The point $q$ is not on $C$, since $\abs{q-ic}=c+s>r$, so $T(C)$ does not pass through $\infty$ and is a circle. A circle with respect to which $0$ and $\infty$ are symmetric has its center at $0$.
:::

:::

::: pf-qed
Step [](#nec){.pf-ref} gives necessity, and steps [](#normal){.pf-ref} and [](#suff){.pf-ref} give sufficiency.
:::
