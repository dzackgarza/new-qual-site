::: {.problem}
Let $(a_n)$ be defined by
$$
1-x^2+x^4-x^6+\cdots
=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-3)^n,
\qquad 0<x<1.
$$
Find
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\abs{a_n}^{1/n}.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
F(z)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-3)^n,
$$
and let $R$ be its radius of convergence.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #geometric-series}
For $0<x<1$,
$$
1-x^2+x^4-x^6+\cdots=\frac1{1+x^2}.
$$

::: pf-proof
The left-hand side is the geometric series
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-x^2)^k,
$$
whose ratio has modulus $x^2<1$. Hence its sum is
$$
\frac1{1-(-x^2)}
=
\frac1{1+x^2}.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #initial-radius-bound}
One has
$$
R\geq3.
$$

::: pf-proof
By hypothesis, the power series defining $F$ converges for every real $x\in(0,1)$. For such $x$,
$$
\abs{x-3}=3-x.
$$
These distances approach $3$ as $x\downarrow0$. A power series converges at every point strictly inside its radius of convergence, so its radius must be at least the supremum of these distances, namely $3$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #identity-theorem}
The function $F$ agrees with
$$
h(z)\coloneqq\frac1{1+z^2}
$$
on the disk
$$
\abs{z-3}<3.
$$

::: pf-proof
By [](#initial-radius-bound){.pf-ref}, $F$ is holomorphic on $\abs{z-3}<3$. The poles of $h$ are at $\pm i$, and
$$
\abs{3-i}=\abs{3+i}=\sqrt{10}>3,
$$
so $h$ is also holomorphic on that disk. By [](#geometric-series){.pf-ref} and the defining identity in the problem,
$$
F(x)=h(x)
$$
for every $x\in(0,1)$. This set has accumulation points inside the disk. The identity theorem therefore gives $F=h$ throughout $\abs{z-3}<3$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #taylor-coefficients}
The coefficients $a_n$ are the Taylor coefficients of $h(z)=1/(1+z^2)$ about $z=3$.

::: pf-proof
[](#identity-theorem){.pf-ref} identifies $F$ with $h$ on a neighborhood of $3$. Taylor expansions of a holomorphic function about a fixed center are unique, so
$$
h(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-3)^n
$$
near $z=3$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #exact-radius}
The Taylor series of $h$ about $3$ has radius of convergence
$$
R=\sqrt{10}.
$$

::: pf-proof
The function $h$ is holomorphic on
$$
\abs{z-3}<\sqrt{10},
$$
because its only singularities are $\pm i$, both at distance $\sqrt{10}$ from $3$. Hence its Taylor series about $3$ converges at least throughout this disk.

It cannot have radius greater than $\sqrt{10}$. If it did, its sum would define a holomorphic function on a neighborhood of $i$. By [](#taylor-coefficients){.pf-ref} and the identity theorem, that sum would agree with $h$ on the punctured part of such a neighborhood. But $h$ has a pole at $i$, whereas a holomorphic function is bounded on some sufficiently small closed disk around $i$, a contradiction. Thus the radius is exactly $\sqrt{10}$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Therefore
$$
\boxed{
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\abs{a_n}^{1/n}
=
\frac1{\sqrt{10}}
}.
$$

::: pf-proof
By the Cauchy--Hadamard formula, the radius of convergence of
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-3)^n
$$
satisfies
$$
\frac1R
=
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\abs{a_n}^{1/n}.
$$
Apply [](#exact-radius){.pf-ref}.
:::
