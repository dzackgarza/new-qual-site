::: {.problem}
Evaluate
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(nx)}{x^4+1}\,dx.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
$$
a\coloneqq |n|,
\qquad
s\coloneqq\frac{a}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Since $\cos(nx)=\cos(ax)$, it is enough to work with $a\geq0$.

<1>1. The poles in the open upper half-plane of
$$
F(z)\coloneqq\frac{e^{iaz}}{z^4+1}
$$
are
$$
z_1=e^{i\pi/4}
=
\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
z_2=e^{3i\pi/4}
=
\frac{-1+i}{\sqrt2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
The roots of $z^4+1=0$ are
$$
e^{i(\pi/4+k\pi/2)},
\qquad
k=0,1,2,3.
$$
Exactly the first two lie in the open upper half-plane.
:::

<1>2. The sum of the residues of $F$ in the upper half-plane is
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_1}F
+
\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_2}F
=
-\frac{i}{2\sqrt2}
e^{-s}
\bigl(\cos s+\sin s\bigr).
$$

::: {.proof}
Both poles are simple, so
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_j}F
=
\frac{e^{iaz_j}}{4z_j^3}.
$$
Since
$$
e^{iaz_1}=e^{-s}e^{is},
\qquad
e^{iaz_2}=e^{-s}e^{-is},
$$
and
$$
\frac1{4z_1^3}
=
\frac{-1-i}{4\sqrt2},
\qquad
\frac1{4z_2^3}
=
\frac{1-i}{4\sqrt2},
$$
their sum is
$$
\frac{e^{-s}}{4\sqrt2}
\left[
(-1-i)e^{is}
+
(1-i)e^{-is}
\right].
$$
Writing $e^{is}=\cos s+i\sin s$, the bracket simplifies to
$$
-2i(\cos s+\sin s),
$$
which gives the claim.
:::

<1>3. One has
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{iax}}{x^4+1}\,dx
=
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}
e^{-s}
\bigl(\cos s+\sin s\bigr).
$$

::: {.proof}
Integrate $F$ over the positively oriented upper semicircular contour of radius $R>1$. On its arc $\Gamma_R$,
$$
|e^{iaz}|=e^{-a\operatorname{Im}z}\leq1
$$
because $a\geq0$ and $\operatorname{Im}z\geq0$, while
$$
|z^4+1|
\geq
R^4-1.
$$
Thus
$$
\left|
\int_{\Gamma_R}F(z)\,dz
\right|
\leq
\frac{\pi R}{R^4-1}
\longrightarrow0.
$$
The residue theorem and step <1>2 therefore give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{iax}}{x^4+1}\,dx
&=
2\pi i
\left[
-\frac{i}{2\sqrt2}
e^{-s}
(\cos s+\sin s)
\right]\\
&=
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}
e^{-s}
(\cos s+\sin s).
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>4. The required integral is
$$
\boxed{
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\cos(nx)}{x^4+1}\,dx
=
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}
e^{-|n|/\sqrt2}
\left(
\cos\frac{|n|}{\sqrt2}
+
\sin\frac{|n|}{\sqrt2}
\right)
}.
$$

::: {.proof}
The imaginary part of
$$
\frac{e^{iax}}{x^4+1}
$$
is
$$
\frac{\sin(ax)}{x^4+1},
$$
an odd function of $x$, so its integral over $\mathbb R$ is zero. Thus the integral in step <1>3 is exactly the cosine integral. Substitute
$$
s=\frac{|n|}{\sqrt2}.
$$
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 gives the requested value.
:::
