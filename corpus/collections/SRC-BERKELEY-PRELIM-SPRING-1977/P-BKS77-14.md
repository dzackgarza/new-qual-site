::: {.problem}
A square matrix is nilpotent if $A^k=0$ for some positive integer $k$.

1. If $A$ and $B$ are nilpotent, must $A+B$ be nilpotent?
2. Prove that if $A$ and $B$ are nilpotent and commute, then $A+B$ is nilpotent.
3. Prove that if $A$ is nilpotent, then both $I+A$ and $I-A$ are invertible.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The answer to part 1 is no.

::: {.proof}
Take
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then
$$
A^2=B^2=0,
$$
so both matrices are nilpotent. However,
$$
A+B=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
$$
and
$$
(A+B)^2=I.
$$
Hence no positive power of $A+B$ is zero.
:::

<1>2. Suppose $A^m=0$, $B^n=0$, and $AB=BA$. Then
$$
(A+B)^{m+n-1}=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
Because $A$ and $B$ commute, the binomial theorem applies:
$$
(A+B)^{m+n-1}
=
\sum_{k=0}^{m+n-1}
\binom{m+n-1}{k}
A^kB^{m+n-1-k}.
$$
For every $k$, either
$$
k\geq m
$$
or
$$
m+n-1-k\geq n.
$$
Indeed, if both inequalities failed, then
$$
k\leq m-1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
m+n-1-k\leq n-1,
$$
whose sum would give $m+n-1\leq m+n-2$, impossible. Thus every term in the binomial sum contains either the factor $A^m$ or the factor $B^n$ and is zero.
:::

<1>3. Therefore, if $A$ and $B$ are nilpotent and commute, then $A+B$ is nilpotent.

::: {.proof}
Choose positive integers $m,n$ with
$$
A^m=0,
\qquad
B^n=0.
$$
Step <1>2 gives a positive power of $A+B$ equal to zero.
:::

<1>4. If $A^m=0$, then
$$
(I-A)
\left(
I+A+\cdots+A^{m-1}
\right)
=I.
$$

::: {.proof}
This is the finite geometric-series identity:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(I-A)
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}A^k
&=
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}A^k
-\sum_{k=1}^{m}A^k\\
&=
I-A^m\\
&=I.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since every factor is a polynomial in $A$, the factors commute, so the same expression is also a left inverse.
:::

<1>5. If $A^m=0$, then
$$
(I+A)
\left(
I-A+A^2-\cdots+(-1)^{m-1}A^{m-1}
\right)
=I.
$$

::: {.proof}
Apply the finite geometric-series identity to $-A$:
$$
(I-(-A))
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-A)^k
=
I-(-A)^m.
$$
Because $A^m=0$, the right-hand side is $I$. Again the factors commute because both are polynomials in $A$.
:::

<1>6. Thus both $I-A$ and $I+A$ are invertible.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 gives the inverse
$$
(I-A)^{-1}
=
I+A+\cdots+A^{m-1},
$$
and step <1>5 gives
$$
(I+A)^{-1}
=
I-A+A^2-\cdots+(-1)^{m-1}A^{m-1}.
$$
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 answers part 1, step <1>3 proves part 2, and step <1>6 proves part 3.
:::
