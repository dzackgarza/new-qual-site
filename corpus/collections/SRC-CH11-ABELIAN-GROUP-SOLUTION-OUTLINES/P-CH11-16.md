::: {.problem}
How does the number (up to isomorphism) of Abelian groups of order $n$ compare with the number (up to isomorphism) of Abelian groups of order $m$ where:

(a) $n = 3^2$ and $m = 5^2$?

(b) $n = 2^4$ and $m = 5^4$?

(c) $n = p^r$ and $m = q^r$, where $p$ and $q$ are primes?

(d) $n = p^r$ and $m = p^r q$ where $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes?

(e) $n = p^r$ and $m = p^r q^2$ where $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes?
:::

::: {.solution}

Write $p(a)$ for the number of partitions of $a$, with $p(0)=p(1)=1$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #principle}

If $n=\prod_{i} p_{i}^{a_{i}}$ then the number of Abelian groups of order $n$ up
to isomorphism is $\prod_{i} p(a_{i})$, depending only on the multiset of
exponents $a_{i}$.

::: pf-proof

By the fundamental theorem of finite Abelian groups every finite Abelian group
is a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, uniquely up to
isomorphism and reordering of the factors. Collecting the factors whose orders
are powers of one prime $p_{i}$ gives the Sylow $p_{i}$-subgroup, and the
exponents of its cyclic factors form a partition of $a_{i}$. Conversely any
partition $\sum_{j} m_{j}=a_{i}$ gives the group $\prod_{j}\ZZ_{p_{i}^{m_{j}}}$
of order $p_{i}^{a_{i}}$. Distinct partitions of $a_{i}$ give non-isomorphic
$p_{i}$-groups, since the multiset of exponents is recovered from the group by
the uniqueness clause. The choices are independent over the distinct primes
because a group of order $n$ is the direct product of its Sylow subgroups. So
the isomorphism classes correspond bijectively to the tuples of partitions of
the exponents, and there are $\prod_{i}p(a_{i})$ of them.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #small-partitions}

$p(1)=1$, $p(2)=2$ and $p(4)=5$.

::: pf-proof

The partitions of $1$ is $\{1\}$; of $2$ are $\{2\}$ and $\{1,1\}$; of $4$ are
$\{4\}$, $\{3,1\}$, $\{2,2\}$, $\{2,1,1\}$ and $\{1,1,1,1\}$. Note $p$ depends
only on the exponent, never on the prime: the primes themselves do not enter the
count.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #parts-abc}

Parts (a), (b) and (c) are "Same": in each case the two orders have the same
exponents, and by step [](#principle){.pf-ref} the count uses only the exponents.

::: pf-proof

(a) $3^{2}$ has count $p(2)=2$, with groups $\ZZ_{9}$ and $\ZZ_{3}\oplus \ZZ_{3}$;
$5^{2}$ has count $p(2)=2$, with $\ZZ_{25}$ and $\ZZ_{5}\oplus\ZZ_{5}$.
(b) $2^{4}$ and $5^{4}$ both have count $p(4)=5$.
(c) $p^{r}$ and $q^{r}$ both have count $p(r)$ for the same reason. In each
case the two numbers of groups agree because the exponent is unchanged and the
prime is irrelevant to the count.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #part-d}

Part (d) is "Same": $p^{r}$ has $p(r)$ groups and $p^{r}q$ has
$p(r)p(1)=p(r)$ of them.

::: pf-proof

Here $a=(r)$ for $p^{r}$ and $a=(r,1)$ for $p^{r}q$, so the counts are $p(r)$
and $p(r)p(1)=p(r)$. The extra factor $q$ contributes a cyclic Sylow
$q$-subgroup, which is the only group of order $q$, so adjoining it multiplies
the number of isomorphism classes by $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #part-e}

Part (e) is "twice as many": $p^{r}$ has $p(r)$ groups, while $p^{r}q^{2}$ has
$p(r)p(2)=2p(r)$.

::: pf-proof

The two groups of order $q^{2}$ are $\ZZ_{q^{2}}$ and $\ZZ_{q}\oplus\ZZ_{q}$,
and by step [](#principle){.pf-ref} each of the $p(r)$ isomorphism classes of
order $p^{r}$ can be combined with either one, giving $2p(r)$ classes of order
$p^{r}q^{2}$ and no others.

:::
