::: {.problem}
Let $f$ be entire, real-valued on the real axis, satisfy $f(0)=0$, and not be identically zero. Prove that if $f$ maps the imaginary axis into a straight line, then that line is either the real axis or the imaginary axis.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $L$ be the straight line containing
$$
f(i\RR).
$$

<1>1. The line $L$ passes through the origin and is therefore a
one-dimensional real vector subspace of $\CC$.

::: {.proof}
Since $0$ lies on the imaginary axis and
$$
f(0)=0,
$$
one has $0\in L$. Any straight line in $\CC\cong\RR^2$ through the origin
is a one-dimensional real vector subspace.
:::

<1>2. In the Taylor expansion
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n,
$$
every coefficient $a_n$ is real.

::: {.proof}
Define
$$
g(z)\coloneqq\overline{f(\overline z)}.
$$
The function $g$ is entire. For real $x$, the hypothesis that $f(x)$ is
real gives
$$
g(x)
=
\overline{f(x)}
=
f(x).
$$
By the identity theorem,
$$
g=f
$$
on $\CC$. Comparing Taylor expansions at $0$ gives
$$
\overline{a_n}=a_n
$$
for every $n$, so all coefficients are real.
:::

<1>3. There is a least integer $m\geq1$ such that
$$
a_m\neq0.
$$

::: {.proof}
The condition $f(0)=0$ gives $a_0=0$. Since $f$ is not identically zero,
some Taylor coefficient is nonzero. Choose the least such index. It is at
least $1$.
:::

<1>4. For every nonzero real $y$,
$$
\frac{f(iy)}{y^m}\in L,
$$
and
$$
\lim_{y\to0}\frac{f(iy)}{y^m}
=
a_mi^m.
$$

::: {.proof}
By hypothesis,
$$
f(iy)\in L.
$$
Since $L$ is a real vector subspace by step <1>1 and $y^m$ is a nonzero
real number,
$$
\frac{f(iy)}{y^m}\in L.
$$
By the choice of $m$,
$$
f(z)
=
a_mz^m+O(z^{m+1})
\qquad(z\to0).
$$
Substituting $z=iy$ and dividing by $y^m$ gives
$$
\frac{f(iy)}{y^m}
=
a_mi^m+O(y),
$$
which proves the limit.
:::

<1>5. The line $L$ is
$$
L=i^m\RR.
$$

::: {.proof}
The line $L$ is closed in $\CC$. Step <1>4 therefore implies
$$
a_mi^m\in L.
$$
By steps <1>2 and <1>3,
$$
a_m\in\RR\setminus\{0\},
$$
so $a_mi^m$ is a nonzero real multiple of $i^m$. A one-dimensional real
subspace containing this nonzero vector must be exactly
$$
i^m\RR.
$$
:::

<1>6. The line $L$ is either the real axis or the imaginary axis.

::: {.proof}
If $m$ is even, then
$$
i^m\RR=\RR.
$$
If $m$ is odd, then
$$
i^m\RR=i\RR.
$$
Apply step <1>5.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>6 is the required conclusion.
:::
