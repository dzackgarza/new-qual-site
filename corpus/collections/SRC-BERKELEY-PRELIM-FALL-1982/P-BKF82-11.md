::: {.problem}
(a) Prove there is no continuous map from $[0,1]$ onto $(0,1)$.

(b) Find a continuous surjection from $(0,1)$ onto $[0,1]$.

(c) Prove no map in part (b) can be bijective.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. There is no continuous surjection $[0,1]\to(0,1)$.
::: {.proof}
The interval $[0,1]$ is compact. The continuous image of a compact space is
compact, so the image of any continuous map
$$
[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb R
$$
is compact. But $(0,1)$ is not compact. Hence no continuous map from
$[0,1]$ can have image exactly $(0,1)$.
:::

<1>2. Construct a continuous surjection $(0,1)\to[0,1]$.
::: {.proof}
Define
$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&0<x\le\frac13,\\[1mm]
3x-1,&\frac13\le x\le\frac23,\\[1mm]
1,&\frac23\le x<1.
\end{cases}
$$
The formulas agree at $x=1/3$ and $x=2/3$, so $f$ is continuous. On the
middle interval it maps linearly from $0$ to $1$, hence
$$
f((0,1))=[0,1].
$$
Thus $f$ is a continuous surjection.
:::

<1>3. No continuous surjection $(0,1)\to[0,1]$ can be injective.
::: {.proof}
Suppose $f:(0,1)\to[0,1]$ were continuous and bijective. By surjectivity,
choose $x_0,x_1\in(0,1)$ with
$$
f(x_0)=0,
\qquad
f(x_1)=1.
$$
After interchanging them if necessary, suppose $x_0<x_1$. Since $x_0>0$,
choose $x\in(0,x_0)$. Put $c=f(x)$.

If $c=0$ or $c=1$, injectivity already fails because $x$ is distinct from
$x_0,x_1$. If $0<c<1$, the intermediate value theorem applied on
$[x_0,x_1]$ gives some $y\in(x_0,x_1)$ with
$$
f(y)=c=f(x),
$$
again contradicting injectivity. Therefore no continuous surjection in part
(b) can be bijective.
:::
:::
