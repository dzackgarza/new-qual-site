::: {.problem}
Let
\[
R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-10}] = \{ a + b\sqrt{-10} \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Z} \}.
\]
Let $I = \langle 2, \sqrt{-10} \rangle$ be the ideal of $R$ generated by $2$ and $\sqrt{-10}$.

(a) Show that $I$ is not a free $R$-module.

(b) Show that $I$ is a projective $R$-module.
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
\[
s:=\sqrt{-10},
\qquad
s^2=-10.
\]
Thus
\[
R=\mathbb Z[s],
\qquad
I=(2,s).
\]

<1>1. The ideal $I$ is proper.
::: {.proof}
Define
\[
\varphi:R\longrightarrow\mathbb F_2,
\qquad
\varphi(a+bs)=\bar a.
\]
This is a surjective ring homomorphism.
Its kernel consists of the elements whose integer coefficient is even.
Every element of $I=(2,s)$ has even integer coefficient, and conversely every element
\[
2a+bs
\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb Z)
\]
lies in $I$.
Hence
\[
\ker\varphi=I,
\]
so
\[
R/I\cong\mathbb F_2.
\]
In particular, $I\neq R$.
:::

<1>2. If $I$ were a free $R$-module, then it would be free of rank $1$ and hence principal.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
F:=\operatorname{Frac}(R)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-10}).
\]
Since $I$ is a nonzero ideal of the domain $R$, localization gives
\[
I\otimes_R F\cong F.
\]
Indeed, after inverting all nonzero elements of $R$, the element $2\in I$ becomes invertible, so the localized ideal is the whole field $F$.

If $I$ were free, then since it is generated by two elements it would have finite rank, say
\[
I\cong R^r.
\]
Tensoring with $F$ would give
\[
F\cong I\otimes_RF\cong F^r.
\]
Thus
\[
r=1.
\]
A free rank-one ideal is generated by the image of a basis vector, so it is principal.
:::

<1>3. The ideal $I$ is not principal.
::: {.proof}
Suppose
\[
I=(\alpha)
\]
for some nonzero
\[
\alpha=a+bs\in R.
\]
Since $2,s\in I$, the element $\alpha$ divides both $2$ and $s$ in $R$.
Use the multiplicative norm
\[
N(a+bs)=(a+bs)(a-bs)=a^2+10b^2.
\]
From $\alpha\mid2$ and $\alpha\mid s$, multiplicativity gives
\[
N(\alpha)\mid N(2)=4
\]
and
\[
N(\alpha)\mid N(s)=10.
\]
Hence
\[
N(\alpha)\mid2.
\]

By <1>1, $I$ is proper, so $\alpha$ cannot be a unit.
A unit has norm $1$ here, and conversely norm $1$ gives an inverse by conjugation.
Thus
\[
N(\alpha)>1.
\]
Consequently
\[
N(\alpha)=2.
\]
But this would require integers $a,b$ satisfying
\[
a^2+10b^2=2.
\]
If $b\neq0$, the left side is at least $10$, while if $b=0$ it would say $a^2=2$.
Both are impossible.
Therefore $I$ is not principal.
:::

<1>4. The ideal $I$ is not a free $R$-module.
::: {.proof}
If $I$ were free, <1>2 would make it principal, contradicting <1>3.
This proves part (a).
:::

<1>5. The natural surjection
\[
\pi:R^2\longrightarrow I,
\qquad
\pi(u,v)=2u+sv
\]
has an $R$-linear section.
::: {.proof}
For $z\in I$, define
\[
\sigma(z):=
\left(3z,\frac{s}{2}z\right).
\]
We first check that the second coordinate lies in $R$.
Write
\[
z=2r+st
\qquad(r,t\in R).
\]
Then
\[
\frac{s}{2}z
=sr+\frac{s^2}{2}t
=sr-5t
\in R.
\]
Thus
\[
\sigma:I\longrightarrow R^2
\]
is well defined, and it is $R$-linear.
Moreover,
\[
\pi(\sigma(z))
=2(3z)+s\left(\frac{s}{2}z\right)
=6z+\frac{s^2}{2}z
=6z-5z
=z.
\]
Hence
\[
\pi\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}_I.
\]
:::

<1>6. The ideal $I$ is projective.
::: {.proof}
By <1>5, the surjection
\[
R^2\twoheadrightarrow I
\]
splits.
Therefore
\[
R^2\cong\ker\pi\oplus I.
\]
Thus $I$ is a direct summand of the free module $R^2$, and hence is projective.
This proves part (b).
:::
