::: {.problem}
Give the Laurent series for the following functions, and characterize their singularities at zero (essential, removable, or pole):

(a) $\dfrac{\sin^2 z}{z}$

(b) $ze^{-1/z^2}$

(c) $\dfrac{1}{z(4-z)}$
:::

::: {.solution}
The collection's provenance is the examination paper alone, so no retained solution set covers this exercise; the argument is the card's own.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #a}
$\dfrac{\sin^2z}{z}=\displaystyle\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{2^{2k-1}}{(2k)!}z^{2k-1}$, and the singularity at $0$ is removable.

::: pf-proof
$\sin^2z=\frac12(1-\cos 2z)$ is a trigonometric identity, and $\cos 2z=\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^k\frac{(2z)^{2k}}{(2k)!}$ converges for all $z$; so
$$
\sin^2z=\frac12\left(1-1+\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{2^{2k}z^{2k}}{(2k)!}\right)=\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{2^{2k-1}}{(2k)!}z^{2k},
$$
dividing each coefficient by $2$. Dividing by $z$ gives the displayed series, whose terms begin $z-\frac{z^3}{3}+\frac{2z^5}{45}-\cdots$ (from $k=1,2,3$: coefficients $\frac{2}{2}$, $-\frac{8}{24}$, $\frac{32}{720}$). No negative power of $z$ occurs, so the principal part vanishes and $0$ is removable; the extension assigns the value $0$ at $z=0$, the value of the series there.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #b}
$ze^{-1/z^2}=\displaystyle\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}z^{1-2k}$, and $0$ is an essential singularity.

::: pf-proof
$e^{w}=\sum_{k\ge0}w^k/k!$ for all $w$, and substituting $w=-1/z^2$ for $z\ne0$ gives $e^{-1/z^2}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}z^{-2k}$; multiplying by $z$,
$$
ze^{-1/z^2}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}z^{1-2k}=z-\frac1z+\frac{1}{2z^3}-\frac{1}{6z^5}+\cdots
$$
This is the Laurent expansion on $\CC\setminus\{0\}$. Infinitely many coefficients of negative index ($1-2k<0$ for every $k\ge1$) are nonzero, which is exactly the criterion for an essential singularity at $0$, so $0$ is neither removable nor a pole.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #c}
For $0<\abs{z}<4$, $\dfrac{1}{z(4-z)}=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^{n-1}}{4^{n+1}}=\frac1{4z}+\frac1{16}+\frac z{64}+\cdots$, and $0$ is a simple pole.

::: pf-proof
$\frac{1}{z(4-z)}=\frac{1}{4z}\cdot\frac{1}{1-z/4}$, and for $\abs{z/4}<1$ the geometric series gives $\frac{1}{1-z/4}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{4^n}$; multiplying by $\frac1{4z}$ gives $\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^{n-1}}{4^{n+1}}$, whose first three terms are $\frac1{4z}$, $\frac1{16}$, $\frac z{64}$. The principal part is $\frac1{4z}$, a single term of order one, so $0$ is a pole of order $1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
The three singularities at $0$ are: removable for $\frac{\sin^2z}{z}$, essential for $ze^{-1/z^2}$, a simple pole for $\frac{1}{z(4-z)}$.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#a){.pf-ref}, [](#b){.pf-ref} and [](#c){.pf-ref} give the Laurent series and the classification of each.
:::
