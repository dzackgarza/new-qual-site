::: {.problem}
The first two homework assignments referenced by the sample midterm are:

1. Using stereographic projection from the north and south poles, construct two
   coordinate neighborhoods covering the standard unit sphere
   \[
   S^2\subset\mathbb R^3
   \]
   and prove that they determine a smooth structure on $S^2$.

2. Let
   \[
   \alpha:\mathbb R\to M
   \]
   be a smooth curve with
   \[
   \alpha(0)=p.
   \]
   For a smooth function $f:M\to\mathbb R$, define the second-order functional
   \[
   A_\alpha(f)
   =
   \left.
   \frac{d^2}{du^2}
   (f\circ\alpha)(u)
   \right|_{u=0}.
   \]

   (a) If the velocity
   \[
   \alpha'(0)=0\in T_pM,
   \]
   show that $A_\alpha$ is a tangent vector at $p$.

   (b) Show that if
   \[
   \alpha'(0)\ne0,
   \]
   then $A_\alpha$ is not a tangent vector at $p$.

   (c) For
   \[
   \alpha(u)=(u^2,u^4)
   \]
   in $\mathbb R^2$, use this construction to obtain a nonzero tangent vector to
   the curve at the origin.
:::

::: {.solution}
For the first assignment, let
$$
N=(0,0,1),
\qquad
S=(0,0,-1),
$$
and put
$$
U_N=S^2\setminus\{N\},
\qquad
U_S=S^2\setminus\{S\}.
$$

<1>1. Stereographic projection from the north pole defines
$$
\phi_N:
U_N\to\mathbb R^2,
\qquad
\boxed{
\phi_N(x,y,z)
=
\left(
\frac{x}{1-z},
\frac{y}{1-z}
\right)}.
$$

::: {.proof}
For
$$
q=(x,y,z)\in S^2\setminus\{N\},
$$
the line through
$$
N
$$
and
$$
q
$$
meets the plane
$$
z=0
$$
at a unique point.

Parametrize the line by
$$
N+t(q-N)
=
(tx,ty,1+t(z-1)).
$$
Setting its third coordinate equal to
$$
0
$$
gives
$$
t
=
\frac1{1-z}.
$$
The first two coordinates of the intersection point are therefore the
displayed formula.
:::

<1>2. The map
$$
\phi_N
$$
is a homeomorphism with inverse
$$
\boxed{
\phi_N^{-1}(u,v)
=
\left(
\frac{2u}{1+u^2+v^2},
\frac{2v}{1+u^2+v^2},
\frac{u^2+v^2-1}{1+u^2+v^2}
\right)}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Write
$$
r^2=u^2+v^2.
$$
The displayed point has squared Euclidean norm
$$
\frac{
4r^2+(r^2-1)^2
}{
(1+r^2)^2
}
=
\frac{
r^4+2r^2+1
}{
(1+r^2)^2
}
=1,
$$
so it lies on
$$
S^2.
$$
Its third coordinate is strictly less than
$$
1,
$$
so it lies in
$$
U_N.
$$

A direct substitution gives
$$
\phi_N\circ\phi_N^{-1}
=
\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb R^2}
$$
and
$$
\phi_N^{-1}\circ\phi_N
=
\operatorname{id}_{U_N}.
$$
Both maps are continuous because their denominators do not vanish on their
domains.
:::

<1>3. Stereographic projection from the south pole defines
$$
\phi_S:
U_S\to\mathbb R^2,
\qquad
\boxed{
\phi_S(x,y,z)
=
\left(
\frac{x}{1+z},
\frac{y}{1+z}
\right)}.
$$
Its inverse is
$$
\boxed{
\phi_S^{-1}(u,v)
=
\left(
\frac{2u}{1+u^2+v^2},
\frac{2v}{1+u^2+v^2},
\frac{1-u^2-v^2}{1+u^2+v^2}
\right)}.
$$

::: {.proof}
The derivation is the same as in steps <1>1--<1>2, now using the line through
$$
S
$$
and a point of the sphere.
Direct substitution verifies the inverse formulas.
:::

<1>4. The two coordinate neighborhoods cover
$$
S^2:
$$
$$
\boxed{
U_N\cup U_S=S^2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
The only point omitted by
$$
U_N
$$
is
$$
N,
$$
and
$$
N\in U_S.
$$
Likewise the only point omitted by
$$
U_S
$$
is
$$
S,
$$
and
$$
S\in U_N.
$$
:::

<1>5. On the overlap, the transition function is
$$
\boxed{
\phi_S\circ\phi_N^{-1}(u,v)
=
\left(
\frac{u}{u^2+v^2},
\frac{v}{u^2+v^2}
\right)}
$$
for
$$
(u,v)\ne(0,0).
$$

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
r^2=u^2+v^2.
$$
From step <1>2,
$$
\phi_N^{-1}(u,v)
=
\left(
\frac{2u}{1+r^2},
\frac{2v}{1+r^2},
\frac{r^2-1}{1+r^2}
\right).
$$
Applying
$$
\phi_S
$$
gives
$$
1+z
=
1+
\frac{r^2-1}{1+r^2}
=
\frac{2r^2}{1+r^2},
$$
and hence
$$
\phi_S\circ\phi_N^{-1}(u,v)
=
\left(
\frac{u}{r^2},
\frac{v}{r^2}
\right).
$$

The overlap excludes
$$
S,
$$
which has north-pole coordinate
$$
(0,0).
$$
Thus
$$
r^2>0
$$
on the domain.
:::

<1>6. The transition map in step <1>5 and its inverse are smooth on
$$
\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Each component is a rational function with denominator
$$
u^2+v^2,
$$
which is nonzero on the domain.
Therefore the transition map is
$$
C^\infty.
$$

The same formula is its own inverse:
if
$$
I(u,v)
=
\frac{(u,v)}{u^2+v^2},
$$
then
$$
\|I(u,v)\|^2
=
\frac1{u^2+v^2},
$$
so
$$
I(I(u,v))
=(u,v).
$$
Hence the inverse transition map is smooth as well.
:::

<1>7. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
(U_N,\phi_N),
\quad
(U_S,\phi_S)
}
$$
form a smooth atlas on
$$
S^2.
$$

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>2--<1>4 give two coordinate neighborhoods covering the sphere.
Step <1>6 proves smooth compatibility on their overlap.
Thus the two charts determine a smooth structure on
$$
S^2.
$$
:::

For the second assignment, define
$$
A_\alpha:
C^\infty(M)\to\mathbb R,
\qquad
A_\alpha(f)
=
\left.
\frac{d^2}{du^2}
(f\circ\alpha)(u)
\right|_{u=0}.
$$

<1>8. The functional
$$
A_\alpha
$$
is
$$
\mathbb R
$$
-linear.

::: {.proof}
For
$$
c,d\in\mathbb R
$$
and
$$
f,g\in C^\infty(M),
$$
one has
$$
(cf+dg)\circ\alpha
=
c(f\circ\alpha)+d(g\circ\alpha).
$$
Taking the second derivative at
$$
0
$$
is a linear operation.
:::

<1>9. For
$$
f,g\in C^\infty(M),
$$
one has
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_\alpha(fg)
&=
A_\alpha(f)g(p)
+f(p)A_\alpha(g)\\
&\quad+
2
\left.
\frac d{du}
(f\circ\alpha)
\right|_{0}
\left.
\frac d{du}
(g\circ\alpha)
\right|_{0}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::: {.proof}
Apply the ordinary second-derivative product rule to
$$
(fg)\circ\alpha
=
(f\circ\alpha)(g\circ\alpha).
$$
At
$$
u=0,
$$
the zeroth-order factors are
$$
f(p)
$$
and
$$
g(p).
$$
:::

<1>10. If
$$
\alpha'(0)=0,
$$
then for every smooth
$$
f:M\to\mathbb R
$$
one has
$$
\left.
\frac d{du}
(f\circ\alpha)
\right|_0
=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
By the chain rule,
$$
\left.
\frac d{du}
(f\circ\alpha)
\right|_0
=
df_p(\alpha'(0)).
$$
If
$$
\alpha'(0)=0,
$$
the right-hand side vanishes.
:::

<1>11. If
$$
\alpha'(0)=0,
$$
then
$$
\boxed{
A_\alpha(fg)
=
A_\alpha(f)g(p)
+f(p)A_\alpha(g)}
$$
for all
$$
f,g\in C^\infty(M).
$$

::: {.proof}
The extra cross term in step <1>9 vanishes by step <1>10.
:::

<1>12. Therefore, if
$$
\alpha'(0)=0,
$$
the acceleration functional
$$
A_\alpha
$$
is a tangent vector in
$$
T_pM.
$$

::: {.proof}
In the derivation definition of the tangent space,
$$
T_pM
$$
consists precisely of
$$
\mathbb R
$$
-linear maps
$$
D:C^\infty(M)\to\mathbb R
$$
satisfying the Leibniz rule
$$
D(fg)
=
D(f)g(p)+f(p)D(g).
$$
Step <1>8 gives linearity and step <1>11 gives Leibniz.
:::

<1>13. Suppose now that
$$
v
\coloneqq
\alpha'(0)
\ne0.
$$
There is a smooth function
$$
f\in C^\infty(M)
$$
such that
$$
\boxed{df_p(v)\ne0}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Choose a coordinate chart
$$
(U,x^1,\ldots,x^m)
$$
about
$$
p.
$$
Write
$$
v
=
\sum_i v^i
\left.
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}
\right|_p.
$$
Since
$$
v\ne0,
$$
some coefficient
$$
v^j
$$
is nonzero.
Choose a smooth bump function
$$
\chi:M\to[0,1]
$$
with
$$
\chi\equiv1
$$
on a neighborhood of
$$
p
$$
and
$$
\operatorname{supp}\chi\subseteq U.
$$
Define
$$
f(q)
=
\begin{cases}
\chi(q)x^j(q),&q\in U,\\
0,&q\notin U.
\end{cases}
$$
Because
$$
\chi
$$
vanishes on a neighborhood of
$$
M\setminus U,
$$
this defines a global smooth function.
Near
$$
p,
$$
one has
$$
f=x^j,
$$
and therefore
$$
df_p(v)=v^j\ne0.
$$
:::

<1>14. For such an
$$
f,
$$
the functional
$$
A_\alpha
$$
fails the Leibniz rule.

::: {.proof}
Set
$$
g=f
$$
in step <1>9.
Then
$$
A_\alpha(f^2)
=
2f(p)A_\alpha(f)
+2
\left(
df_p(v)
\right)^2.
$$

The Leibniz rule for a tangent vector would require
$$
A_\alpha(f^2)
=
2f(p)A_\alpha(f).
$$
The extra term is nonzero by step <1>13.
Thus the Leibniz identity fails.
:::

<1>15. Hence if
$$
\alpha'(0)\ne0,
$$
then
$$
\boxed{
A_\alpha\notin T_pM}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>14 shows that
$$
A_\alpha
$$
is not a derivation at
$$
p,
$$
so it is not a tangent vector under the derivation characterization.
:::

<1>16. For
$$
\alpha(u)
=(u^2,u^4),
$$
one has
$$
\alpha(0)=(0,0)
$$
and
$$
\alpha'(0)=(0,0).
$$

::: {.proof}
Differentiate:
$$
\alpha'(u)
=(2u,4u^3).
$$
At
$$
u=0
$$
both components vanish.
:::

<1>17. For every
$$
f\in C^\infty(\mathbb R^2),
$$
the acceleration functional of this curve is
$$
\boxed{
A_\alpha(f)
=
2
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(0,0)}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Using
$$
x(u)=u^2,
\qquad
y(u)=u^4,
$$
the second-order chain rule gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2}{du^2}
f(x(u),y(u))
&=
f_{xx}(x')^2
+2f_{xy}x'y'
+f_{yy}(y')^2\\
&\quad+
f_x x''
+f_y y''.
\end{aligned}
$$
At
$$
u=0,
$$
one has
$$
x'=y'=0,
\qquad
x''=2,
\qquad
y''=0.
$$
Therefore only the term
$$
2f_x(0,0)
$$
remains.
:::

<1>18. Thus the curve determines the nonzero tangent vector
$$
\boxed{
A_\alpha
=
2
\left.
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\right|_{(0,0)}}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>17 identifies the derivation exactly with twice the coordinate tangent
vector
$$
\partial/\partial x.
$$
It is nonzero because, for the coordinate function
$$
f(x,y)=x,
$$
one has
$$
A_\alpha(f)=2.
$$
:::

<1>19. Q.E.D.
