::: {.problem}
Let $D$ be a derivation at $p\in M$.

(a) Suppose $f:M\to\RR$ vanishes on some neighborhood of $p$.
Show that $Df=0$; that is, $D$ is a local operator.

(b) Let $M=\RR^n$ with coordinates $x^1,\ldots,x^n$, and let $D$ be a derivation at $0$.
Show that $D$ is a linear combination of the coordinate derivations $\partial/\partial x^i$, $i=1,\ldots,n$.

You may assume without proof that if $f:\RR^n\to\RR$ is smooth and $f(0)=0$, then there are smooth functions $g_i:\RR^n\to\RR$ such that
$$
f(x^1,\ldots,x^n)=\sum_{i=1}^n x^i g_i(x^1,\ldots,x^n).
$$
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

(a) If $f$ vanishes on a neighborhood of $p$, then $Df=0$.


::: pf-proof

Choose an open neighborhood $U$ of $p$ on which $f=0$. Choose a smooth bump function
$$
\chi:M\to[0,1]
$$
with
$$
\chi(p)=1
$$
and
$$
\operatorname{supp}(\chi)\subset U.
$$
Since $f$ vanishes on $U$ and $\chi$ vanishes outside $U$, one has
$$
f\chi=0
$$
on all of $M$. Applying the derivation $D$ and using the Leibniz rule gives
$$
0=D(f\chi)=f(p)D\chi+\chi(p)Df.
$$
Because $f(p)=0$ and $\chi(p)=1$, this reduces to
$$
Df=0.
$$
Thus the value of $Df$ depends only on the germ of $f$ near $p$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

For part (b), every derivation $D$ at $0\in\RR^n$ annihilates constant functions.


::: pf-proof

Apply the Leibniz rule to the constant function $1$:
$$
D(1)=D(1\cdot1)=1\cdot D(1)+1\cdot D(1)=2D(1).
$$
Hence $D(1)=0$. By linearity, $D(c)=cD(1)=0$ for every constant $c$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Let $f\in C^\infty(\RR^n)$. There are smooth functions $g_i$ such that
$$
f(x)-f(0)=\sum_{i=1}^n x^ig_i(x).
$$


::: pf-proof

The function
$$
h(x)=f(x)-f(0)
$$
satisfies $h(0)=0$. Apply the factorization supplied in the problem statement.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Applying $D$ to the identity in step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} gives
$$
Df=\sum_{i=1}^n g_i(0)D(x^i).
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref},
$$
D(f-f(0))=Df.
$$
Using step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} and the Leibniz rule,
$$
\begin{aligned}
Df
&=\sum_{i=1}^n D(x^ig_i)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(x^i(0)Dg_i+g_i(0)D(x^i)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $x^i(0)=0$, the first terms vanish.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

For every $i$,
$$
g_i(0)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}(0).
$$


::: pf-proof

Restrict the identity of step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} to the $i$-th coordinate axis:
$$
f(te_i)-f(0)
=tg_i(te_i).
$$
For $t\ne0$,
$$
\frac{f(te_i)-f(0)}t=g_i(te_i).
$$
Letting $t\to0$ and using continuity of $g_i$ gives
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}(0)=g_i(0).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
D=\sum_{i=1}^n D(x^i)\evaluated{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}}_0
}.
$$


::: pf-proof

For every smooth $f$, steps [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} give
$$
Df
=\sum_{i=1}^n D(x^i)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}(0).
$$
The right-hand side is exactly the action on $f$ of the displayed linear combination of coordinate derivations. Since the two derivations agree on every smooth function, they are equal.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} proves locality, and step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} proves that every derivation at the origin is a linear combination of the coordinate derivations.

:::
