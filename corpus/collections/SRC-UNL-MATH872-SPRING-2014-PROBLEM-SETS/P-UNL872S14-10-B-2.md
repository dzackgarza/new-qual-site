::: {.problem}
Let G be the group presented by $< \mathsf { a } , \mathsf { b } \mid \mathsf { b } ^ { 2 } = 1 >$

(a) Draw the presentation complex for this presentation, and write this complex as a wedge sum of two familiar spaces.

(b) Draw the Cayley graph and the Cayley complex for this presentation.

(c) Draw a simply connected covering space of the wedge sum of a circle $\mathrm { S } ^ { 1 }$and a 2-sphere$\mathrm { S } ^ { 2 }$

How does this differ from the Cayley complex in part (b)?
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
[
G=langle a,bmid b^2=1angle.
]

<1>1. The presentation complex has one (0)-cell, two (1)-cells (a,b), and one (2)-cell attached along the loop (b^2).

::: {.proof}
This is the standard CW complex associated to a presentation: one vertex, one oriented (1)-cell for each generator, and one (2)-cell for each relator.
:::

<1>2. The subcomplex consisting of the (b)-circle and the (2)-cell attached along (b^2) is homeomorphic to (mathbb{RP}^2).

::: {.proof}
The usual CW structure on (mathbb{RP}^2) has one cell in dimensions (0,1,2), with the (2)-cell attached to the (1)-skeleton (S^1) by the degree-(2) map. This is exactly the attaching map (b^2).
:::

<1>3. Therefore the presentation complex is
[
oxed{S^1eemathbb{RP}^2}.
]

::: {.proof}
The (a)-circle meets the (b)-circle/(2)-cell subcomplex only at the unique (0)-cell. Step <1>2 identifies the second subcomplex with (mathbb{RP}^2), so the whole complex is the indicated wedge.
:::

<1>4. The group itself is
[
oxed{Gcongmathbb Z*mathbb Z/2}.
]

::: {.proof}
The presentation has no relation involving (a), so (a) generates an infinite cyclic free factor. The relation (b^2=1) makes (b) generate a cyclic group of order (2). Hence the presented group is their free product.
:::

<1>5. The Cayley graph has vertex set (G). From each vertex (g) there are oriented (a)-edges
[
glongrightarrow ga,
qquad
glongrightarrow ga^{-1},
]
and two oriented (b)-edges
[
glongrightarrow gb,
qquad
gblongrightarrow g.
]

::: {.proof}
This is the definition of the Cayley graph associated to the generating symbols (a,b) and their inverses. Since
[
b^{-1}=b,
]
the two oriented (b)-edges have the same two endpoints but opposite orientations; they are distinct lifts of the single oriented (b)-cell in the presentation complex.
:::

<1>6. For each pair of vertices
[
{g,gb},
]
the two (b)-edges form a circle.

::: {.proof}
There are exactly two lifted (b)-edges over the base (b)-cell: one from (g) to (gb), and one from (gb) back to (g). Their union is a (2)-edge circle.
:::

<1>7. In the Cayley complex, two lifted (2)-cells are attached to the circle in step <1>6, one on each lift of the relator (b^2). Their union is a (2)-sphere.

::: {.proof}
At the vertex (g), the relator (b^2) lifts to the closed path
[
g	o gb	o g.
]
At the vertex (gb), the same relator gives the same (2)-edge circle with the opposite choice of initial vertex. Thus two distinct lifts of the unique (2)-cell have the same boundary circle. Gluing two disks along their common boundary produces (S^2).
:::

<1>8. Hence the Cayley complex can be described as follows:
[
oxed{	ext{one }S^2	ext{ for each coset }glangle bangle,
	ext{ joined to the others by the }a	ext{-edges}.}
]

::: {.proof}
A coset
[
glangle bangle={g,gb}
]
is exactly the pair of vertices supporting the sphere from step <1>7. The (a)-edges join vertices belonging to different such cosets. Since
[
G=mathbb Z*mathbb Z/2,
]
the incidence pattern of these cosets and (a)-edges is the Bass--Serre tree of the free product.
:::

<1>9. The Cayley complex is simply connected.

::: {.proof}
The Cayley complex of a presentation is the universal cover of its presentation complex. Equivalently, the (1)-skeleton contains precisely the loops generated by conjugates of the relator (b^2), and the lifted (2)-cells fill all of these. Hence its fundamental group is trivial.
:::

<1>10. For part (c), let
[
Z=S^1ee S^2.
]
Its universal cover is obtained from the real line by attaching one copy of (S^2) at every integer point:
[
oxed{
widetilde Z
=
mathbb R
 cupigcup_{ninmathbb Z}S_n^2,
}
]
where (S_n^2) meets the line only at the point (n).

::: {.proof}
The universal cover of the (S^1) summand is
[
mathbb R	o S^1,
qquad
tmapsto e^{2pi it}.
]
Over the simply connected (S^2) summand, the restriction of the universal cover has one component over each lift (n) of the wedge point, and each component maps homeomorphically onto (S^2). Thus a sphere is attached at every integer point of the lifted circle.
:::

<1>11. The space (widetilde Z) is simply connected.

::: {.proof}
The line is contractible and each sphere is simply connected. Any loop has compact image, so it meets only finitely many attached spheres. Repeated van Kampen applied to this finite union shows that its fundamental group is trivial. Hence every loop in (widetilde Z) is nullhomotopic.
:::

<1>12. The essential difference from the Cayley complex in part (b) is:
[
oxed{
egin{array}{l}
	ext{over }S^1ee S^2,	ext{ each lifted }S^2	ext{ meets the lifted }1	ext{-skeleton at one point};\
	ext{over }S^1eemathbb{RP}^2,	ext{ each lifted }S^2	omathbb{RP}^2	ext{ contains two lifts of the wedge point.}
end{array}}
]

::: {.proof}
The (S^2) summand is simply connected, so each component of its inverse image maps homeomorphically to (S^2) and contains one chosen lift of the wedge point.

By contrast, the universal cover
[
S^2	omathbb{RP}^2
]
is (2)-sheeted, so each component over the (mathbb{RP}^2) summand contains two lifts of the wedge point. These are the two vertices ({g,gb}) on the sphere described in steps <1>6--<1>8.
:::
