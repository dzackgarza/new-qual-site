::: {.problem}
Let $f:\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb R$ be differentiable. Suppose that for each $j=1,\dots,n$ the limit
\[
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}(x)
\]
exists.

1. Can $f$ be extended to a continuous map $\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$?
2. Assuming continuity at the origin, is the extension differentiable at the origin?
:::

::: {.solution}
For
$$
j=1,\ldots,n,
$$
write
$$
a_j
\coloneqq
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}(x),
\qquad
a\coloneqq(a_1,\ldots,a_n).
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

The gradient is bounded on some punctured neighborhood of the
origin, and
$$
\nabla f(x)\longrightarrow a
$$
as $x\to0$.

::: pf-proof

For each $j$, the stated finite limit implies that
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}
$$
is bounded on some punctured neighborhood of $0$. Since there are only
finitely many coordinates, there are $\delta>0$ and $M>0$ such that
$$
\|\nabla f(x)\|\le M
$$
whenever
$$
0<\|x\|<\delta.
$$

Coordinatewise convergence in the finite-dimensional space $\RR^n$
is equivalent to convergence in the Euclidean norm, so the same
hypotheses give
$$
\nabla f(x)\to a.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

If $n=1$, the hypotheses do not in general force a continuous
extension to the origin.

::: pf-proof

Define
$$
f(x)
=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
1,&x>0.
\end{cases}
$$
Then $f$ is differentiable on $\RR\setminus\{0\}$ and
$$
f'(x)=0
$$
there, so
$$
\lim_{x\to0}f'(x)=0.
$$
However,
$$
\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=1.
$$
Thus no choice of $f(0)$ makes the function continuous at $0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

Assume $n\ge2$. If
$$
0<\|x\|,\|y\|<\delta,
$$
then there is a piecewise $C^1$ path in
$$
B_\delta(0)\setminus\{0\}
$$
from $x$ to $y$ whose length is at most
$$
(\pi+2)\max\{\|x\|,\|y\|\}.
$$

::: pf-proof

Write
$$
x=r u,
\qquad
y=s v,
$$
where
$$
r=\|x\|,
\qquad
s=\|y\|,
\qquad
\|u\|=\|v\|=1,
$$
and put
$$
\rho=\max\{r,s\}.
$$

First move radially from $ru$ to $\rho u$, then along a shortest
great-circle arc on the sphere of radius $\rho$ from $\rho u$ to
$\rho v$, and finally radially from $\rho v$ to $sv$. Such an arc
exists because $n\ge2$, and its length is at most $\pi\rho$.
The two radial pieces have total length
$$
(\rho-r)+(\rho-s)\le2\rho.
$$
Hence the whole path has length at most
$$
(\pi+2)\rho.
$$
It stays on points of norm between $\min\{r,s\}$ and $\rho$, except
possibly that one radial segment is constant, so it never meets the
origin and lies inside $B_\delta(0)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

If $n\ge2$, then for
$$
0<\|x\|,\|y\|<\delta
$$
one has
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|
\le
M(\pi+2)\max\{\|x\|,\|y\|\}.
$$

::: pf-proof

Take the path from step [](#s3){.pf-ref} and split it into its finitely many smooth
pieces, each parameterized by arclength. On each piece $\gamma$, the
one-variable function
$$
t\longmapsto f(\gamma(t))
$$
is differentiable, and the chain rule with step [](#s1){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\left|
\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))
\right|
=
|\nabla f(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)|
\le
M
$$
because $\|\gamma'(t)\|=1$. The one-variable mean value theorem on each
piece therefore bounds
the change of $f$ by $M$ times the length of that piece. Summing over
the pieces and using step [](#s3){.pf-ref} gives the displayed inequality.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

If $n\ge2$, the limit
$$
L\coloneqq\lim_{x\to0}f(x)
$$
exists.

::: pf-proof

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose
$$
0<\eta<\delta
$$
such that
$$
M(\pi+2)\eta<\varepsilon.
$$
Then step [](#s4){.pf-ref} gives
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon
$$
whenever
$$
0<\|x\|,\|y\|<\eta.
$$
Thus $f$ satisfies the Cauchy criterion for a finite limit at the
puncture. Since $\RR$ is complete, the limit $L$ exists.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

Consequently, for $n\ge2$, defining
$$
F(0)=L,
\qquad
F(x)=f(x)\quad(x\ne0)
$$
gives a continuous extension
$$
F:\RR^n\to\RR.
$$

::: pf-proof

Away from $0$, differentiability of $f$ implies continuity. At $0$,
continuity is exactly the limit statement in step [](#s5){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s7}

Now assume, in any dimension $n\ge1$, that $f$ has been extended
continuously to a function
$$
F:\RR^n\to\RR.
$$
For every $h\ne0$, define
$$
\phi_h(t)
\coloneqq
F(th)-F(0)-t\,a\cdot h
$$
for
$$
0\le t\le1.
$$
Then there is $\theta_h\in(0,1)$ such that
$$
F(h)-F(0)-a\cdot h
=
\bigl(\nabla f(\theta_hh)-a\bigr)\cdot h.
$$

::: pf-proof

Continuity of $F$ makes $\phi_h$ continuous on $[0,1]$. For
$0<t<1$, the point $th$ is nonzero, so differentiability of $f$ and
the chain rule give
$$
\phi_h'(t)
=
\bigl(\nabla f(th)-a\bigr)\cdot h.
$$
The one-variable mean value theorem applied on $[0,1]$ gives some
$\theta_h\in(0,1)$ such that
$$
\phi_h(1)-\phi_h(0)=\phi_h'(\theta_h).
$$
Since $\phi_h(0)=0$, this is exactly the displayed identity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s8}

The continuous extension $F$ is differentiable at $0$, with
derivative
$$
DF(0)(h)=a\cdot h
=
\sum_{j=1}^n a_jh_j.
$$

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s7){.pf-ref} and Cauchy--Schwarz,
$$
\frac{
|F(h)-F(0)-a\cdot h|
}{
\|h\|
}
\le
\|\nabla f(\theta_hh)-a\|.
$$
As $h\to0$,
$$
\|\theta_hh\|
\le
\|h\|
\longrightarrow0.
$$
Therefore step [](#s1){.pf-ref} implies
$$
\|\nabla f(\theta_hh)-a\|
\longrightarrow0.
$$
This is precisely the definition of differentiability of $F$ at $0$
with derivative $h\mapsto a\cdot h$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s9}

The answers are therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{(1)}&
\text{not always if }n=1,\text{ but yes if }n\ge2,\\[2mm]
\text{(2)}&
\text{yes in every dimension once continuity at }0\text{ is assumed.}
\end{array}
}
$$

::: pf-proof

Step [](#s2){.pf-ref} gives the obstruction in dimension $1$. Steps [](#s3){.pf-ref}, [](#s4){.pf-ref}, [](#s5){.pf-ref} and [](#s6){.pf-ref}
prove existence of the continuous extension for $n\ge2$. Steps
[](#s7){.pf-ref} and [](#s8){.pf-ref} prove differentiability under the continuity assumption in
all dimensions.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s9){.pf-ref} gives both requested conclusions.
