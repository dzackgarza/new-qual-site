::: {.problem}
Find all real solutions of
\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=\sqrt y,
\qquad
y(0)=0.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
For $c\in[0,\infty)$, define
$$
y_c(x)
\coloneqq
\begin{cases}
0,&x\le c,\\
\dfrac{(x-c)^2}{4},&x\ge c.
\end{cases}
$$
Also write $y_\infty\equiv0$.

<1>1. Every real solution $y:\RR\to\RR$ satisfies
$$
y(x)\ge0
$$
for all $x$, is nondecreasing, and vanishes for every $x\le0$.

::: {.proof}
The expression $\sqrt{y(x)}$ is real only when $y(x)\ge0$. Hence the
differential equation gives
$$
y'(x)=\sqrt{y(x)}\ge0,
$$
so $y$ is nondecreasing. If $x\le0$, then monotonicity and the initial
condition give
$$
0\le y(x)\le y(0)=0.
$$
Thus $y(x)=0$ for every $x\le0$.
:::

<1>2. If $y$ is not identically zero, there is a unique number
$$
c\in[0,\infty)
$$
such that
$$
y(x)=0\quad(x\le c),
\qquad
y(x)>0\quad(x>c).
$$

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
P\coloneqq\{x\in\RR:y(x)>0\}.
$$
By step <1>1, $P\subseteq(0,\infty)$. Since $y$ is nondecreasing, $P$
is upward closed: if $x\in P$ and $t>x$, then
$$
y(t)\ge y(x)>0,
$$
so $t\in P$.

Assume $P\ne\varnothing$ and put
$$
c\coloneqq\inf P.
$$
Then $c\ge0$. If $x<c$, then $x\notin P$, and since $y(x)\ge0$ we have
$y(x)=0$. Continuity of $y$ gives $y(c)=0$. If $x>c$, the definition of
the infimum gives some $p\in P$ with $c\le p<x$, and monotonicity then
gives
$$
y(x)\ge y(p)>0.
$$
This proves the assertion, and the transition point is unique because it
is the boundary between the zero and positive sets.
:::

<1>3. For the number $c$ from step <1>2,
$$
y(x)=\frac{(x-c)^2}{4}
$$
for every $x>c$.

::: {.proof}
On $(c,\infty)$, step <1>2 gives $y>0$. Hence
$$
u(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{y(x)}
$$
is differentiable there, and the chain rule together with the
differential equation gives
$$
u'(x)
=
\frac{y'(x)}{2\sqrt{y(x)}}
=
\frac12.
$$
Therefore
$$
u(x)=\frac{x}{2}+K
$$
on $(c,\infty)$ for some constant $K$. By continuity of $y$ and
$y(c)=0$ from step <1>2,
$$
\lim_{x\downarrow c}u(x)=0.
$$
Thus $K=-c/2$, so
$$
\sqrt{y(x)}=\frac{x-c}{2}
$$
for $x>c$, and squaring gives the claimed formula.
:::

<1>4. Every solution is one of the functions $y_c$ with
$$
c\in[0,\infty],
$$
where $c=\infty$ denotes the identically-zero solution.

::: {.proof}
If $y\equiv0$, then $y=y_\infty$. Otherwise step <1>2 gives a finite
$c\ge0$. On $x\le c$, that step gives $y=0$, while step <1>3 gives
$$
y(x)=\frac{(x-c)^2}{4}
$$
for $x>c$. Hence $y=y_c$.
:::

<1>5. Conversely, every $y_c$ with $c\in[0,\infty]$ is a solution.

::: {.proof}
For finite $c$, the two defining pieces agree at $x=c$. Their derivatives
are respectively
$$
0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{x-c}{2},
$$
so the one-sided derivatives also agree at $c$ and equal $0$. Thus $y_c$
is differentiable on $\RR$. For $x<c$,
$$
y_c'(x)=0=\sqrt{y_c(x)},
$$
and for $x>c$,
$$
y_c'(x)=\frac{x-c}{2}
=
\sqrt{\frac{(x-c)^2}{4}}
=
\sqrt{y_c(x)},
$$
because $x-c>0$. At $x=c$, both sides are $0$. Since $c\ge0$, also
$y_c(0)=0$.

The function $y_\infty\equiv0$ plainly satisfies the same equation and
initial condition.
:::

<1>6. Therefore the complete set of real solutions on $\RR$ is
$$
\boxed{
y_c(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x\le c,\\
\dfrac{(x-c)^2}{4},&x\ge c,
\end{cases}
\quad c\in[0,\infty),
\qquad
y_\infty\equiv0.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 proves that no other solution exists, and step <1>5 verifies
every displayed function. If a solution is understood to have an arbitrary
interval domain containing $0$, the same argument on that interval shows
that it is the restriction of one of these global solutions.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>6 gives all solutions of the differential equation satisfying
$y(0)=0$.
:::
:::
