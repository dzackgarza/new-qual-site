::: {.problem}
(a) Suppose $(X,\mathcal T)$ and $(Y,\mathcal T')$ are topological spaces, $A\subseteq X$, and
\[
f:(X,\mathcal T)\longrightarrow(Y,\mathcal T')
\]
is a homeomorphism.
Show that the restriction
\[
f|_A:(A,\mathcal T_A)\longrightarrow(f(A),\mathcal T'_{f(A)})
\]
is a homeomorphism for the corresponding subspace topologies.

(b) Show that there is no homeomorphism
\[
[0,1)\longrightarrow(0,1),
\]
where both spaces carry the subspace topology inherited from the usual topology on $\mathbb R$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The restriction
\[
f|_A:A\to f(A)
\]
is bijective.

::: {.proof}
Since $f:X\to Y$ is injective, its restriction to $A$ is injective.
By definition of $f(A)$, every point of $f(A)$ has the form $f(a)$ for some
$a\in A$, so the restriction is surjective onto $f(A)$.
:::

<1>2. The restriction
\[
f|_A:(A,\mathcal T_A)
\longrightarrow
(f(A),\mathcal T'_{f(A)})
\]
is continuous.

::: {.proof}
Let
\[
V\subseteq f(A)
\]
be open in the subspace topology. Then there is an open set
\[
U\in\mathcal T'
\]
such that
\[
V=U\cap f(A).
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
(f|_A)^{-1}(V)
&=
A\cap f^{-1}(U).
\end{aligned}
\]
Because $f$ is continuous,
\[
f^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal T.
\]
Hence
\[
A\cap f^{-1}(U)
\]
is open in the subspace topology on $A$.
:::

<1>3. The inverse of the restriction is
\[
(f|_A)^{-1}
=
f^{-1}|_{f(A)}.
\]

::: {.proof}
If
\[
y\in f(A),
\]
there is a unique $a\in A$ with $f(a)=y$ because $f$ is injective. Thus
both sides send $y$ to that same point $a$.
:::

<1>4. The inverse
\[
(f|_A)^{-1}:f(A)\to A
\]
is continuous.

::: {.proof}
The map
\[
f^{-1}:Y\to X
\]
is continuous because $f$ is a homeomorphism. Apply step <1>2 to
\[
f^{-1}
\]
and the subset
\[
f(A)\subseteq Y.
\]
Its restriction
\[
f^{-1}|_{f(A)}:f(A)\to A
\]
is continuous. By step <1>3, this is exactly $(f|_A)^{-1}$.
:::

<1>5. Hence
\[
\boxed{
f|_A:(A,\mathcal T_A)
\xrightarrow{\cong}
(f(A),\mathcal T'_{f(A)})
}
\]
is a homeomorphism.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 gives bijectivity, step <1>2 gives continuity, and step <1>4
gives continuity of the inverse.
:::

<1>6. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that there is a homeomorphism
\[
h:[0,1)\to(0,1).
\]
Let
\[
y_0=h(0).
\]
Then the restriction
\[
h|_{(0,1)}
:
(0,1)
\longrightarrow
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}
\]
is a homeomorphism.

::: {.proof}
Apply part (a), proved in step <1>5, with
\[
A=[0,1)\setminus\{0\}
=(0,1).
\]
Since $h$ is bijective,
\[
h(A)
=(0,1)\setminus\{h(0)\}
=(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}.
\]
Thus the indicated restriction is a homeomorphism.
:::

<1>7. The interval
\[
(0,1)
\]
is connected.

::: {.proof}
Every interval in $\mathbb R$ is connected in the usual topology. The
space $(0,1)$ carries precisely the corresponding subspace topology.
:::

<1>8. The punctured interval
\[
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}
\]
is disconnected.

::: {.proof}
Because
\[
y_0\in(0,1),
\]
one has the disjoint union
\[
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}
=
(0,y_0)
\sqcup
(y_0,1).
\]
Both sets are nonempty and open in the subspace
\[
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}.
\]
Each is also the complement of the other in that subspace, so both are
closed there. Thus they form a separation.
:::

<1>9. The homeomorphism in step <1>6 cannot exist.

::: {.proof}
Homeomorphisms preserve connectedness. Step <1>6 would make the connected
space from step <1>7 homeomorphic to the disconnected space from step
<1>8, a contradiction.
:::

<1>10. Therefore
\[
\boxed{
[0,1)\not\cong(0,1).
}
\]

::: {.proof}
The assumption that a homeomorphism exists led to the contradiction in
step <1>9.
:::

<1>11. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 proves part (a), and step <1>10 proves part (b).
:::
