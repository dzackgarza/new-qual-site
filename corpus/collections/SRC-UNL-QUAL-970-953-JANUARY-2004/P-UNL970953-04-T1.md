::: {.problem}
(a) Suppose $(X,\mathcal T)$ and $(Y,\mathcal T')$ are topological spaces, $A\subseteq X$, and
\[
f:(X,\mathcal T)\longrightarrow(Y,\mathcal T')
\]
is a homeomorphism.
Show that the restriction
\[
f|_A:(A,\mathcal T_A)\longrightarrow(f(A),\mathcal T'_{f(A)})
\]
is a homeomorphism for the corresponding subspace topologies.

(b) Show that there is no homeomorphism
\[
[0,1)\longrightarrow(0,1),
\]
where both spaces carry the subspace topology inherited from the usual topology on $\RR$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The restriction
\[
f|_A:A\to f(A)
\]
is bijective.


::: pf-proof

Since $f:X\to Y$ is injective, its restriction to $A$ is injective.
By definition of $f(A)$, every point of $f(A)$ has the form $f(a)$ for some
$a\in A$, so the restriction is surjective onto $f(A)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The restriction
\[
f|_A:(A,\mathcal T_A)
\longrightarrow
(f(A),\mathcal T'_{f(A)})
\]
is continuous.


::: pf-proof

Let
\[
V\subseteq f(A)
\]
be open in the subspace topology. Then there is an open set
\[
U\in\mathcal T'
\]
such that
\[
V=U\cap f(A).
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
(f|_A)^{-1}(V)
&=
A\cap f^{-1}(U).
\end{aligned}
\]
Because $f$ is continuous,
\[
f^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal T.
\]
Hence
\[
A\cap f^{-1}(U)
\]
is open in the subspace topology on $A$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The inverse of the restriction is
\[
(f|_A)^{-1}
=
f^{-1}|_{f(A)}.
\]


::: pf-proof

If
\[
y\in f(A),
\]
there is a unique $a\in A$ with $f(a)=y$ because $f$ is injective. Thus
both sides send $y$ to that same point $a$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The inverse
\[
(f|_A)^{-1}:f(A)\to A
\]
is continuous.


::: pf-proof

The map
\[
f^{-1}:Y\to X
\]
is continuous because $f$ is a homeomorphism. Apply step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} to
\[
f^{-1}
\]
and the subset
\[
f(A)\subseteq Y.
\]
Its restriction
\[
f^{-1}|_{f(A)}:f(A)\to A
\]
is continuous. By step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}, this is exactly $(f|_A)^{-1}$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Hence
\[
\boxed{
f|_A:(A,\mathcal T_A)
\xrightarrow{\cong}
(f(A),\mathcal T'_{f(A)})
}
\]
is a homeomorphism.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} gives bijectivity, step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} gives continuity, and step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref}
gives continuity of the inverse.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that there is a homeomorphism
\[
h:[0,1)\to(0,1).
\]
Let
\[
y_0=h(0).
\]
Then the restriction
\[
h|_{(0,1)}
:
(0,1)
\longrightarrow
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}
\]
is a homeomorphism.


::: pf-proof

Apply part (a), proved in step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}, with
\[
A=[0,1)\setminus\{0\}
=(0,1).
\]
Since $h$ is bijective,
\[
h(A)
=(0,1)\setminus\{h(0)\}
=(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}.
\]
Thus the indicated restriction is a homeomorphism.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

The interval
\[
(0,1)
\]
is connected.


::: pf-proof

Every interval in $\RR$ is connected in the usual topology. The
space $(0,1)$ carries precisely the corresponding subspace topology.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The punctured interval
\[
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}
\]
is disconnected.


::: pf-proof

Because
\[
y_0\in(0,1),
\]
one has the disjoint union
\[
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}
=
(0,y_0)
\sqcup
(y_0,1).
\]
Both sets are nonempty and open in the subspace
\[
(0,1)\setminus\{y_0\}.
\]
Each is also the complement of the other in that subspace, so both are
closed there. Thus they form a separation.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

The homeomorphism in step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} cannot exist.


::: pf-proof

Homeomorphisms preserve connectedness. Step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} would make the connected
space from step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} homeomorphic to the disconnected space from step
[](#s1-8){.pf-ref}, a contradiction.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
[0,1)\not\cong(0,1).
}
\]


::: pf-proof

The assumption that a homeomorphism exists led to the contradiction in
step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} proves part (a), and step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} proves part (b).

:::
