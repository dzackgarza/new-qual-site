::: {.problem}
Consider
\[
x'=\frac{x^3-x}{1+e^x}.
\]

1. Find all constant solutions.
2. Discuss $\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)$ for the solution satisfying $x(0)=1/2$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
$$
F(x)\coloneqq\frac{x^3-x}{1+e^x}
=
\frac{x(x-1)(x+1)}{1+e^x}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

The constant solutions are exactly
$$
\boxed{x(t)\equiv-1,\qquad x(t)\equiv0,\qquad x(t)\equiv1.}
$$

::: pf-proof

A constant function $x(t)\equiv c$ solves the equation exactly when
$F(c)=0$. Since $1+e^c>0$ for every real $c$, this is equivalent to
$$
c(c-1)(c+1)=0,
$$
whose solutions are $c=-1,0,1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

On the interval $(0,1)$,
$$
F(x)<0.
$$

::: pf-proof

For $0<x<1$, one has
$$
x>0,
\qquad
x-1<0,
\qquad
x+1>0,
\qquad
1+e^x>0.
$$
The displayed factorization of $F$ therefore gives $F(x)<0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

The solution with $x(0)=1/2$ satisfies
$$
0<x(t)\le\frac12
$$
for every $t\ge0$ in its maximal forward interval, and it is strictly
decreasing there.

::: pf-proof

The vector field $F$ is smooth, so the initial-value problem has locally
unique solutions. While the solution lies in $(0,1)$, step [](#s2){.pf-ref} gives
$x'(t)<0$, hence it decreases from $1/2$.

It cannot reach $0$ at a finite time. If $x(t_0)=0$, then both the given
solution and the constant solution $x\equiv0$ solve the same initial-value
problem at time $t_0$. Uniqueness would force them to agree, contradicting
$x(0)=1/2$. Thus $x(t)>0$, and consequently it remains in $(0,1/2]$ and
is strictly decreasing.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The solution from step [](#s3){.pf-ref} exists for every $t\ge0$.

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s3){.pf-ref}, its graph remains in the compact interval $[0,1/2]$.
The smooth vector field $F$ is bounded and locally Lipschitz on a
neighborhood of that interval. Therefore a finite maximal forward endpoint
would admit continuation of the solution, contradicting maximality. Hence
the forward interval is $[0,\infty)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

The limit
$$
L\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)
$$
exists and satisfies $F(L)=0$.

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s3){.pf-ref}, $x(t)$ is decreasing and bounded below by $0$, so it has a
limit
$$
L\in[0,1/2].
$$
If $F(L)<0$, continuity of $F$ would give numbers $c>0$ and $T$ such that
$$
x'(t)=F(x(t))\le-c
$$
for every $t\ge T$. Integrating would force $x(t)$ eventually below $0$,
contradicting step [](#s3){.pf-ref}. Since $F(x)\le0$ on $[0,1/2]$, the only remaining
possibility is $F(L)=0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

For the solution with $x(0)=1/2$,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)=0.}
$$

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s5){.pf-ref}, the limit is a zero of $F$ in $[0,1/2]$. Step [](#s1){.pf-ref} shows
that the only such zero is $0$.

:::
