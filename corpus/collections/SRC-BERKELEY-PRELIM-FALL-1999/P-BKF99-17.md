::: {.problem}
Show that a group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the additive group $\mathbb Q$ if and only if $G$ is countable and every finite subset of $G$ is contained in an infinite cyclic subgroup of $G$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Every nontrivial subgroup $H\leq(\QQ,+)$ is countable and every
finite subset of $H$ is contained in an infinite cyclic subgroup of $H$.

::: {.proof}
Countability follows from $H\subseteq\QQ$. Let
$$
S=\{q_1,\ldots,q_r\}\subseteq H
$$
be finite. If every $q_i=0$, choose any nonzero $h\in H$; then
$S\subseteq\langle h\rangle$.

Otherwise write the nonzero $q_i$ with a common positive denominator:
$$
q_i=\frac{a_i}{N}.
$$
Let $d$ be the greatest common divisor of the nonzero integers $a_i$. By
Bezout's identity, $d/N$ is an integer linear combination of the $q_i$, so
$$
\frac dN\in H.
$$
Each $q_i$ is an integer multiple of $d/N$. Hence
$$
S\subseteq\left\langle\frac dN\right\rangle,
$$
and this cyclic subgroup is infinite.
:::

<1>2. Suppose $G$ is countable and every finite subset of $G$ is contained
in an infinite cyclic subgroup. Then there is an increasing sequence
$$
C_1\subseteq C_2\subseteq\cdots
$$
of infinite cyclic subgroups whose union is $G$.

::: {.proof}
Enumerate
$$
G=\{g_1,g_2,\ldots\}.
$$
Choose an infinite cyclic subgroup $C_1$ containing $g_1$. Inductively,
suppose
$$
C_n=\langle c_n\rangle
$$
has been chosen and contains $g_1,\ldots,g_n$. By hypothesis, the finite
set $\{c_n,g_{n+1}\}$ is contained in an infinite cyclic subgroup
$C_{n+1}$. Then $C_n=\langle c_n\rangle\subseteq C_{n+1}$ and
$g_{n+1}\in C_{n+1}$. Thus the construction continues, and every $g_n$
belongs to $C_n$, so the union is $G$.
:::

<1>3. There are injective homomorphisms
$$
\phi_n:C_n\longrightarrow\QQ
$$
such that
$$
\phi_{n+1}|_{C_n}=\phi_n.
$$

::: {.proof}
Choose a generator $c_n$ of each $C_n$. Since
$C_n\subseteq C_{n+1}$, there is a nonzero integer $m_n$ such that
$$
c_n=m_n c_{n+1}.
$$
Define recursively nonzero rational numbers $r_n$ by
$$
r_1=1,
\qquad
r_{n+1}=\frac{r_n}{m_n},
$$
and define
$$
\phi_n(kc_n)=kr_n
\qquad(k\in\ZZ).
$$
Each $\phi_n$ is an injective homomorphism. Moreover,
$$
\phi_{n+1}(c_n)
=
\phi_{n+1}(m_nc_{n+1})
=
m_nr_{n+1}
=
r_n
=
\phi_n(c_n),
$$
so the two homomorphisms agree on the generator of $C_n$ and therefore on
all of $C_n$.
:::

<1>4. The compatible maps in step <1>3 define an injective homomorphism
$$
\phi:G\longrightarrow\QQ.
$$

::: {.proof}
For $g\in G$, choose $n$ with $g\in C_n$ and set
$$
\phi(g)=\phi_n(g).
$$
Compatibility in step <1>3 makes this independent of the choice of $n$.
Given $g,h\in G$, both lie in some common $C_N$, so
$$
\phi(g+h)=\phi_N(g+h)=\phi_N(g)+\phi_N(h)=\phi(g)+\phi(h).
$$
If $\phi(g)=0$, choose $n$ with $g\in C_n$. Then
$\phi_n(g)=0$, and injectivity of $\phi_n$ gives $g=0$. Thus $\phi$ is
injective.
:::

<1>5. The group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $(\QQ,+)$.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>4, $\phi$ identifies $G$ with the subgroup $\phi(G)$ of
$(\QQ,+)$.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
For nontrivial groups, step <1>1 proves the forward implication, and steps
<1>2--<1>5 prove the converse.
:::
:::

::: {.remark}
The trivial group $G=\{0\}$ is a subgroup of $(\QQ,+)$, but its finite
subset $\{0\}$ is not contained in any infinite cyclic subgroup of $G$. The
equivalence holds for every nontrivial group $G$.
:::
