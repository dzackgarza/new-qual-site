::: {.problem}
(a) Define absolute continuity of a function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ and of a function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$.

(b) Show that if $f$ and $g$ are absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$, $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$, $a<b$, then $f\cdot g$ is absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$.

(c) Give an example to show that (b) is false if $[a,b]$ is replaced by $\mathbb{R}$.
:::


::: {.solution}
<1>1. A function $f:I\to\mathbb R$, where $I$ is either $[a,b]$ or $\mathbb R$, is absolutely continuous if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ such that for every finite family of pairwise disjoint intervals $(x_k,y_k)\subset I$ satisfying
\[
\sum_{k=1}^N (y_k-x_k)<\delta,
\]
one has
\[
\sum_{k=1}^N |f(y_k)-f(x_k)|<\varepsilon.
\]
::: {.proof}
This is the definition of absolute continuity on either domain. For $I=\mathbb R$, the same $\delta$ must work simultaneously for finite disjoint families located anywhere on the real line.
:::

<1>2. If $f$ and $g$ are absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$, then $fg$ is absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$.
::: {.proof}
Absolute continuity implies continuity. Since $[a,b]$ is compact,
\[
M_f:=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)|<\infty,
\qquad
M_g:=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|g(x)|<\infty.
\]
Fix $\varepsilon>0$.

If $M_f=0$ or $M_g=0$, then $fg\equiv0$, so there is nothing to prove. Assume both are positive.
By absolute continuity of $f$ and $g$, choose $\delta_f,\delta_g>0$ such that
\[
\sum_k(y_k-x_k)<\delta_f
\implies
\sum_k|f(y_k)-f(x_k)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2M_g},
\]
and
\[
\sum_k(y_k-x_k)<\delta_g
\implies
\sum_k|g(y_k)-g(x_k)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2M_f}.
\]
Set $\delta=\min\{\delta_f,\delta_g\}$.
For any finite pairwise disjoint family $(x_k,y_k)\subset[a,b]$ with total length less than $\delta$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_k|f(y_k)g(y_k)-f(x_k)g(x_k)|\\
&\le
\sum_k |f(y_k)|\,|g(y_k)-g(x_k)|
+
\sum_k |g(x_k)|\,|f(y_k)-f(x_k)|\\
&\le
M_f\sum_k|g(y_k)-g(x_k)|
+
M_g\sum_k|f(y_k)-f(x_k)|\\
&<
M_f\frac{\varepsilon}{2M_f}
+
M_g\frac{\varepsilon}{2M_g}
=\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence $fg$ is absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$.
:::

<1>3. The corresponding statement on $\mathbb R$ is false.
::: {.proof}
Take
\[
f(x)=g(x)=x.
\]
Both functions are $1$-Lipschitz, hence absolutely continuous on $\mathbb R$.
Their product is
\[
(fg)(x)=x^2.
\]
Every absolutely continuous function on $\mathbb R$ is uniformly continuous: in the definition, take a family consisting of a single interval $(x,y)$. Thus for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $|x-y|<\delta$ implies $|h(x)-h(y)|<\varepsilon$.

But $x^2$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R$. Indeed, let
\[
x_n=n,
\qquad
y_n=n+\frac1n.
\]
Then
\[
|x_n-y_n|=\frac1n\longrightarrow0,
\]
while
\[
|y_n^2-x_n^2|
=
\left(n+\frac1n\right)^2-n^2
=2+\frac1{n^2}
\longrightarrow2.
\]
Therefore $x^2$ is not uniformly continuous, hence cannot be absolutely continuous on $\mathbb R$.
