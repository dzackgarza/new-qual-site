::: {.problem}
State the Open Mapping Theorem and the Closed Graph Theorem for Banach spaces.
Derive the Open Mapping Theorem from the Closed Graph Theorem.
:::


::: {.solution}
<1>1. State the two theorems.
::: {.proof}
**Open Mapping Theorem.** If $X$ and $Y$ are Banach spaces and
\[
T:X\to Y
\]
is bounded, linear, and surjective, then $T$ is an open map.

**Closed Graph Theorem.** If $X$ and $Y$ are Banach spaces and
\[
S:X\to Y
\]
is linear with closed graph
\[
\Gamma(S)=\{(x,Sx):x\in X\}\subseteq X\times Y,
\]
then $S$ is bounded.
:::

<1>2. Let $T:X\to Y$ be bounded, linear, and surjective. The quotient $X/\ker T$ is Banach, and $T$ induces a bounded linear bijection
\[
\widetilde T:X/\ker T\longrightarrow Y,
\qquad
\widetilde T(x+\ker T)=Tx.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $T$ is continuous,
\[
\ker T=T^{-1}(\{0\})
\]
is closed. Therefore $X/\ker T$ is Banach with the quotient norm
\[
\|x+\ker T\|_q=\inf_{z\in\ker T}\|x-z\|.
\]
The map $\widetilde T$ is well defined because $Tx=Tx'$ whenever $x-x'\in\ker T$; it is injective by construction and surjective because $T$ is surjective.
Moreover,
\[
\|\widetilde T(x+\ker T)\|
=\|Tx\|
\le \|T\|\,\|x-z\|
\]
for every $z\in\ker T$.
Taking the infimum over $z$ gives
\[
\|\widetilde T(x+\ker T)\|
\le \|T\|\,\|x+\ker T\|_q,
\]
so $\widetilde T$ is bounded.
:::

<1>3. The inverse
\[
S:=\widetilde T^{-1}:Y\to X/\ker T
\]
has closed graph.
::: {.proof}
Suppose
\[
y_n\to y\quad\text{in }Y
\]
and
\[
S y_n\to u\quad\text{in }X/\ker T.
\]
Since $\widetilde T$ is bounded,
\[
\widetilde T(Sy_n)\to \widetilde T(u).
\]
But
\[
\widetilde T(Sy_n)=y_n\to y.
\]
Limits in the Banach space $Y$ are unique, hence
\[
\widetilde T(u)=y.
\]
Applying $S$ gives
\[
u=Sy.
\]
Thus $(y,u)$ belongs to the graph of $S$, and the graph is closed.
:::

<1>4. By the Closed Graph Theorem, $S$ is bounded.
::: {.proof}
Both $Y$ and $X/\ker T$ are Banach spaces, and by <1>3 the graph of $S$ is closed. Therefore the Closed Graph Theorem gives a constant $C\ge0$ such that
\[
\|Sy\|_q\le C\|y\|
\qquad(y\in Y).
\]
If $Y=\{0\}$, the open-mapping conclusion is trivial. Assume henceforth that $Y\ne\{0\}$; then we may take $C>0$.
:::

<1>5. $T$ maps the open unit ball of $X$ onto a set containing a neighborhood of $0$ in $Y$.
::: {.proof}
Set
\[
\delta=\frac1{2C}.
\]
Let $y\in Y$ satisfy $\|y\|<\delta$.
Then
\[
\|Sy\|_q\le C\|y\|<\frac12.
\]
Write
\[
Sy=x+\ker T.
\]
By the definition of the quotient norm, there exists $z\in\ker T$ such that
\[
\|x-z\|<1.
\]
Because $\widetilde T(Sy)=y$,
\[
y=Tx=T(x-z).
\]
Thus
\[
y\in T(B_X(0,1)).
\]
Hence
\[
B_Y(0,\delta)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)).
\]
:::

<1>6. Therefore $T$ is open.
::: {.proof}
Let $U\subseteq X$ be open and let $y_0\in T(U)$. Choose $x_0\in U$ with $Tx_0=y_0$.
There exists $r>0$ such that
\[
x_0+rB_X(0,1)\subseteq U.
\]
By <1>5,
\[
B_Y(0,\delta)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)),
\]
so by linearity
\[
y_0+rB_Y(0,\delta)
\subseteq
T(x_0+rB_X(0,1))
\subseteq T(U).
\]
Thus $T(U)$ is a neighborhood of each of its points, hence is open. This proves the Open Mapping Theorem from the Closed Graph Theorem.
:::
:::
