::: {.problem}
a. What does it mean for a finite group $G$ to be solvable?

b. Prove that $S_4$ is solvable.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. A finite group $G$ is solvable if there exists a subnormal series $1=G_0\trianglelefteq G_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_k=G$ whose successive quotients $G_{i+1}/G_i$ are abelian.
::: {.proof}
This is the definition of solvability. Equivalently, the derived series $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(j+1)}=[G^{(j)},G^{(j)}]$ terminates at the identity.
:::

<1>2. The subgroup $V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ is normal in $A_4$ and $S_4$.
::: {.proof}
It is closed under multiplication, and conjugation by any permutation sends a double transposition to another double transposition. Hence $V_4$ is normal in $S_4$, and therefore also in $A_4$. Its three nonidentity elements have order two, so $V_4\cong C_2\times C_2$.
:::

<1>3. The series $1\trianglelefteq V_4\trianglelefteq A_4\trianglelefteq S_4$ has abelian successive quotients.
::: {.proof}
The normalities follow from step <1>2 and the kernel description $A_4=\ker(\operatorname{sgn})$. The successive quotients have orders four, three, and two, respectively. The first is $V_4$, hence abelian; the other two have prime order and are cyclic, hence abelian.
:::

<1>4. The group $S_4$ is solvable.
::: {.proof}
Step <1>3 exhibits the subnormal series required by the definition in step <1>1.
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>4 answer parts (a) and (b), respectively.
:::
