::: {.problem}
a. What does it mean for a finite group $G$ to be solvable?

b. Prove that $S_4$ is solvable.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

A finite group $G$ is solvable if there exists a subnormal series $1=G_0\trianglelefteq G_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_k=G$ whose successive quotients $G_{i+1}/G_i$ are abelian.

::: pf-proof

This is the definition of solvability. Equivalently, the derived series $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(j+1)}=[G^{(j)},G^{(j)}]$ terminates at the identity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

The subgroup $V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ is normal in $A_4$ and $S_4$.

::: pf-proof

It is closed under multiplication, and conjugation by any permutation sends a double transposition to another double transposition. Hence $V_4$ is normal in $S_4$, and therefore also in $A_4$. Its three nonidentity elements have order two, so $V_4\cong C_2\times C_2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

The series $1\trianglelefteq V_4\trianglelefteq A_4\trianglelefteq S_4$ has abelian successive quotients.

::: pf-proof

The normalities follow from step [](#s2){.pf-ref} and the kernel description $A_4=\ker(\operatorname{sgn})$. The successive quotients have orders four, three, and two, respectively. The first is $V_4$, hence abelian; the other two have prime order and are cyclic, hence abelian.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

The group $S_4$ is solvable.

::: pf-proof

Step [](#s3){.pf-ref} exhibits the subnormal series required by the definition in step [](#s1){.pf-ref}.

:::
