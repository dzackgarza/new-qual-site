::: {.problem}
For which positive integers $n$ is there a $2\times2$ matrix
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}
$$
with integer entries and order $n$; that is, $A^n=I$ but $A^k\ne I$ for $0<k<n$?
:::

::: {.solution}
The possible orders are
$$
n\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #minimal-polynomial}
If $A\in M_2(\ZZ)$ has finite order $n$, then its minimal polynomial over $\QQ$ is a product of distinct cyclotomic polynomials.

::: pf-proof
Since $A^n=I$, the minimal polynomial $m_A(x)$ divides $x^n-1$. Over characteristic zero,
$$
\gcd(x^n-1,nx^{n-1})=1,
$$
so $x^n-1$ has no repeated irreducible factor. Its irreducible factors over $\QQ$ are precisely the cyclotomic polynomials $\Phi_d(x)$ for $d\mid n$. Hence $m_A$ is a product of distinct such factors.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Every eigenvalue of $A$ has order in $\{1,2,3,4,6\}$.

::: pf-proof
If an eigenvalue $\lambda$ has order $d$, then its minimal polynomial over $\QQ$ is $\Phi_d$. Since $\Phi_d\mid m_A$ and $\deg m_A\leq2$,
$$
\varphi(d)=\deg\Phi_d\leq2.
$$
The positive integers with Euler totient at most $2$ are
$$
d=1,2,3,4,6.
$$
Indeed $\varphi(d)=1$ gives $d=1,2$, while $\varphi(d)=2$ gives $d=3,4,6$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The order of $A$ itself lies in $\{1,2,3,4,6\}$.

::: pf-proof
By [](#minimal-polynomial){.pf-ref}, $m_A$ is squarefree, so $A$ is diagonalizable over $\CC$. Therefore the order of $A$ is the least common multiple of the orders of its eigenvalues.

If $A$ has an eigenvalue of order $3$, $4$, or $6$, its cyclotomic polynomial already has degree $2$ and hence equals $m_A$; both eigenvalues are its two conjugate roots and have the same order. Thus $A$ has order $3$, $4$, or $6$.

Otherwise both eigenvalues lie in $\{1,-1\}$, so their orders divide $2$ and $A$ has order $1$ or $2$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Each of the five listed orders occurs for an integer matrix.

::: pf-proof
The following matrices have the indicated orders:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
1&I\\[2mm]
2&-I\\[2mm]
3&\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\\[4mm]
4&\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\\[4mm]
6&\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{array}
$$
For the last three matrices, the characteristic polynomials are respectively
$$
\Phi_3(x)=x^2+x+1,\qquad
\Phi_4(x)=x^2+1,\qquad
\Phi_6(x)=x^2-x+1.
$$
Their eigenvalues are primitive roots of unity of orders $3$, $4$, and $6$, respectively, so the matrices have exactly those orders.
:::
