::: problem
Let $M$ be a real $3\times3$ matrix such that $M^3=I$ and $M\ne I$.

(a) What are the eigenvalues of $M$?

(b) Give an example of such a matrix.
:::

::: solution
Let
$$
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
$$
so the three cube roots of unity are
$$
1,\omega,\omega^2,
$$
with $\overline\omega=\omega^2$.

<1>1. Determine the eigenvalues of $M$.
::: proof
If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $M$ with eigenvector $v\ne0$, then
$$
Mv=\lambda v.
$$
Applying $M^3=I$ gives
$$
v=M^3v=\lambda^3v,
$$
so
$$
\lambda^3=1.
$$
Thus every eigenvalue belongs to
$$
\{1,\omega,\omega^2\}.
$$

Because $M$ is real, its characteristic polynomial has real coefficients.
Hence nonreal roots occur with their complex conjugates and with the same
multiplicity. If all three eigenvalues were $1$, then the minimal polynomial
of $M$ would divide both $(x-1)^3$ and
$$
x^3-1=(x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2).
$$
The latter polynomial has distinct roots, so their greatest common divisor is
$x-1$; consequently $M=I$, contrary to the hypothesis.

Therefore at least one of $\omega,\omega^2$ occurs. Reality forces both to
occur, once each, and the remaining third eigenvalue must be $1$. Hence the
eigenvalues are exactly
$$
\boxed{1,\omega,\omega^2.}
$$
:::

<1>2. Give an explicit example.
::: proof
Take the permutation matrix
$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It cyclically permutes the standard basis vectors, so applying it three times
returns every basis vector to itself. Thus
$$
M^3=I,
$$
while plainly $M\ne I$. This is a real $3\times3$ example with the required
properties.
:::
:::
