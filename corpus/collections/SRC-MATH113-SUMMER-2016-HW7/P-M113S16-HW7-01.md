::: {.problem}
Compute the minimal polynomials and find the degrees of the following simple extensions of $\mathbb Q$:

(a) $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$;

(b) $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3+i)$;

(c) $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2-\sqrt{10})$;

(d) $\mathbb Q(e^{2\pi i/p})$, for $p$ an odd prime.
:::

::: pf

::: pf-step

For
$$
\alpha=\sqrt{-3},
$$
one has
$$
\boxed{m_\alpha(x)=x^2+3}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Clearly
$$
\alpha^2+3=0.
$$
The polynomial
$$
x^2+3
$$
has no rational root, so it is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.
Hence it is the minimal polynomial.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3}):\mathbb Q]=2.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

The degree of a simple algebraic extension equals the degree of the minimal polynomial of its generator.
Apply step <1>1.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let
$$
\beta=\sqrt3+i.
$$
Then
$$
\beta
$$
is a root of
$$
\boxed{
x^4-4x^2+16.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Compute
$$
\beta^2
=(\sqrt3+i)^2
=
2+2\sqrt3\,i.
$$
Thus
$$
\beta^2-2
=
2\sqrt3\,i.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
(\beta^2-2)^2
=
-12.
$$
Hence
$$
\beta^4-4\beta^2+4+12=0,
$$
so
$$
\beta^4-4\beta^2+16=0.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
\mathbb Q(\beta)
=
\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i).
$$


::: pf-proof

The inclusion
$$
\mathbb Q(\beta)
\subseteq
\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i)
$$
is immediate.

Also
$$
\beta(\sqrt3-i)
=(\sqrt3+i)(\sqrt3-i)
=
4.
$$
Thus
$$
\frac4\beta
=
\sqrt3-i.
$$
Therefore
$$
\sqrt3
=
\frac12
\left(
\beta+\frac4\beta
\right)
\in
\mathbb Q(\beta),
$$
and
$$
i
=
\frac12
\left(
\beta-\frac4\beta
\right)
\in
\mathbb Q(\beta).
$$
Hence the reverse inclusion also holds.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The extension
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i)/\mathbb Q
$$
has degree $4$.


::: pf-proof

One has
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt3):\mathbb Q]=2.
$$
Moreover,
$$
i\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)
$$
because every element of
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)
$$
is real.
Thus
$$
x^2+1
$$
remains irreducible over
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt3),
$$
so
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i):\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)]
=
2.
$$
By the tower law,
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i):\mathbb Q]
=
2\cdot2
=
4.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
m_\beta(x)
=
x^4-4x^2+16,
\qquad
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt3+i):\mathbb Q]
=
4.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Step <1>3 gives a monic polynomial of degree $4$ annihilating $\beta$.
Steps <1>4--<1>5 show
$$
[\mathbb Q(\beta):\mathbb Q]=4.
$$
Hence the minimal polynomial has degree $4$ and must equal the polynomial from step <1>3.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let
$$
\gamma=\sqrt2-\sqrt{10}.
$$
Then $\gamma$ is a root of
$$
\boxed{
x^4-24x^2+64.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Compute
$$
\gamma^2
=
2+10-2\sqrt{20}
=
12-4\sqrt5.
$$
Thus
$$
\gamma^2-12
=
-4\sqrt5.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
(\gamma^2-12)^2
=
80.
$$
Hence
$$
\gamma^4-24\gamma^2+144-80=0,
$$
so
$$
\gamma^4-24\gamma^2+64=0.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
\mathbb Q(\gamma)
=
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt5).
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
\sqrt{10}
=
\sqrt2\,\sqrt5,
$$
one has
$$
\gamma
\in
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt5).
$$
Thus
$$
\mathbb Q(\gamma)
\subseteq
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt5).
$$

From step <1>7,
$$
\gamma^2
=
12-4\sqrt5,
$$
so
$$
\sqrt5
=
\frac{12-\gamma^2}{4}
\in
\mathbb Q(\gamma).
$$
Now
$$
\gamma
=
\sqrt2(1-\sqrt5).
$$
Since
$$
1-\sqrt5\ne0,
$$
it is invertible in the field
$$
\mathbb Q(\gamma).
$$
Hence
$$
\sqrt2
=
\frac{\gamma}{1-\sqrt5}
\in
\mathbb Q(\gamma).
$$
Thus the reverse inclusion holds.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The extension
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt5)/\mathbb Q
$$
has degree $4$.


::: pf-proof

One has
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2):\mathbb Q]=2.
$$
It remains to show
$$
\sqrt5\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$
Suppose
$$
\sqrt5=a+b\sqrt2
$$
with
$$
a,b\in\mathbb Q.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
5
=
a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2.
$$
Thus
$$
2ab=0.
$$
If
$$
b=0,
$$
then
$$
\sqrt5=a\in\mathbb Q,
$$
impossible.
If
$$
a=0,
$$
then
$$
5=2b^2,
$$
so
$$
b^2=5/2,
$$
which has no rational solution.
Therefore
$$
\sqrt5\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$
Hence
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt5):\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)]
=
2.
$$
The tower law gives degree $4$ over $\mathbb Q$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
m_\gamma(x)
=
x^4-24x^2+64,
\qquad
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2-\sqrt{10}):\mathbb Q]
=
4.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Step <1>7 gives a monic quartic annihilating $\gamma$.
Steps <1>8--<1>9 show that the simple extension generated by $\gamma$ has degree $4$.
Thus the quartic is the minimal polynomial.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let
$$
\zeta_p
\coloneqq
e^{2\pi i/p}.
$$
Then $\zeta_p$ is a root of
$$
\Phi_p(x)
\coloneqq
1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
\zeta_p^p=1
$$
and
$$
\zeta_p\ne1,
$$
one has
$$
0
=
\frac{\zeta_p^p-1}{\zeta_p-1}
=
1+\zeta_p+\cdots+\zeta_p^{p-1}.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The polynomial
$$
\Phi_p(x)
=
1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}
$$
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.


::: pf-proof

Consider
$$
\Phi_p(x+1)
=
\frac{(x+1)^p-1}{x}.
$$
Expanding,
$$
\Phi_p(x+1)
=
x^{p-1}
+
\binom p{p-1}x^{p-2}
+
\cdots
+
\binom p2 x
+
p.
$$

For a prime $p$,
$$
p
\mid
\binom pk
$$
for
$$
1\le k\le p-1.
$$
Thus every nonleading coefficient is divisible by $p$.
The constant term is exactly $p$, so it is not divisible by
$$
p^2.
$$
By Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $p$,
$$
\Phi_p(x+1)
$$
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

Translation
$$
x\longmapsto x+1
$$
is an automorphism of
$$
\mathbb Q[x],
$$
so irreducibility of
$$
\Phi_p(x+1)
$$
implies irreducibility of
$$
\Phi_p(x).
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
m_{\zeta_p}(x)
=
x^{p-1}+x^{p-2}+\cdots+x+1,
\qquad
[\mathbb Q(\zeta_p):\mathbb Q]
=
p-1.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Step <1>11 shows that
$$
\zeta_p
$$
is a root of
$$
\Phi_p.
$$
Step <1>12 shows that this polynomial is irreducible.
Its degree is
$$
p-1.
$$
Thus it is the minimal polynomial, and the extension degree is
$$
p-1.
$$

:::
