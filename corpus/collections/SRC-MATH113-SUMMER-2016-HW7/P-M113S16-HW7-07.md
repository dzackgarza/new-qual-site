::: {.problem}
Let
\[
K=\mathbb Q(i\sqrt[4]{2}),\qquad L=\mathbb Q(i\sqrt[4]{2},\sqrt3),
\]
so that $\mathbb Q\subset K\subset L$.

(a) Give an example of an embedding of $K$ which is not an automorphism.

(b) Give an example of an automorphism of $L$ which does not fix $K$ pointwise.
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
$$
\alpha
\coloneqq
i\sqrt[4]{2}.
$$
Then
$$
K=\mathbb Q(\alpha).
$$

<1>1. The element $\alpha$ has minimal polynomial
$$
\boxed{x^4-2}
$$
over $\mathbb Q$.

::: {.proof}
One has
$$
\alpha^4
=
i^4
\left(
\sqrt[4]{2}
\right)^4
=
2.
$$
Thus $\alpha$ is a root of
$$
x^4-2.
$$
This polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $2$.
Hence it is the minimal polynomial.
:::

<1>2. Let
$$
\beta
\coloneqq
\sqrt[4]{2}.
$$
There is a unique $\mathbb Q$-embedding
$$
\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C
$$
such that
$$
\sigma(\alpha)=\beta.
$$

::: {.proof}
The element $\beta$ is another root of the minimal polynomial
$$
x^4-2.
$$
For a simple algebraic extension
$$
K
=
\mathbb Q(\alpha)
\cong
\mathbb Q[x]/(x^4-2),
$$
choosing any root of the minimal polynomial in $\mathbb C$ determines a unique $\mathbb Q$-embedding into $\mathbb C$.
Thus
$$
\alpha\mapsto\beta
$$
defines the required embedding.
:::

<1>3. The image of $\sigma$ is
$$
\mathbb Q(\beta)
\subset
\mathbb R.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since
$$
\sigma
$$
fixes $\mathbb Q$ and sends the generator $\alpha$ to $\beta$,
$$
\sigma(K)
=
\mathbb Q(\beta).
$$
The element
$$
\beta=\sqrt[4]{2}
$$
is real, so
$$
\mathbb Q(\beta)\subset\mathbb R.
$$
:::

<1>4. The field $K$ is not contained in $\mathbb R$.

::: {.proof}
The generator
$$
\alpha
=
i\sqrt[4]{2}
$$
is nonreal.
Since
$$
\alpha\in K,
$$
the field $K$ is not a subfield of $\mathbb R$.
:::

<1>5. Therefore $\sigma$ is not an automorphism of $K$.

::: {.proof}
An automorphism of $K$ must map $K$ onto itself.
But by step <1>3,
$$
\sigma(K)
\subset
\mathbb R,
$$
while step <1>4 shows
$$
K
\not\subset
\mathbb R.
$$
Hence
$$
\sigma(K)\ne K.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{
\sigma:
i\sqrt[4]{2}
\longmapsto
\sqrt[4]{2}
}
$$
is a $\mathbb Q$-embedding of $K$ that is not an automorphism.
This solves part (a).
:::

<1>6. The map
$$
\tau:K\to K,
\qquad
\tau(\alpha)=-\alpha
$$
is a $\mathbb Q$-automorphism.

::: {.proof}
The element
$$
-\alpha
$$
is also a root of
$$
x^4-2.
$$
Thus
$$
\alpha\mapsto-\alpha
$$
defines a $\mathbb Q$-embedding
$$
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C.
$$
Its image is
$$
\mathbb Q(-\alpha)
=
\mathbb Q(\alpha)
=
K.
$$
Hence it is an automorphism.
Moreover,
$$
\tau^2=\operatorname{id}_K.
$$
:::

<1>7. Every element of $K$ has a unique expression
$$
a+b\alpha+c\alpha^2+d\alpha^3,
\qquad
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Q.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1,
$$
[K:\mathbb Q]=4.
$$
Thus
$$
1,\alpha,\alpha^2,\alpha^3
$$
is a $\mathbb Q$-basis of $K$.
:::

<1>8. The real subfield of $K$ is
$$
\boxed{
K\cap\mathbb R
=
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Write
$$
r
\coloneqq
\sqrt[4]{2}.
$$
Then
$$
\alpha=ir,
\qquad
\alpha^2=-\sqrt2,
\qquad
\alpha^3=-i\,2^{3/4}.
$$
Thus an element of $K$ has the form
$$
a-c\sqrt2
+
i
\left(
br-d\,2^{3/4}
\right),
$$
with
$$
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Q.
$$
The imaginary part vanishes exactly when
$$
br-d\,2^{3/4}=0.
$$
Factoring
$$
r\ne0,
$$
this becomes
$$
b-d\sqrt2=0.
$$
Since
$$
1,\sqrt2
$$
are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$,
$$
b=d=0.
$$
Hence every real element of $K$ lies in
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$
The reverse inclusion holds because
$$
\alpha^2=-\sqrt2\in K.
$$
:::

<1>9. One has
$$
\sqrt3
\notin
K.
$$

::: {.proof}
If
$$
\sqrt3
\in
K,
$$
then since $\sqrt3$ is real, step <1>8 would give
$$
\sqrt3
\in
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$
Suppose
$$
\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2
$$
with
$$
a,b\in\mathbb Q.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
3
=
a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2.
$$
Hence
$$
ab=0.
$$
If
$$
b=0,
$$
then $\sqrt3\in\mathbb Q$, impossible.
If
$$
a=0,
$$
then
$$
b^2=3/2,
$$
which has no rational solution.
Thus
$$
\sqrt3\notin K.
$$
:::

<1>10. Therefore
$$
[L:K]=2,
$$
and every element of $L$ has a unique expression
$$
u+v\sqrt3,
\qquad
u,v\in K.
$$

::: {.proof}
The element $\sqrt3$ satisfies
$$
x^2-3.
$$
By step <1>9, it is not in $K$, so its minimal polynomial over $K$ has degree $2$.
Hence
$$
L
=
K(\sqrt3)
$$
is quadratic over $K$, with basis
$$
1,\sqrt3.
$$
:::

<1>11. Define
$$
\widetilde\tau:L\to L
$$
by
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde\tau
\left(
u+v\sqrt3
\right)
=
\tau(u)
+
\tau(v)\sqrt3.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>10 gives a unique representation
$$
u+v\sqrt3,
$$
so the formula is well-defined.
It fixes $\sqrt3$ and extends the automorphism
$$
\tau
$$
on $K$.
Because $\tau$ preserves addition and multiplication, direct expansion gives
$$
\widetilde\tau(x+y)
=
\widetilde\tau(x)
+
\widetilde\tau(y)
$$
and
$$
\widetilde\tau(xy)
=
\widetilde\tau(x)
\widetilde\tau(y).
$$
Also
$$
\widetilde\tau^2
=
\operatorname{id}_L,
$$
so the map is bijective.
Hence it is an automorphism of $L$.
:::

<1>12. The automorphism $\widetilde\tau$ does not fix $K$ pointwise.

::: {.proof}
The element
$$
\alpha
=
i\sqrt[4]{2}
\in
K.
$$
By construction,
$$
\widetilde\tau(\alpha)
=
\tau(\alpha)
=
-\alpha
\ne
\alpha.
$$
Thus $\widetilde\tau$ does not fix $K$ pointwise.
:::

<1>13. Therefore an answer to part (b) is
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde\tau
\left(
i\sqrt[4]{2}
\right)
=
-i\sqrt[4]{2},
\qquad
\widetilde\tau(\sqrt3)
=
\sqrt3.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>11 proves this assignment extends to an automorphism of $L$, and step <1>12 proves it does not fix $K$ pointwise.
:::

<1>14. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 solves part (a), and step <1>13 solves part (b).
:::
