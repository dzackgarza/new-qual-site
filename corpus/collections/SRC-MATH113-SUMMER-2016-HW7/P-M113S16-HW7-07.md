::: {.problem}
Let
\[
K=\QQ(i\sqrt[4]{2}),\qquad L=\QQ(i\sqrt[4]{2},\sqrt3),
\]
so that $\QQ\subset K\subset L$.

(a) Give an example of an embedding of $K$ which is not an automorphism.

(b) Give an example of an automorphism of $L$ which does not fix $K$ pointwise.
:::

::: {.solution}

Set
$$
\alpha
\coloneqq
i\sqrt[4]{2}.
$$
Then
$$
K=\QQ(\alpha).
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The element $\alpha$ has minimal polynomial
$$
\boxed{x^4-2}
$$
over $\QQ$.


::: pf-proof

One has
$$
\alpha^4
=
i^4
\left(
\sqrt[4]{2}
\right)^4
=
2.
$$
Thus $\alpha$ is a root of
$$
x^4-2.
$$
This polynomial is irreducible over $\QQ$ by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $2$.
Hence it is the minimal polynomial.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let
$$
\beta
\coloneqq
\sqrt[4]{2}.
$$
There is a unique $\QQ$-embedding
$$
\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\CC
$$
such that
$$
\sigma(\alpha)=\beta.
$$


::: pf-proof

The element $\beta$ is another root of the minimal polynomial
$$
x^4-2.
$$
For a simple algebraic extension
$$
K
=
\QQ(\alpha)
\cong
\QQ[x]/(x^4-2),
$$
choosing any root of the minimal polynomial in $\CC$ determines a unique $\QQ$-embedding into $\CC$.
Thus
$$
\alpha\mapsto\beta
$$
defines the required embedding.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The image of $\sigma$ is
$$
\QQ(\beta)
\subset
\RR.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
\sigma
$$
fixes $\QQ$ and sends the generator $\alpha$ to $\beta$,
$$
\sigma(K)
=
\QQ(\beta).
$$
The element
$$
\beta=\sqrt[4]{2}
$$
is real, so
$$
\QQ(\beta)\subset\RR.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The field $K$ is not contained in $\RR$.


::: pf-proof

The generator
$$
\alpha
=
i\sqrt[4]{2}
$$
is nonreal.
Since
$$
\alpha\in K,
$$
the field $K$ is not a subfield of $\RR$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Therefore $\sigma$ is not an automorphism of $K$.


::: pf-proof

An automorphism of $K$ must map $K$ onto itself.
But by step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref},
$$
\sigma(K)
\subset
\RR,
$$
while step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} shows
$$
K
\not\subset
\RR.
$$
Hence
$$
\sigma(K)\ne K.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{
\sigma:
i\sqrt[4]{2}
\longmapsto
\sqrt[4]{2}
}
$$
is a $\QQ$-embedding of $K$ that is not an automorphism.
This solves part (a).

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The map
$$
\tau:K\to K,
\qquad
\tau(\alpha)=-\alpha
$$
is a $\QQ$-automorphism.


::: pf-proof

The element
$$
-\alpha
$$
is also a root of
$$
x^4-2.
$$
Thus
$$
\alpha\mapsto-\alpha
$$
defines a $\QQ$-embedding
$$
K\hookrightarrow\CC.
$$
Its image is
$$
\QQ(-\alpha)
=
\QQ(\alpha)
=
K.
$$
Hence it is an automorphism.
Moreover,
$$
\tau^2=\operatorname{id}_K.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Every element of $K$ has a unique expression
$$
a+b\alpha+c\alpha^2+d\alpha^3,
\qquad
a,b,c,d\in\QQ.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref},
$$
[K:\QQ]=4.
$$
Thus
$$
1,\alpha,\alpha^2,\alpha^3
$$
is a $\QQ$-basis of $K$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The real subfield of $K$ is
$$
\boxed{
K\cap\RR
=
\QQ(\sqrt2).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Write
$$
r
\coloneqq
\sqrt[4]{2}.
$$
Then
$$
\alpha=ir,
\qquad
\alpha^2=-\sqrt2,
\qquad
\alpha^3=-i\,2^{3/4}.
$$
Thus an element of $K$ has the form
$$
a-c\sqrt2
+
i
\left(
br-d\,2^{3/4}
\right),
$$
with
$$
a,b,c,d\in\QQ.
$$
The imaginary part vanishes exactly when
$$
br-d\,2^{3/4}=0.
$$
Factoring
$$
r\ne0,
$$
this becomes
$$
b-d\sqrt2=0.
$$
Since
$$
1,\sqrt2
$$
are linearly independent over $\QQ$,
$$
b=d=0.
$$
Hence every real element of $K$ lies in
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2).
$$
The reverse inclusion holds because
$$
\alpha^2=-\sqrt2\in K.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

One has
$$
\sqrt3
\notin
K.
$$


::: pf-proof

If
$$
\sqrt3
\in
K,
$$
then since $\sqrt3$ is real, step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} would give
$$
\sqrt3
\in
\QQ(\sqrt2).
$$
Suppose
$$
\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2
$$
with
$$
a,b\in\QQ.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
3
=
a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2.
$$
Hence
$$
ab=0.
$$
If
$$
b=0,
$$
then $\sqrt3\in\QQ$, impossible.
If
$$
a=0,
$$
then
$$
b^2=3/2,
$$
which has no rational solution.
Thus
$$
\sqrt3\notin K.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Therefore
$$
[L:K]=2,
$$
and every element of $L$ has a unique expression
$$
u+v\sqrt3,
\qquad
u,v\in K.
$$


::: pf-proof

The element $\sqrt3$ satisfies
$$
x^2-3.
$$
By step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}, it is not in $K$, so its minimal polynomial over $K$ has degree $2$.
Hence
$$
L
=
K(\sqrt3)
$$
is quadratic over $K$, with basis
$$
1,\sqrt3.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

Define
$$
\widetilde\tau:L\to L
$$
by
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde\tau
\left(
u+v\sqrt3
\right)
=
\tau(u)
+
\tau(v)\sqrt3.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} gives a unique representation
$$
u+v\sqrt3,
$$
so the formula is well-defined.
It fixes $\sqrt3$ and extends the automorphism
$$
\tau
$$
on $K$.
Because $\tau$ preserves addition and multiplication, direct expansion gives
$$
\widetilde\tau(x+y)
=
\widetilde\tau(x)
+
\widetilde\tau(y)
$$
and
$$
\widetilde\tau(xy)
=
\widetilde\tau(x)
\widetilde\tau(y).
$$
Also
$$
\widetilde\tau^2
=
\operatorname{id}_L,
$$
so the map is bijective.
Hence it is an automorphism of $L$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

The automorphism $\widetilde\tau$ does not fix $K$ pointwise.


::: pf-proof

The element
$$
\alpha
=
i\sqrt[4]{2}
\in
K.
$$
By construction,
$$
\widetilde\tau(\alpha)
=
\tau(\alpha)
=
-\alpha
\ne
\alpha.
$$
Thus $\widetilde\tau$ does not fix $K$ pointwise.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

Therefore an answer to part (b) is
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde\tau
\left(
i\sqrt[4]{2}
\right)
=
-i\sqrt[4]{2},
\qquad
\widetilde\tau(\sqrt3)
=
\sqrt3.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref} proves this assignment extends to an automorphism of $L$, and step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} proves it does not fix $K$ pointwise.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} solves part (a), and step [](#s1-13){.pf-ref} solves part (b).
