::: {.problem}
Define
\[
\mathcal A=\{\alpha\in\mathbb C: \text{there exists } f\in\mathbb Q[x]\text{ such that }f(\alpha)=0\},
\]
the set of algebraic numbers.
For example, $\sqrt2\in\mathcal A$ since it is a root of $x^2-2$, and $\sqrt{-2}+\sqrt3\in\mathcal A$ since it is a root of $x^4-2x^2+25$.

(a) Show that $\mathbb Q\subset\mathcal A$.

(b) Let $\mathbb Q\subset L$ be an algebraic extension.
Prove that $L\subset\mathcal A$.

(c) Prove that $\mathcal A$ is a field.
Deduce that it is the largest algebraic extension of $\mathbb Q$ in $\mathbb C$.

(d) Explain, using a single sentence, why $\mathcal A\ne\mathbb C$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Every rational number is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
q\in\mathbb Q.
$$
Then $q$ is a root of the nonzero polynomial
$$
x-q
\in
\mathbb Q[x].
$$
Hence
$$
q\in\mathcal A.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{
\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathcal A.
}
$$
This proves part (a).
:::

<1>2. Let
$$
\mathbb Q\subseteq L
$$
be an algebraic extension.
Then every element of $L$ lies in $\mathcal A$.

::: {.proof}
If
$$
\alpha\in L,
$$
then by definition of an algebraic extension, $\alpha$ is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$.
Thus some nonzero
$$
f\in\mathbb Q[x]
$$
satisfies
$$
f(\alpha)=0.
$$
Hence
$$
\alpha\in\mathcal A.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{
L\subseteq\mathcal A.
}
$$
This proves part (b).
:::

<1>3. If
$$
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal A,
$$
then the field
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta)
$$
is finite over $\mathbb Q$.

::: {.proof}
Because
$$
\alpha
$$
is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$,
$$
[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]
<\infty.
$$
Because
$$
\beta
$$
is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$, it is also algebraic over
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha),
$$
since the same polynomial in $\mathbb Q[x]$ that annihilates $\beta$ also lies in
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha)[x].
$$
Thus
$$
[\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb Q(\alpha)]
<\infty.
$$
The tower law gives
$$
[\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb Q]
<\infty.
$$
:::

<1>4. Every element of
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta)
$$
is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$.

::: {.proof}
A finite field extension is algebraic.
Apply step <1>3.
:::

<1>5. The set $\mathcal A$ is closed under addition and multiplication.

::: {.proof}
If
$$
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal A,
$$
then
$$
\alpha+\beta
$$
and
$$
\alpha\beta
$$
belong to
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta).
$$
By step <1>4, both are algebraic over $\mathbb Q$.
Thus
$$
\alpha+\beta,\alpha\beta\in\mathcal A.
$$
:::

<1>6. The set $\mathcal A$ is closed under additive inverses.

::: {.proof}
If
$$
\alpha\in\mathcal A,
$$
then
$$
-\alpha
\in
\mathbb Q(\alpha).
$$
The simple extension
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha)/\mathbb Q
$$
is finite, so every element of it is algebraic.
Hence
$$
-\alpha\in\mathcal A.
$$
:::

<1>7. The set $\mathcal A$ is closed under multiplicative inverses of nonzero elements.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
0\ne\alpha\in\mathcal A.
$$
Then
$$
\alpha^{-1}
\in
\mathbb Q(\alpha).
$$
Again,
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha)/\mathbb Q
$$
is finite and therefore algebraic.
Thus
$$
\alpha^{-1}
\in
\mathcal A.
$$
:::

<1>8. The elements
$$
0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
1
$$
belong to $\mathcal A$.

::: {.proof}
They are rational numbers.
Apply step <1>1.
:::

<1>9. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
\mathcal A
\text{ is a field}.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>5--<1>8 verify the field axioms inherited from $\mathbb C$: closure under addition, multiplication, additive inverses, and nonzero multiplicative inverses, together with the presence of $0$ and $1$.
:::

<1>10. The extension
$$
\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathcal A
$$
is algebraic.

::: {.proof}
By definition, every element of
$$
\mathcal A
$$
is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$.
:::

<1>11. The field $\mathcal A$ contains every algebraic extension of $\mathbb Q$ that lies inside $\mathbb C$.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
\mathbb Q\subseteq L\subseteq\mathbb C
$$
be algebraic.
Step <1>2 gives
$$
L\subseteq\mathcal A.
$$
:::

<1>12. Hence
$$
\boxed{
\mathcal A
\text{ is the largest algebraic extension of }\mathbb Q
\text{ contained in }\mathbb C.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>10 shows that $\mathcal A$ itself is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$, while step <1>11 shows that every other algebraic extension inside $\mathbb C$ is contained in it.
This proves part (c).
:::

<1>13. One has
$$
\boxed{
\mathcal A\ne\mathbb C.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
The number
$$
\pi
$$
is transcendental over $\mathbb Q$, so
$$
\pi\in\mathbb C
\setminus
\mathcal A.
$$
This is the requested one-sentence explanation for part (d).
:::

<1>14. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1, <1>2, <1>9--<1>12, and <1>13 establish parts (a), (b), (c), and (d), respectively.
:::
