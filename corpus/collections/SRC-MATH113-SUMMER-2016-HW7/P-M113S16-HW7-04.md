::: {.problem}
Let $K$ be the field obtained by adjoining all three cube roots of $2$ to $\QQ$.
Show that $K$ contains all cube roots of unity and compute $[K:\QQ]$.

*Hint.* For the degree computation, you may want to let $L=\QQ(\omega)$, where
$$
\omega=\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2},
$$
and factor the extension as $\QQ\subset L\subset K$.
Note that $\omega^3=1$.
:::

::: {.solution}

Let
$$
\alpha
\coloneqq
\sqrt[3]{2},
$$
and let
$$
\omega
\coloneqq
\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2}.
$$

::: pf

::: pf-step

The three roots of
$$
x^3-2
$$
are
$$
\alpha,
\qquad
\omega\alpha,
\qquad
\omega^2\alpha.
$$


::: pf-proof

The element $\omega$ satisfies
$$
\omega^3=1
$$
and
$$
\omega\ne1.
$$
Hence
$$
(\omega^j\alpha)^3
=
\omega^{3j}\alpha^3
=
2
$$
for
$$
j=0,1,2.
$$
These three numbers are distinct because
$$
\alpha\ne0
$$
and
$$
1,\omega,\omega^2
$$
are distinct.
A cubic has exactly three roots in $\CC$, counted with multiplicity, so these are all of them.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The field $K$ contains $\omega$.


::: pf-proof

By definition, $K$ contains all three cube roots of $2$, hence it contains
$$
\alpha
$$
and
$$
\omega\alpha.
$$
Since
$$
\alpha\ne0,
$$
the field $K$ contains
$$
\frac{\omega\alpha}{\alpha}
=
\omega.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The field $K$ contains all cube roots of unity.


::: pf-proof

The cube roots of unity are
$$
1,
\qquad
\omega,
\qquad
\omega^2.
$$
The element $1$ lies in every field.
Step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\omega\in K,
$$
and therefore
$$
\omega^2\in K.
$$
Thus all three cube roots of unity lie in $K$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

One has
$$
\boxed{
K
=
\QQ(\alpha,\omega).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

By definition, $K$ contains the three roots
$$
\alpha,
\quad
\omega\alpha,
\quad
\omega^2\alpha.
$$
Step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} shows that it contains both $\alpha$ and $\omega$, hence
$$
\QQ(\alpha,\omega)
\subseteq
K.
$$

Conversely,
$$
\QQ(\alpha,\omega)
$$
contains
$$
\alpha,
\quad
\omega\alpha,
\quad
\omega^2\alpha,
$$
so it contains all generators used to define $K$.
Thus
$$
K
\subseteq
\QQ(\alpha,\omega).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The field
$$
L
\coloneqq
\QQ(\omega)
$$
has degree $2$ over $\QQ$.


::: pf-proof

The element $\omega$ satisfies
$$
\omega^2+\omega+1=0.
$$
The polynomial
$$
x^2+x+1
$$
has discriminant
$$
1-4=-3,
$$
which is not a square in $\QQ$.
Thus it is irreducible over $\QQ$, so
$$
[L:\QQ]=2.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The only real elements of $L$ are the rational numbers:
$$
L\cap\RR
=
\QQ.
$$


::: pf-proof

Since
$$
\omega
=
\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2},
$$
one has
$$
L
=
\QQ(i\sqrt3)
=
\{
a+b\,i\sqrt3
:
a,b\in\QQ
\}.
$$
If such an element is real, then its imaginary part
$$
b\sqrt3
$$
must vanish.
Hence
$$
b=0,
$$
so the element lies in $\QQ$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

The polynomial
$$
x^3-2
$$
is irreducible over $L$.


::: pf-proof

Suppose
$$
x^3-2
$$
were reducible over the field $L$.
Since it has degree $3$, it would have a root in $L$.
Every root is one of
$$
\alpha,
\quad
\omega\alpha,
\quad
\omega^2\alpha.
$$

If
$$
\alpha\in L,
$$
then because $\alpha$ is real, step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} would give
$$
\alpha\in\QQ,
$$
contradicting irreducibility of
$$
x^3-2
$$
over $\QQ$ by Eisenstein at $2$.

If
$$
\omega\alpha\in L,
$$
then multiplying by
$$
\omega^{-1}
\in
L
$$
would give
$$
\alpha\in L,
$$
again impossible.
The same argument applies to
$$
\omega^2\alpha.
$$
Therefore the cubic has no root in $L$ and is irreducible.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Hence
$$
[K:L]=3.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref},
$$
K
=
L(\alpha).
$$
Step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} shows that the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $L$ is
$$
x^3-2,
$$
of degree $3$.
Thus
$$
[K:L]=3.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
[K:\QQ]
=
6.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

By the tower law and steps [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-8){.pf-ref},
$$
[K:\QQ]
=
[K:L][L:\QQ]
=
3\cdot2
=
6.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} proves that $K$ contains all cube roots of unity, and step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} computes its degree.
