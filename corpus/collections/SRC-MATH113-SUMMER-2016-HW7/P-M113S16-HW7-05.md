::: {.problem}
Suppose $f\in\QQ[x]$, not necessarily irreducible.

(a) Show that there is a smallest subfield of $\CC$ over which $f$ factors into linear factors.
In other words, prove that there exists a subfield $K_f\subset\CC$ such that:

(i) $f$ factors into linear factors in $K_f[x]$; and

(ii) if $L\subset\CC$ is any other subfield over which $f$ factors into linear factors, then $L\supseteq K_f$.

(b) Taking
$$
f=x^7-x^4-4x^3+4,
$$
find $K_f$ by writing it as $\QQ(\alpha,\beta,\ldots)$, and compute $[K_f:\QQ]$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Let
$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r
\in
\CC
$$
be the distinct roots of $f$, and define
$$
\boxed{
K_f
\coloneqq
\QQ(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Every polynomial over $\QQ$ has only finitely many roots in $\CC$, so adjoining all distinct roots produces a well-defined subfield of $\CC$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The polynomial $f$ factors into linear factors over $K_f$.


::: pf-proof

Write
$$
f(x)
=
c
\prod_{j=1}^{r}
(x-\alpha_j)^{m_j}
$$
in $\CC[x]$.
The leading coefficient
$$
c
$$
lies in $\QQ\subseteq K_f$, and every root
$$
\alpha_j
$$
lies in $K_f$ by definition.
Therefore the same factorization lies in
$$
K_f[x].
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

If
$$
L\subseteq\CC
$$
is a subfield over which $f$ factors into linear factors, then
$$
\alpha_j\in L
$$
for every $j$.


::: pf-proof

If
$$
f
$$
factors into linear factors in
$$
L[x],
$$
then all of its roots in $\CC$ occur among the constants appearing in those linear factors.
Hence every distinct root
$$
\alpha_j
$$
lies in $L$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
K_f
\subseteq
L
}
$$
for every subfield $L\subseteq\CC$ over which $f$ splits.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}, $L$ contains every generator
$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r.
$$
It also contains
$$
\QQ.
$$
Therefore it contains the field generated by these elements:
$$
\QQ(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r)
=
K_f.
$$
This proves the minimality required in part (a).

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

For
$$
f(x)
=
x^7-x^4-4x^3+4,
$$
one has
$$
\boxed{
f(x)
=
(x^4-4)(x^3-1).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Factor by grouping:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x^7-x^4-4x^3+4
&=
x^4(x^3-1)
-
4(x^3-1)\\
&=
(x^4-4)(x^3-1).
\end{aligned}
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Over $\CC$,
$$
x^4-4
=
(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2)(x-i\sqrt2)(x+i\sqrt2).
$$


::: pf-proof

One has
$$
x^4-4
=
(x^2-2)(x^2+2),
$$
and
$$
x^2-2
=
(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2),
$$
while
$$
x^2+2
=
(x-i\sqrt2)(x+i\sqrt2).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

If
$$
\omega
\coloneqq
\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2},
$$
then
$$
x^3-1
=
(x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2).
$$


::: pf-proof

The numbers
$$
1,\omega,\omega^2
$$
are the three cube roots of unity.
Hence they are exactly the roots of
$$
x^3-1.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

Thus
$$
K_f
=
\QQ
\left(
\sqrt2,
i\sqrt2,
\omega
\right).
$$


::: pf-proof

By steps [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}, these elements generate all roots of $f$:
$$
\pm\sqrt2,
\quad
\pm i\sqrt2,
\quad
1,
\quad
\omega,
\quad
\omega^2.
$$
Conversely each displayed generator is itself a root or is generated by roots.
Thus the root-generated field from part (a) is exactly the displayed field.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

One has
$$
i\in K_f.
$$


::: pf-proof

Both
$$
\sqrt2
$$
and
$$
i\sqrt2
$$
belong to $K_f$ by step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}.
Since
$$
\sqrt2\ne0,
$$
$$
i
=
\frac{i\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}
\in
K_f.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

One has
$$
\sqrt3\in K_f.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref},
$$
2\omega+1
=
i\sqrt3.
$$
Thus
$$
i\sqrt3
\in
K_f.
$$
By step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref},
$$
i\in K_f,
$$
so
$$
\sqrt3
=
-i(i\sqrt3)
\in
K_f.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
K_f
=
\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} give
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3)
\subseteq
K_f.
$$

Conversely,
$$
i\sqrt2
\in
\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3),
$$
and
$$
\omega
=
\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2}
\in
\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3).
$$
Thus the generators in step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} lie in
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3),
$$
giving the reverse inclusion.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

One has
$$
[\QQ(\sqrt2):\QQ]
=
2.
$$


::: pf-proof

The polynomial
$$
x^2-2
$$
is irreducible over $\QQ$ by the rational-root test.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

One has
$$
[\QQ(\sqrt2,i):\QQ(\sqrt2)]
=
2.
$$


::: pf-proof

The field
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2)
$$
is contained in $\RR$.
Hence
$$
i
\notin
\QQ(\sqrt2).
$$
Since $i$ satisfies
$$
x^2+1,
$$
its minimal polynomial over
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2)
$$
has degree $2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

One has
$$
\sqrt3
\notin
\QQ(\sqrt2,i).
$$


::: pf-proof

Every element of
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2,i)
$$
has the form
$$
a+b\sqrt2
+
i(c+d\sqrt2),
$$
with
$$
a,b,c,d\in\QQ.
$$
If such an element is real, then
$$
c+d\sqrt2=0.
$$
Because
$$
1,\sqrt2
$$
are linearly independent over $\QQ$,
$$
c=d=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\QQ(\sqrt2,i)\cap\RR
=
\QQ(\sqrt2).
$$

If
$$
\sqrt3
\in
\QQ(\sqrt2,i),
$$
then
$$
\sqrt3
\in
\QQ(\sqrt2).
$$
Suppose
$$
\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2
$$
with
$$
a,b\in\QQ.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
3
=
a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2.
$$
Hence
$$
ab=0.
$$
If
$$
b=0,
$$
then
$$
\sqrt3\in\QQ,
$$
impossible.
If
$$
a=0,
$$
then
$$
b^2=3/2,
$$
which has no rational solution.
Thus
$$
\sqrt3
\notin
\QQ(\sqrt2,i).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-15}

Hence
$$
[\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3):
\QQ(\sqrt2,i)]
=
2.
$$


::: pf-proof

The element
$$
\sqrt3
$$
satisfies
$$
x^2-3.
$$
By step [](#s1-14){.pf-ref} it does not belong to the base field, so its minimal polynomial there has degree $2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-16}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
[K_f:\QQ]
=
8.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref} and the tower law,
$$
\begin{aligned}
[K_f:\QQ]
&=
[\QQ(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3):
\QQ(\sqrt2,i)]\\
&\quad\cdot
[\QQ(\sqrt2,i):
\QQ(\sqrt2)]\\
&\quad\cdot
[\QQ(\sqrt2):\QQ]\\
&=
2\cdot2\cdot2\\
&=
8,
\end{aligned}
$$
using steps [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} and [](#s1-13){.pf-ref}, and [](#s1-15){.pf-ref}.

:::
