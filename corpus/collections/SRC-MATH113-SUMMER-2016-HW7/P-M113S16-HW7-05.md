::: {.problem}
Suppose $f\in\mathbb Q[x]$, not necessarily irreducible.

(a) Show that there is a smallest subfield of $\mathbb C$ over which $f$ factors into linear factors.
In other words, prove that there exists a subfield $K_f\subset\mathbb C$ such that:

1. $f$ factors into linear factors in $K_f[x]$; and

2. if $L\subset\mathbb C$ is any other subfield over which $f$ factors into linear factors, then $L\supseteq K_f$.

(b) Taking
\[
f=x^7-x^4-4x^3+4,
\]
find $K_f$ by writing it as $\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta,\ldots)$, and compute $[K_f:\mathbb Q]$.
:::

::: pf

::: pf-step

Let
$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r
\in
\mathbb C
$$
be the distinct roots of $f$, and define
$$
\boxed{
K_f
\coloneqq
\mathbb Q(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Every polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ has only finitely many roots in $\mathbb C$, so adjoining all distinct roots produces a well-defined subfield of $\mathbb C$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The polynomial $f$ factors into linear factors over $K_f$.


::: pf-proof

Write
$$
f(x)
=
c
\prod_{j=1}^{r}
(x-\alpha_j)^{m_j}
$$
in $\mathbb C[x]$.
The leading coefficient
$$
c
$$
lies in $\mathbb Q\subseteq K_f$, and every root
$$
\alpha_j
$$
lies in $K_f$ by definition.
Therefore the same factorization lies in
$$
K_f[x].
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If
$$
L\subseteq\mathbb C
$$
is a subfield over which $f$ factors into linear factors, then
$$
\alpha_j\in L
$$
for every $j$.


::: pf-proof

If
$$
f
$$
factors into linear factors in
$$
L[x],
$$
then all of its roots in $\mathbb C$ occur among the constants appearing in those linear factors.
Hence every distinct root
$$
\alpha_j
$$
lies in $L$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
K_f
\subseteq
L
}
$$
for every subfield $L\subseteq\mathbb C$ over which $f$ splits.


::: pf-proof

By step <1>3, $L$ contains every generator
$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r.
$$
It also contains
$$
\mathbb Q.
$$
Therefore it contains the field generated by these elements:
$$
\mathbb Q(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r)
=
K_f.
$$
This proves the minimality required in part (a).

:::

:::

::: pf-step

For
$$
f(x)
=
x^7-x^4-4x^3+4,
$$
one has
$$
\boxed{
f(x)
=
(x^4-4)(x^3-1).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Factor by grouping:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x^7-x^4-4x^3+4
&=
x^4(x^3-1)
-
4(x^3-1)\\
&=
(x^4-4)(x^3-1).
\end{aligned}
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Over $\mathbb C$,
$$
x^4-4
=
(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2)(x-i\sqrt2)(x+i\sqrt2).
$$


::: pf-proof

One has
$$
x^4-4
=
(x^2-2)(x^2+2),
$$
and
$$
x^2-2
=
(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2),
$$
while
$$
x^2+2
=
(x-i\sqrt2)(x+i\sqrt2).
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If
$$
\omega
\coloneqq
\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2},
$$
then
$$
x^3-1
=
(x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2).
$$


::: pf-proof

The numbers
$$
1,\omega,\omega^2
$$
are the three cube roots of unity.
Hence they are exactly the roots of
$$
x^3-1.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Thus
$$
K_f
=
\mathbb Q
\left(
\sqrt2,
i\sqrt2,
\omega
\right).
$$


::: pf-proof

By steps <1>5--<1>7, these elements generate all roots of $f$:
$$
\pm\sqrt2,
\quad
\pm i\sqrt2,
\quad
1,
\quad
\omega,
\quad
\omega^2.
$$
Conversely each displayed generator is itself a root or is generated by roots.
Thus the root-generated field from part (a) is exactly the displayed field.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
i\in K_f.
$$


::: pf-proof

Both
$$
\sqrt2
$$
and
$$
i\sqrt2
$$
belong to $K_f$ by step <1>8.
Since
$$
\sqrt2\ne0,
$$
$$
i
=
\frac{i\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}
\in
K_f.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
\sqrt3\in K_f.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step <1>7,
$$
2\omega+1
=
i\sqrt3.
$$
Thus
$$
i\sqrt3
\in
K_f.
$$
By step <1>9,
$$
i\in K_f,
$$
so
$$
\sqrt3
=
-i(i\sqrt3)
\in
K_f.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
K_f
=
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3).
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Steps <1>9--<1>10 give
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3)
\subseteq
K_f.
$$

Conversely,
$$
i\sqrt2
\in
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3),
$$
and
$$
\omega
=
\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2}
\in
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3).
$$
Thus the generators in step <1>8 lie in
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3),
$$
giving the reverse inclusion.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2):\mathbb Q]
=
2.
$$


::: pf-proof

The polynomial
$$
x^2-2
$$
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ by the rational-root test.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i):\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)]
=
2.
$$


::: pf-proof

The field
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)
$$
is contained in $\mathbb R$.
Hence
$$
i
\notin
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$
Since $i$ satisfies
$$
x^2+1,
$$
its minimal polynomial over
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)
$$
has degree $2$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

One has
$$
\sqrt3
\notin
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i).
$$


::: pf-proof

Every element of
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)
$$
has the form
$$
a+b\sqrt2
+
i(c+d\sqrt2),
$$
with
$$
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Q.
$$
If such an element is real, then
$$
c+d\sqrt2=0.
$$
Because
$$
1,\sqrt2
$$
are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$,
$$
c=d=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)\cap\mathbb R
=
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$

If
$$
\sqrt3
\in
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i),
$$
then
$$
\sqrt3
\in
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2).
$$
Suppose
$$
\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2
$$
with
$$
a,b\in\mathbb Q.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
3
=
a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2.
$$
Hence
$$
ab=0.
$$
If
$$
b=0,
$$
then
$$
\sqrt3\in\mathbb Q,
$$
impossible.
If
$$
a=0,
$$
then
$$
b^2=3/2,
$$
which has no rational solution.
Thus
$$
\sqrt3
\notin
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i).
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Hence
$$
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3):
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)]
=
2.
$$


::: pf-proof

The element
$$
\sqrt3
$$
satisfies
$$
x^2-3.
$$
By step <1>14 it does not belong to the base field, so its minimal polynomial there has degree $2$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
[K_f:\mathbb Q]
=
8.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

By step <1>11 and the tower law,
$$
\begin{aligned}
[K_f:\mathbb Q]
&=
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i,\sqrt3):
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)]\\
&\quad\cdot
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i):
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)]\\
&\quad\cdot
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2):\mathbb Q]\\
&=
2\cdot2\cdot2\\
&=
8,
\end{aligned}
$$
using steps <1>12, <1>13, and <1>15.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps <1>1--<1>4 prove part (a), and steps <1>5--<1>16 solve part (b).
