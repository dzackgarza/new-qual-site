::: {.problem}
(a) Prove that a connected graph on $n$ vertices has $n$ edges if and only if it contains exactly one cycle.

(b) Let $n\ge 3$ and suppose that $G$ is an $n$-vertex graph such that $G\setminus\{v\}$ is a tree for every $v\in V(G)$. Determine the number of edges in $G$, and thereby determine $G$.
:::

::: {.solution}

## Part (a)

Let $G$ be a connected graph with $n$ vertices.

::: pf

::: pf-step

Choose a spanning tree
$$
T\subseteq G.
$$


::: pf-proof

Every finite connected graph has a spanning tree.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The tree $T$ has exactly
$$
\boxed{n-1}
$$
edges.


::: pf-proof

A tree on $n$ vertices has $n-1$ edges.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Suppose first that
$$
\cardinality{E(G)}=n.
$$
Then exactly one edge of $G$ is not in $T$.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref},
$$
\cardinality{E(T)}=n-1.
$$
Thus
$$
\cardinality{E(G)-E(T)}=1.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Let $e$ be that unique edge. Then $T\cup\{e\}$ contains exactly
one cycle.


::: pf-proof

The endpoints of $e$ are joined by a unique path in the tree $T$. Adding
$e$ to that path creates a cycle.

Any cycle in $T\cup\{e\}$ must contain $e$, because $T$ itself is
acyclic. Removing $e$ from such a cycle leaves a path in $T$ joining the
endpoints of $e$, and that path is unique. Hence the cycle is unique.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Since
$$
G=T\cup\{e\},
$$
the graph $G$ contains exactly one cycle.


::: pf-proof

This follows from steps [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-4){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

Conversely, suppose $G$ contains exactly one cycle $C$.


::: pf-proof

This is the reverse hypothesis.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Choose an edge
$$
e\in E(C).
$$
Then
$$
G-e
$$
is connected.


::: pf-proof

The endpoints of $e$ are still joined by the remaining edges of the cycle
$C-e$. Thus deleting $e$ does not disconnect the graph.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

The graph $G-e$ is acyclic.


::: pf-proof

If $G-e$ contained a cycle, that cycle would also be a cycle of $G$
different from $C$, because it does not use $e$. This contradicts
uniqueness of $C$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Therefore
$$
G-e
$$
is a tree.


::: pf-proof

It is connected by step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} and acyclic by step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Hence
$$
\cardinality{E(G-e)}=n-1.
$$


::: pf-proof

A tree on $n$ vertices has $n-1$ edges.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{\cardinality{E(G)}=n}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The graph $G$ is obtained from $G-e$ by restoring one edge:
$$
\cardinality{E(G)}=(n-1)+1=n.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

Thus
$$
\boxed{
\cardinality{E(G)}=n
\iff
G\text{ contains exactly one cycle}.}
$$


::: pf-proof

The forward implication is steps [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}, and the reverse implication is
steps [](#s1-6){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-11){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

For every vertex $v$,
$$
\boxed{
m-\deg(v)=n-2}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Deleting $v$ removes exactly the $\deg(v)$ edges incident to $v$.
The remaining graph has $n-1$ vertices and is a tree, so it has
$$
(n-1)-1=n-2
$$
edges.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

Hence all vertices have the same degree
$$
\boxed{
d=m-n+2}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Rearrange step [](#s1-13){.pf-ref}:
$$
\deg(v)=m-n+2.
$$
The right-hand side is independent of $v$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-15}

By the handshaking lemma,
$$
\boxed{
nd=2m}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The sum of all vertex degrees equals twice the number of edges. Since the
graph is $d$-regular, the degree sum is $nd$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Substituting step [](#s1-14){.pf-ref} into step [](#s1-15){.pf-ref},
$$
n(m-n+2)=2m.
$$


::: pf-proof

Replace $d$ by $m-n+2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-17}

Rearranging,
$$
(n-2)m=n(n-2).
$$


::: pf-proof

Expand:
$$
nm-n^2+2n=2m.
$$
Move the $m$-terms together:
$$
(n-2)m=n^2-2n=n(n-2).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-18}

Since $n\ge3$,
$$
n-2\ne0,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{m=n}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Divide step [](#s1-17){.pf-ref} by $n-2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-19}

Therefore every vertex has degree
$$
\boxed{d=2}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Substitute
$$
m=n
$$
into
$$
d=m-n+2.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-20}

The graph $G$ is connected.


::: pf-proof

Take any two vertices $x,y\in V(G)$. Since $n\ge3$, choose a vertex
$$
v\ne x,y.
$$
By hypothesis,
$$
G-\{v\}
$$
is a tree, hence connected. Thus $x$ and $y$ are joined by a path in
$G-\{v\}$, hence in $G$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-21}

A finite connected $2$-regular graph is a cycle.


::: pf-proof

Start at a vertex and follow an edge. At each new vertex, degree $2$
forces exactly one unused continuation edge. Since the graph is finite, the
walk eventually returns to a previously visited vertex; degree $2$ forces
the first repetition to close a cycle. No vertex can lie outside that cycle,
because connectedness would require an edge from the cycle to an outside
vertex, giving some cycle vertex degree at least $3$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-22}

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
G\cong C_n}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The graph has $n$ vertices, is connected by step [](#s1-20){.pf-ref}, and is
$2$-regular by step [](#s1-19){.pf-ref}. Apply step [](#s1-21){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} proves part (a), and steps [](#s1-18){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-22){.pf-ref} show that in part (b)
the graph has $n$ edges and is the cycle $C_n$.

:::
