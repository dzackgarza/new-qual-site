::: problem
(a) Prove that $\langle a, b \mid a^2, b^2 \rangle$ is isomorphic to the group of Euclidean symmetries of $\mathbb{Z}$.
(You can use without proof that the group of Euclidean symmetries of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $\{f: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z} \mid f(x) = ax + b,\; a = \pm 1,\; b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ and it is generated by the reflection $f_0(x) := -x$ and the translation $g_0(x) := x + 1$.)

(b) Prove that any group generated by two elements of order 2 is solvable.
:::


::: {.solution}
<1>1. Let \(D\) be the group of Euclidean symmetries of \(\mathbb Z\). Define
\[
r(x)=-x,\qquad s(x)=1-x.
\]
Then \(r^2=s^2=1\), so there is a homomorphism
\[
\Phi:\langle a,b\mid a^2,b^2\rangle\longrightarrow D,
\qquad a\mapsto r,\ b\mapsto s.
\]
::: {.proof}
Both \(r\) and \(s\) are involutions, so the defining relations are satisfied and the universal property of the presented group gives \(\Phi\).
:::

<1>2. The map \(\Phi\) is surjective.
::: {.proof}
The translation \(g_0(x)=x+1\) satisfies \(g_0=sr\). Since the source statement allows us to use that \(D\) is generated by \(r=f_0\) and \(g_0\), the elements \(r\) and \(s\) also generate \(D\).
:::

<1>3. Every nonempty reduced word in \(a,b\) has nontrivial image under \(\Phi\).
::: {.proof}
Using only \(a^2=b^2=1\), every reduced word alternates in \(a,b\). Thus every nonempty reduced word is one of
\[
(ab)^k,\quad (ba)^k,\quad a(ba)^k,\quad b(ab)^k
\]
for some \(k\ge0\), with the first two cases requiring \(k\ge1\).
Now
\[
\Phi(ab)=rs:x\mapsto x-1,
\qquad
\Phi(ba)=sr:x\mapsto x+1.
\]
Hence \(\Phi((ab)^k)\) and \(\Phi((ba)^k)\) are nontrivial translations when \(k\ge1\). The other two forms are compositions of a reflection with a translation, hence reflections, and therefore are also nonidentity. So no nonempty reduced word lies in \(\ker\Phi\).
:::

<1>4. Therefore
\[
\langle a,b\mid a^2,b^2\rangle\cong D.
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>2 the map \(\Phi\) is surjective, and by <1>3 its kernel is trivial.
:::

<1>5. Let \(G=\langle x,y\rangle\) with \(x^2=y^2=1\), and set \(c=xy\). Then \(C=\langle c\rangle\) is a normal cyclic subgroup of \(G\).
::: {.proof}
Certainly \(C\) is cyclic. Since
\[
xcx^{-1}=x(xy)x=yx=(xy)^{-1}=c^{-1}
\]
and similarly \(ycy^{-1}=c^{-1}\), conjugation by the generators \(x,y\) preserves \(C\). Hence \(C\trianglelefteq G\).
:::

<1>6. The quotient \(G/C\) has order at most \(2\), so it is abelian.
::: {.proof}
In \(G/C\) one has \(xy=C\), hence \(yC=x^{-1}C=xC\). Thus the quotient is generated by the single involution \(xC\), so it is trivial or cyclic of order \(2\).
:::

<1>7. The group \(G\) is solvable.
::: {.proof}
The normal subgroup \(C\) is abelian and the quotient \(G/C\) is abelian by <1>6. Therefore \(G'\subseteq C\), so \(G''=1\). Thus \(G\) is solvable of derived length at most \(2\).
:::
:::
