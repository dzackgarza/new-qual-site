::: problem
Suppose $A$ is a unital commutative ring.

(a) Let $M$ and $N$ be two submodules of an $A$-module $K$.
Suppose $M + N$ and $M \cap N$ are finitely generated $A$-modules.
Prove that $M$ is a finitely generated $A$-module.

(b) Let $\Sigma := \{\mathfrak{a} \leq A \mid \mathfrak{a} \text{ is not a finitely generated ideal}\}$.
Suppose $\Sigma$ is not empty.
Prove that $\Sigma$ has a maximal element.

(c) Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be a maximal element of $\Sigma$.
Prove that $\mathfrak{p}$ is a prime ideal.

Hint: Suppose to the contrary that $ab \in \mathfrak{p}$ and $a, b \notin \mathfrak{p}$ for some $a, b \in A$; consider $\mathfrak{p} + \langle a \rangle$ and $\mathfrak{p} \cap \langle a \rangle$.
:::


::: {.solution}
<1>1. For part (a), there is an exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow M\cap N\xrightarrow{\iota}M\oplus N\xrightarrow{\pi}M+N\longrightarrow0,
\]
where \(\iota(x)=(x,-x)\) and \(\pi(m,n)=m+n\).
::: {.proof}
The map \(\iota\) is injective. The map \(\pi\) is surjective by definition of \(M+N\). Its kernel consists of pairs \((m,n)\) with \(m=-n\in M\cap N\), exactly the image of \(\iota\).
:::

<1>2. The module \(M\oplus N\) is finitely generated.
::: {.proof}
Let \(x_1,\ldots,x_r\) generate \(M\cap N\), and let \(y_1,\ldots,y_s\) generate \(M+N\). Choose lifts \(z_j\in M\oplus N\) with \(\pi(z_j)=y_j\). Then the finite set
\[
\{\iota(x_1),\ldots,\iota(x_r),z_1,\ldots,z_s\}
\]
generates \(M\oplus N\): subtracting a suitable combination of the \(z_j\)'s from any element leaves an element of \(\ker\pi=\operatorname{im}\iota\).
:::

<1>3. Hence \(M\) is finitely generated.
::: {.proof}
The projection \(M\oplus N\twoheadrightarrow M\) is surjective. A quotient of a finitely generated module is finitely generated, so <1>2 implies that \(M\) is finitely generated.
:::

<1>4. For part (b), let \(\mathcal C\) be a chain in \(\Sigma\). Its union
\[
I=\bigcup_{\mathfrak a\in\mathcal C}\mathfrak a
\]
is an ideal of \(A\).
::: {.proof}
Because \(\mathcal C\) is totally ordered by inclusion, any two elements of the union lie together in one member of the chain. Closure under addition and multiplication by arbitrary elements of \(A\) follows there.
:::

<1>5. The ideal \(I\) is not finitely generated, so \(I\in\Sigma\).
::: {.proof}
Suppose \(I=(x_1,\ldots,x_t)\). Each \(x_i\) lies in some \(\mathfrak a_i\in\mathcal C\). Since the chain is totally ordered and the set is finite, one member \(\mathfrak a\in\mathcal C\) contains all the \(\mathfrak a_i\), hence all the generators \(x_i\). Then
\[
I=(x_1,\ldots,x_t)\subseteq\mathfrak a\subseteq I,
\]
so \(I=\mathfrak a\), contradicting \(\mathfrak a\in\Sigma\).
:::

<1>6. Therefore \(\Sigma\) has a maximal element.
::: {.proof}
By <1>4 and <1>5, every chain in \(\Sigma\) has an upper bound in \(\Sigma\). Zorn's lemma gives a maximal element.
:::

<1>7. For part (c), let \(\mathfrak p\) be maximal in \(\Sigma\). Suppose for contradiction that \(ab\in\mathfrak p\) while \(a,b\notin\mathfrak p\).
Then both
\[
\mathfrak p+(a)\qquad\text{and}\qquad (\mathfrak p:a)=\{x\in A:ax\in\mathfrak p\}
\]
strictly contain \(\mathfrak p\), so both are finitely generated.
::: {.proof}
Because \(a\notin\mathfrak p\), the ideal \(\mathfrak p+(a)\) strictly contains \(\mathfrak p\). Also \(\mathfrak p\subseteq(\mathfrak p:a)\), while \(b\in(\mathfrak p:a)\setminus\mathfrak p\) because \(ab\in\mathfrak p\) and \(b\notin\mathfrak p\). Thus both ideals strictly contain the maximal element \(\mathfrak p\) of \(\Sigma\), and therefore neither belongs to \(\Sigma\); each is finitely generated.
:::

<1>8. The intersection \(\mathfrak p\cap(a)\) is finitely generated.
::: {.proof}
Multiplication by \(a\) maps \((\mathfrak p:a)\) onto \(\mathfrak p\cap(a)\): every \(x\in(\mathfrak p:a)\) gives \(ax\in\mathfrak p\cap(a)\), and every element of \(\mathfrak p\cap(a)\) has the form \(ax\) with \(ax\in\mathfrak p\), hence \(x\in(\mathfrak p:a)\). Since \((\mathfrak p:a)\) is finitely generated by <1>7, so is its image \(\mathfrak p\cap(a)\).
:::

<1>9. Applying part (a) to the submodules \(\mathfrak p\) and \((a)\) of the \(A\)-module \(A\), we conclude that \(\mathfrak p\) is finitely generated, a contradiction.
::: {.proof}
Their sum \(\mathfrak p+(a)\) is finitely generated by <1>7, and their intersection is finitely generated by <1>8. Part (a) therefore implies that \(\mathfrak p\) is finitely generated, contradicting \(\mathfrak p\in\Sigma\).
:::

<1>10. Hence \(\mathfrak p\) is prime.
::: {.proof}
The contradiction in <1>9 shows that \(ab\in\mathfrak p\) implies \(a\in\mathfrak p\) or \(b\in\mathfrak p\). Also \(\mathfrak p\ne A\), because \(A=(1)\) is finitely generated and hence does not lie in \(\Sigma\). Thus \(\mathfrak p\) is a prime ideal.
:::
