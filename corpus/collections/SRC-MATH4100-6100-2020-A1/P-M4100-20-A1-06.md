::: {.problem}
Suppose one reverses the usual order of quantifiers and says that a sequence $a_n$ “verconges” to $a$ when there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $N\in\mathbb N$, $n\ge N$ implies
\[
|a_n-a|<\varepsilon.
\]
Give an example of a vercongent sequence, give a vercongent sequence that diverges in the usual sense, and characterize exactly what this altered definition describes.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $(a_n)$ verconges to $a$, then there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such
that
$$
|a_n-a|<\varepsilon
$$
for every $n\in\NN$.

::: {.proof}
By definition, there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every
$N\in\NN$, every $n\geq N$ satisfies
$$
|a_n-a|<\varepsilon.
$$
Taking $N=1$ gives the displayed inequality for every $n\geq1$.
:::

<1>2. Every sequence that verconges to some real number is bounded.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1,
$$
|a_n|
\leq
|a_n-a|+|a|
<
\varepsilon+|a|
$$
for every $n$.  Thus $(a_n)$ is bounded.
:::

<1>3. Conversely, every bounded sequence verconges to every real number $a$.

::: {.proof}
Suppose
$$
|a_n|\leq M
$$
for every $n$, and fix $a\in\RR$.  Choose
$$
\varepsilon=M+|a|+1.
$$
Then for every $n$,
$$
|a_n-a|
\leq
|a_n|+|a|
\leq
M+|a|
<
\varepsilon.
$$
Therefore, for every $N$ and every $n\geq N$, the same inequality holds, so
$(a_n)$ verconges to $a$.
:::

<1>4. The altered definition describes exactly bounded sequences.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>2 shows that vercongence implies boundedness, and step <1>3 shows that
boundedness implies vercongence.  Moreover, step <1>3 shows that the proposed
“limit” $a$ carries no information: a bounded sequence verconges to every
real number.
:::

<1>5. The constant sequence $a_n=0$ is an example of a vercongent sequence.

::: {.proof}
It is bounded, so step <1>3 applies.  For example, it verconges to $0$ with
$\varepsilon=1$.
:::

<1>6. The sequence
$$
a_n=(-1)^n
$$
is vercongent but divergent in the usual sense.

::: {.proof}
The sequence is bounded, hence vercongent by step <1>3.  In particular,
$$
|a_n-0|=1<2
$$
for every $n$, so it verconges to $0$ with $\varepsilon=2$.

On the other hand, the subsequence of even-indexed terms is constantly $1$,
while the subsequence of odd-indexed terms is constantly $-1$.  Since these
subsequences have different limits, $(a_n)$ does not converge in the usual
sense.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>4--<1>6 give the exact characterization and the two requested
examples.
:::
:::
