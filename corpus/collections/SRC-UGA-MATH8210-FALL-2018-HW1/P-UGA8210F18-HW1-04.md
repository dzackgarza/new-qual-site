::: {.problem}
In this exercise you will show that $S^{n}$ is a manifold which can be covered by two charts.
Think of $S^{n} \subset \RR^{n+1}$ as the set
$$
S^{n} = \{ x_{1}^{2} + \cdots + x_{n+1}^{2} = 1 : (x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n+1}) \in \RR^{n+1} \}.
$$

Write $N = (0, \ldots, 0, 1)$ and $S = (0, \ldots, 0, -1)$. (These are the north and south poles.)

- Define a map $p : S^{n} \setminus N \to \RR^{n}$ as follows.
  Let $x \in S^{n} \setminus N$. There is a unique line $\ell_{x}$ between $N$ and $x$. This line intersects the plane $\{x_{n+1} = 0\}$ in one point.
  This point is $p(x)$. Identify $\RR^{n}$ with the set
  $$
  \{ (x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n+1}) : x_{n+1} = 0 \}
  $$
  so that $p$ really is a map to $\RR^{n}$.
  First, check that $p(x)$ is well-defined: why does $\ell_{x}$ intersect $\{x_{n+1} = 0\}$ in exactly one point?
- Write down a nice expression for $p(x)$ in terms of $(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n+1})$. (Hint: your expression shouldn't make sense if you try to set $x = N$.)
- Show that $p$ has an inverse and write down a nice expression for it.
- $p^{-1}(\RR^{n})$ covers most of the sphere.
  Find another good chart which covers the rest.
- Show that $S^{n}$ cannot be covered by a single chart.
:::

::: {.solution}

Write a point of $\RR^{n+1}$ as
$$
(u,t),
\qquad
u\in\RR^n,\quad t\in\RR.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

For every $x=(u,t)\in S^n\setminus\{N\}$, the line through $N$ and
$x$ meets the hyperplane
$$
\RR^n\times\{0\}
$$
in exactly one point.


::: pf-proof

The line through
$$
N=(0,1)
$$
and $x=(u,t)$ is
$$
\ell_x(s)
=
N+s(x-N)
=
(su,\ 1+s(t-1)).
$$
It meets the hyperplane $x_{n+1}=0$ exactly when
$$
1+s(t-1)=0.
$$
Since $x\ne N$, one has $t\ne1$, so this equation has the unique solution
$$
s=\frac1{1-t}.
$$
Thus the intersection exists and is unique.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The stereographic projection from the north pole is
$$
\boxed{
p_N:S^n\setminus\{N\}\longrightarrow\RR^n,
\qquad
p_N(u,t)=\frac{u}{1-t}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Substitute the value
$$
s=\frac1{1-t}
$$
from step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} into the first $n$ coordinates $su$ of the line.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Define
$$
q_N:\RR^n\longrightarrow S^n\setminus\{N\}
$$
by
$$
\boxed{
q_N(y)
=
\left(
\frac{2y}{1+\norm{y}^2},
\frac{\norm{y}^2-1}{1+\norm{y}^2}
\right)}.
$$
Then $q_N$ is well-defined.


::: pf-proof

Let
$$
r^2=\norm{y}^2.
$$
The squared Euclidean norm of the displayed vector is
$$
\frac{4r^2+(r^2-1)^2}{(1+r^2)^2}
=
\frac{r^4+2r^2+1}{(1+r^2)^2}
=1.
$$
Thus $q_N(y)\in S^n$.

Its last coordinate is
$$
\frac{r^2-1}{r^2+1}<1
$$
for every finite $r$, so $q_N(y)\ne N$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The maps $p_N$ and $q_N$ are mutual inverses.


::: pf-proof

For $y\in\RR^n$, step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} gives
$$
1-\frac{\norm{y}^2-1}{1+\norm{y}^2}
=
\frac{2}{1+\norm{y}^2}.
$$
Hence
$$
p_N(q_N(y))
=
\frac{\frac{2y}{1+\norm{y}^2}}
{\frac{2}{1+\norm{y}^2}}
=y.
$$

Conversely, let $x=(u,t)\in S^n\setminus\{N\}$ and set
$$
y=p_N(x)=\frac{u}{1-t}.
$$
Since
$$
\norm{u}^2+t^2=1,
$$
one has
$$
\norm{y}^2
=
\frac{\norm{u}^2}{(1-t)^2}
=
\frac{1-t^2}{(1-t)^2}
=
\frac{1+t}{1-t}.
$$
Therefore
$$
1+\norm{y}^2
=
\frac{2}{1-t}.
$$
The first $n$ coordinates of $q_N(y)$ are then
$$
\frac{2y}{1+\norm{y}^2}
=
\frac{2u/(1-t)}{2/(1-t)}
=u,
$$
and the final coordinate is
$$
\frac{\norm{y}^2-1}{1+\norm{y}^2}
=
\frac{\frac{1+t}{1-t}-1}{\frac{2}{1-t}}
=t.
$$
Thus $q_N(p_N(x))=x$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The second stereographic chart is projection from the south pole
$$
S=(0,-1):
$$
$$
\boxed{
p_S:S^n\setminus\{S\}\longrightarrow\RR^n,
\qquad
p_S(u,t)=\frac{u}{1+t}}.
$$
Its inverse is
$$
\boxed{
q_S(y)
=
\left(
\frac{2y}{1+\norm{y}^2},
\frac{1-\norm{y}^2}{1+\norm{y}^2}
\right)}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Repeat steps [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} with the line through
$$
S=(0,-1)
$$
and $(u,t)$. Its last coordinate is
$$
-1+s(t+1),
$$
which vanishes uniquely at
$$
s=\frac1{1+t}.
$$
This gives the formula for $p_S$.

The formula for $q_S$ is verified exactly as for $q_N$, with the sign of the
last coordinate reversed.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The two chart domains cover the sphere:
$$
\boxed{
S^n
=
\bigl(S^n\setminus\{N\}\bigr)
\cup
\bigl(S^n\setminus\{S\}\bigr)}.
$$


::: pf-proof

The north and south poles are distinct, so every point of $S^n$ lies in at
least one of the two complements.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

If $n\ge1$, the sphere $S^n$ cannot be covered by a single chart.


::: pf-proof

Suppose a single chart covered all of $S^n$. Then there would be a
homeomorphism
$$
\phi:S^n\longrightarrow U
$$
onto an open subset $U\subseteq\RR^n$.

The sphere $S^n$ is compact, so $U$ would be compact. But $U$ is nonempty
and open. If $U=\RR^n$, then $U$ is unbounded and hence not compact.
If $U$ is a proper subset, then because $\RR^n$ is connected for
$n\ge1$, the nonempty proper open set $U$ cannot also be closed. By the
Heine--Borel theorem, a compact subset of $\RR^n$ must be closed and
bounded. Thus $U$ is not compact in either case, a contradiction.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

For $n=0$, the sphere $S^0$ also cannot be covered by a single
$0$-dimensional chart.


::: pf-proof

The space
$$
S^0=\{-1,1\}
$$
has two points, whereas
$$
\RR^0
$$
has one point. The only nonempty open subset of $\RR^0$ is
$\RR^0$ itself, so no chart domain equal to all of $S^0$ can be
homeomorphic to an open subset of $\RR^0$.

:::
