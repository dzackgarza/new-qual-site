::: {.problem}
Let $Y=(0,1)\times(0,1)$ and let $X=Y\cup\{*\}$.
Let $\mathcal T_1:=\{U\mid U\text{ is a Euclidean open subset of }Y\}$, $\mathcal T_2:=\{X-C\mid C\text{ is a compact subset of the Euclidean space }Y\}$, and let $\mathcal T:=\mathcal T_1\cup\mathcal T_2$.
Then $(X,\mathcal T)$ is a topological space called the one-point compactification of $(0,1)\times(0,1)$ (you do not need to prove this).
Show that the space $(X,\mathcal T)$ is compact.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Cover the point at infinity $*$:
<2>1. Let $\mathcal{U} = \{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be an arbitrary open cover of $(X, \mathcal{T})$, so $X = \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha$.
Proof: setup.
<2>2. Since $* \in X$, there exists some $\alpha_0 \in A$ such that $* \in U_{\alpha_0}$.
Proof: $\mathcal{U}$ covers $X$.
<2>3. By definition of the topology $\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{T}_1 \cup \mathcal{T}_2$, any open set containing $*$ must belong to $\mathcal{T}_2$.
Therefore $U_{\alpha_0} = X \setminus C_0$ for some compact subset $C_0 \subseteq Y$.
Proof: definition of $\mathcal{T}_2$.

<1>2. Cover the remaining compact set $C_0$:
<2>1. The complement of $U_{\alpha_0}$ in $X$ is:
\[
X \setminus U_{\alpha_0} = X \setminus (X \setminus C_0) = C_0 \subseteq Y.
\]
Proof: set difference.
<2>2. For each $\alpha \in A$, define $V_\alpha = U_\alpha \cap Y$.
- If $U_\alpha \in \mathcal{T}_1$, $V_\alpha = U_\alpha$ is open in the Euclidean topology on $Y$.
- If $U_\alpha \in \mathcal{T}_2$, $U_\alpha = X \setminus C_\alpha$ for compact $C_\alpha \subseteq Y$.
  Since $Y$ is a metric space (hence Hausdorff), $C_\alpha$ is closed in $Y$, so $V_\alpha = Y \setminus C_\alpha$ is open in $Y$.
In either case, each $V_\alpha$ is open in $Y$.
Proof: compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed.
<2>3. Since $\mathcal{U}$ covers $X$, the collection $\{V_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ is an open cover of $C_0$ in $Y$:
\[
C_0 \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} V_\alpha.
\]
Proof: $C_0 \subseteq X = \bigcup U_\alpha$ and $C_0 \subseteq Y$.

<1>3. Extract a finite subcover of $X$:
<2>1. Since $C_0$ is compact in $Y$, there exist finitely many indices $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n \in A$ such that:
\[
C_0 \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n V_{\alpha_i} \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n U_{\alpha_i}.
\]
Proof: definition of compactness for $C_0$.
<2>2. Combining $U_{\alpha_0}$ with this finite collection gives:
\[
X = U_{\alpha_0} \cup C_0 \subseteq U_{\alpha_0} \cup \left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n U_{\alpha_i}\right).
\]
Proof: $X = U_{\alpha_0} \cup (X \setminus U_{\alpha_0})$.
<2>3. Thus $\{U_{\alpha_0}, U_{\alpha_1}, \dots, U_{\alpha_n}\} \subseteq \mathcal{U}$ is a finite subcover of $X$.
Proof: <2>2.

<1>4. Conclusion:
Every open cover of $(X, \mathcal{T})$ has a finite subcover, so $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is compact. Q.E.D.
Proof: <1>1 through <1>3.
:::
