::: {.problem}
A subset of $\mathbb R$ is called $F_\sigma$ if it is a countable union of closed sets and $G_\delta$ if it is a countable intersection of open sets.

(a) Prove that $A$ is $G_\delta$ if and only if $A^c$ is $F_\sigma$.

(b) Show that a closed interval $[a,b]$ is $G_\delta$; that $[a,b)$ is both $G_\delta$ and $F_\sigma$; and that $\mathbb Q$ is $F_\sigma$ while $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ is $G_\delta$.

(c) Show that every closed set is $G_\delta$ and every open set is $F_\sigma$. Give an example of an $F_\sigma$ set that is not $G_\delta$, and an example of a set that is neither $F_\sigma$ nor $G_\delta$.

Hint for the first example: use that $\mathbb R$ is not a countable union of nowhere-dense sets.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If
$$
A=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n
$$
with each $U_n$ open, then
$$
A^c=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n^c
$$
is $F_\sigma$.

::: {.proof}
By De Morgan's law,
$$
\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n\right)^c
=
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n^c.
$$
Each $U_n^c$ is closed, so the right-hand side is a countable union of closed
sets.
:::

<1>2. If
$$
A^c=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_n
$$
with each $F_n$ closed, then
$$
A=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}F_n^c
$$
is $G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
Again by De Morgan,
$$
A
=
(A^c)^c
=
\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_n\right)^c
=
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}F_n^c.
$$
Each $F_n^c$ is open.
:::

<1>3. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
A\text{ is }G_\delta
\iff
A^c\text{ is }F_\sigma.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>2 prove the two implications.
:::

<1>4. Part (a) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>3.
:::

<1>5. Every closed interval $[a,b]$ is $G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
One has
$$
[a,b]
=
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left(a-\frac1n,b+\frac1n\right).
$$
Each set on the right is open.

Indeed, every point of $[a,b]$ lies in every displayed interval. Conversely,
if $x<a$, choose $n$ with $1/n<a-x$; then
$x\notin(a-1/n,b+1/n)$. The case $x>b$ is analogous.
:::

<1>6. The half-open interval $[a,b)$ is $G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
One has
$$
[a,b)
=
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left(a-\frac1n,b\right).
$$
Each set is open. The same endpoint argument as in step <1>5 shows equality.
:::

<1>7. The half-open interval $[a,b)$ is $F_\sigma$.

::: {.proof}
Choose $N$ so large that
$$
\frac1N<b-a.
$$
Then
$$
[a,b)
=
\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}
\left[a,b-\frac1n\right].
$$
Every set on the right is closed. If $x<b$, choose $n$ sufficiently large
that
$$
\frac1n<b-x;
$$
then $x\leq b-1/n$, so every $x\in[a,b)$ belongs to the union.
:::

<1>8. The rational numbers $\QQ$ form an $F_\sigma$ set.

::: {.proof}
Enumerate
$$
\QQ=\{q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots\}.
$$
Then
$$
\QQ
=
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{q_n\}.
$$
Each singleton is closed.
:::

<1>9. The irrational numbers $\RR\setminus\QQ$ form a $G_\delta$ set.

::: {.proof}
This follows from steps <1>3 and <1>8 by taking complements.
:::

<1>10. Part (b) follows.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>5--<1>9 establish all four requested assertions.
:::

<1>11. Every closed subset $F\subseteq\RR$ is $G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
For $n\geq1$, define
$$
U_n
=
\bigcup_{y\in F}
\left(y-\frac1n,y+\frac1n\right).
$$
Each $U_n$ is open, and clearly
$$
F\subseteq\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n.
$$

Now let $x\notin F$. Since $F$ is closed, $F^c$ is open, so there exists
$\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\cap F=\varnothing.
$$
Choose $n$ with $1/n<\varepsilon$. Then $x\notin U_n$. Therefore
$$
F
=
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n.
$$
:::

<1>12. Every open subset of $\RR$ is $F_\sigma$.

::: {.proof}
Let $U$ be open. Then $U^c$ is closed, hence $G_\delta$ by step <1>11.
Part (a) then implies that
$$
U=(U^c)^c
$$
is $F_\sigma$.
:::

<1>13. The set $\QQ$ is not $G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
Suppose
$$
\QQ
=
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n
$$
with every $U_n$ open. Since $\QQ$ is dense and
$$
\QQ\subseteq U_n,
$$
each $U_n$ is dense.

Set
$$
F_n=\RR\setminus U_n.
$$
Then each $F_n$ is closed and has empty interior, hence is nowhere dense in
the standard Baire-category sense. Also,
$$
\RR\setminus\QQ
=
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_n.
$$

But
$$
\QQ
=
\bigcup_{q\in\QQ}\{q\},
$$
and every singleton is nowhere dense. Thus $\RR$ would be a countable union
of nowhere-dense sets:
$$
\RR
=
\QQ\cup(\RR\setminus\QQ).
$$
This contradicts the Baire category theorem for $\RR$.
:::

<1>14. Hence $\QQ$ is an $F_\sigma$ set that is not $G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>8 gives the $F_\sigma$ property, and step <1>13 excludes the
$G_\delta$ property.
:::

<1>15. There are at most continuum-many $F_\sigma$ subsets of $\RR$ and at
most continuum-many $G_\delta$ subsets of $\RR$.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
\mathfrak c=|\RR|=2^{\aleph_0}.
$$
Every open subset of $\RR$ is determined by which rational-endpoint open
intervals it contains in a countable basis. Hence there are at most
$$
2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c
$$
open subsets of $\RR$, and therefore at most $\mathfrak c$ closed subsets.

An $F_\sigma$ set is determined by a sequence of closed sets. The number of
such sequences is at most
$$
\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}
=(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}
=2^{\aleph_0}
=\mathfrak c.
$$
The same argument applies to countable sequences of open sets and therefore to
$G_\delta$ sets.
:::

<1>16. There exists a subset of $\RR$ that is neither $F_\sigma$ nor
$G_\delta$.

::: {.proof}
By Cantor's theorem,
$$
|\mathcal P(\RR)|
=
2^{\mathfrak c}
>
\mathfrak c.
$$
By step <1>15, the union of the families of all $F_\sigma$ sets and all
$G_\delta$ sets has cardinality at most $\mathfrak c$. Hence some subset
$$
E\subseteq\RR
$$
belongs to neither family. Choosing such an $E$ gives the required example.
:::

<1>17. Part (c) follows.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>11 and <1>12 prove the general closed/open assertions, step <1>14
gives an $F_\sigma$ set that is not $G_\delta$, and step <1>16 gives a set
that is neither.
:::

<1>18. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>4, <1>10, and <1>17 establish parts (a), (b), and (c), respectively.
:::
