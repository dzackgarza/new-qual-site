::: {.problem}
Let $B$ be the set of all Bernoulli sequences $(x_n)$ with $x_n\in\{0,1\}$.

(a) Prove that
\[
\rho((x_n),(y_n))
=
\sum_{n=0}^\infty 2^{-n}|x_n-y_n|
\]
defines a metric on $B$.

(b) Prove that the set of all Bernoulli sequences beginning with $0,1$ is both open and closed.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. For every $x=(x_n),y=(y_n)\in B$, the series
$$
\rho(x,y)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}|x_n-y_n|
$$
converges.

::: {.proof}
Since $x_n,y_n\in\{0,1\}$,
$$
0\leq |x_n-y_n|\leq1.
$$
Hence
$$
0
\leq
2^{-n}|x_n-y_n|
\leq
2^{-n}.
$$
The geometric series
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}=2
$$
converges, so comparison gives convergence of the defining series.
:::

<1>2. The function $\rho$ is nonnegative and symmetric.

::: {.proof}
Every summand is nonnegative, so $\rho(x,y)\geq0$. Also,
$$
|x_n-y_n|=|y_n-x_n|
$$
for every $n$, hence
$$
\rho(x,y)=\rho(y,x).
$$
:::

<1>3. One has
$$
\rho(x,y)=0
\iff
x=y.
$$

::: {.proof}
If $x=y$, then every summand is zero, so $\rho(x,y)=0$.

Conversely, suppose $\rho(x,y)=0$. Since all summands are nonnegative, every
summand must be zero:
$$
2^{-n}|x_n-y_n|=0
$$
for every $n$. Thus
$$
x_n=y_n
$$
for every $n$, so $x=y$.
:::

<1>4. The triangle inequality holds:
$$
\rho(x,z)
\leq
\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z).
$$

::: {.proof}
For every $n$, the ordinary triangle inequality in $\RR$ gives
$$
|x_n-z_n|
\leq
|x_n-y_n|+|y_n-z_n|.
$$
Multiplying by $2^{-n}$ and summing gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(x,z)
&=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}|x_n-z_n|\\
&\leq
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}|x_n-y_n|
+
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}|y_n-z_n|\\
&=
\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z).
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>5. Therefore $\rho$ is a metric on $B$.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1--<1>4 verify that $\rho$ is well-defined and satisfies all metric
axioms.
:::

<1>6. Part (a) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>5.
:::

<1>7. Let
$$
C=
\{x=(x_n)\in B:x_0=0,\ x_1=1\}.
$$
If $x\in C$ and
$$
\rho(x,y)<\frac12,
$$
then $y\in C$.

::: {.proof}
If $y_0\neq x_0$, then
$$
\rho(x,y)
\geq
2^0|x_0-y_0|
=1,
$$
contrary to $\rho(x,y)<1/2$.

If $y_1\neq x_1$, then
$$
\rho(x,y)
\geq
2^{-1}|x_1-y_1|
=\frac12,
$$
again a contradiction.

Hence
$$
y_0=x_0=0,
\qquad
y_1=x_1=1,
$$
so $y\in C$.
:::

<1>8. The set $C$ is open.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>7, for every $x\in C$,
$$
B_{1/2}(x)\subseteq C.
$$
Thus every point of $C$ has an open ball contained in $C$.
:::

<1>9. If $x\in B\setminus C$ and
$$
\rho(x,y)<\frac12,
$$
then $y\in B\setminus C$.

::: {.proof}
Since $x\notin C$, either $x_0=1$ or else $x_0=0$ and $x_1=0$.

If $x_0=1$, then the argument from step <1>7 shows that
$\rho(x,y)<1/2$ forces
$$
y_0=x_0=1,
$$
so $y\notin C$.

If $x_0=0$ and $x_1=0$, then $\rho(x,y)<1/2$ forces
$$
y_0=0,
\qquad
y_1=0,
$$
again by the coordinate-weight estimates from step <1>7. Hence $y\notin C$.
:::

<1>10. The complement $B\setminus C$ is open.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>9, for every $x\in B\setminus C$,
$$
B_{1/2}(x)\subseteq B\setminus C.
$$
Hence the complement is open.
:::

<1>11. The set $C$ is closed.

::: {.proof}
A subset of a metric space is closed exactly when its complement is open.
Apply step <1>10.
:::

<1>12. Part (b) follows:
$$
\boxed{C\text{ is both open and closed}.}
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>8 proves openness and step <1>11 proves closedness.
:::

<1>13. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>6 and <1>12 establish parts (a) and (b), respectively.
:::
:::
