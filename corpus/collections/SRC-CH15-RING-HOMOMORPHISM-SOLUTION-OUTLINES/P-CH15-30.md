::: {.problem}
Prove that the sum of the squares of three consecutive integers can not be a square.
:::

::: {.solution}
The collection retains its solution outline as `assets/attachments/Ch15Sltns.pdf`, and entry "# 30" of that outline covers Gallian 7e Chapter 15 Exercise 30. The outline writes the sum for a starting integer $n$ and reduces it modulo $3$. The same computation is retained, followed by an exhaustive check of square residues.

Let $n\in\ZZ$ be arbitrary, so that $n,n+1,n+2$ are arbitrary three consecutive integers. Define
$$
N=n^{2}+(n+1)^{2}+(n+2)^{2}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #residue}
$N\equiv2\pmod3$ for every $n\in\ZZ$.

::: pf-proof
Expanding the squares gives
$$
N=n^{2}+(n^{2}+2n+1)+(n^{2}+4n+4)=3n^{2}+6n+5.
$$
The terms $3n^{2}$ and $6n$ are divisible by $3$, while $5\equiv2\pmod3$. Hence $N\equiv2\pmod3$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The sum $N$ cannot be a perfect square.

::: pf-proof
Suppose instead that $N=N^{\prime2}$ for some integer $N^{\prime}$. Reducing modulo $3$ gives $N^{\prime2}\equiv N\equiv2\pmod3$. But every integer is congruent modulo $3$ to $0,1$, or $2$, and their squares are respectively congruent to $0,1$, and $1$. Thus no integer square is congruent to $2$ modulo $3$, contradicting step [](#residue){.pf-ref}.
:::
