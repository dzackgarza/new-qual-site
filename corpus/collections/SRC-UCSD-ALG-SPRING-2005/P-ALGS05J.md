::: {.problem}
Classify all rings with identity elements that have nine elements.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

Let $R$ be a ring with identity and $\cardinality{R}=9$. Its additive group is isomorphic either to $C_9$ or to $C_3\times C_3$.

::: pf-proof

These are the two abelian groups of order $9$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

If $\operatorname{char}R=9$, then
$$
R\cong\ZZ/9\ZZ.
$$

::: pf-proof

The subring generated by $1_R$ is the image of the canonical homomorphism
$$
\ZZ\longrightarrow R,
\qquad
n\longmapsto n1_R.
$$
If $\operatorname{char}R=9$, this image has $9$ elements. Since $R$ itself has $9$ elements, the image is all of $R$, giving the asserted isomorphism.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If $\operatorname{char}R=3$, then $R$ is a two-dimensional algebra over $\FF_3$.

::: pf-proof

The prime subring generated by $1_R$ is $\FF_3$. Since the additive group has order $3^2$, it is a two-dimensional vector space over $\FF_3$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

In the characteristic-$3$ case, choose $x\in R\setminus\FF_3 1$. Then $R=\FF_3[x]$ and
$$
R\cong\FF_3[t]/(q(t))
$$
for a monic quadratic polynomial $q$.

::: pf-proof

The vectors $1,x$ are linearly independent, hence form an $\FF_3$-basis of the two-dimensional vector space $R$. Thus every element of $R$ is of the form $a+bx$, so $R=\FF_3[x]$.

Because $1,x$ is a basis, there exist $a,b\in\FF_3$ with
$$
x^2=ax+b.
$$
Hence the evaluation map $\FF_3[t]\to R$ is surjective and its kernel contains the monic quadratic
$$
q(t)=t^2-at-b.
$$
The quotient by $(q)$ has $3^2=9$ elements, the same number as $R$, so the induced surjection
$$
\FF_3[t]/(q)\longrightarrow R
$$
is an isomorphism.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

If $q$ is irreducible, then
$$
R\cong\FF_9.
$$

::: pf-proof

The quotient of $\FF_3[t]$ by an irreducible quadratic is a field with $3^2=9$ elements. All fields of order $9$ are isomorphic.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

If $q$ splits as two distinct linear factors, then
$$
R\cong\FF_3\times\FF_3.
$$

::: pf-proof

Write
$$
q(t)=(t-a)(t-b),
\qquad a\ne b.
$$
The two linear ideals are comaximal, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives
$$
\FF_3[t]/(q)
\cong
\FF_3[t]/(t-a)\times\FF_3[t]/(t-b)
\cong
\FF_3\times\FF_3.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s7}

If $q$ has a repeated linear factor, then
$$
R\cong\FF_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
$$

::: pf-proof

Write $q(t)=(t-a)^2$. Replacing $t$ by $\varepsilon+a$ gives
$$
\FF_3[t]/((t-a)^2)
\cong
\FF_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The four rings
$$
\ZZ/9\ZZ,
\qquad
\FF_9,
\qquad
\FF_3\times\FF_3,
\qquad
\FF_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)
$$
are pairwise nonisomorphic.

::: pf-proof

The first has characteristic $9$, whereas the other three have characteristic $3$. Among the characteristic-$3$ rings, $\FF_9$ is a field; $\FF_3\times\FF_3$ is reduced but has nontrivial idempotents; and $\FF_3[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ has the nonzero nilpotent element $\varepsilon$. These properties are preserved by ring isomorphism.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

These four rings exhaust all rings with identity having nine elements.

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1){.pf-ref}, the characteristic is either $9$ or $3$. The characteristic-$9$ case is step [](#s2){.pf-ref}. In characteristic $3$, step [](#s4){.pf-ref} reduces the classification to the three possible factorization types of a quadratic polynomial, handled in steps [](#s5){.pf-ref}, [](#s6){.pf-ref} and [](#s7){.pf-ref}.

:::
