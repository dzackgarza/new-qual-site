::: {.exercise}
If $R$ is a ufd in which all nonzero prime ideals are minimal, prove $R$ is a pid.
(See DF, problem 6, parts a, b, c, page 283.)
:::


::: {.solution}
Let $R$ be a UFD in which every nonzero prime ideal is minimal among nonzero
prime ideals.

<1>1. Every nonzero prime ideal of $R$ is maximal.
::: {.proof}
Let $P$ be a nonzero prime ideal. Since $P$ is proper, it is contained in a
maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$. Every maximal ideal is prime, and
$\mathfrak m\ne0$ because it contains the nonzero ideal $P$.

By hypothesis, $\mathfrak m$ is a minimal nonzero prime ideal. But
$$
0\subsetneq P\subseteq\mathfrak m
$$
with $P$ prime. Minimality of $\mathfrak m$ therefore forces
$$
P=\mathfrak m.
$$
Thus every nonzero prime ideal is maximal.
:::

<1>2. Set up induction for a nonzero ideal.
::: {.proof}
Let $I\ne0$ be an ideal. For each nonzero $a\in I$, write a factorization
$$
a=u p_1\cdots p_r
$$
with $u$ a unit and the $p_i$ irreducible; if $a$ is a unit, take $r=0$.
Let
$$
r(I)
$$
be the minimum possible number $r$. This minimum exists because it is the
minimum of a nonempty set of nonnegative integers.

We prove by induction on $r(I)$ that $I$ is principal.
:::

<1>3. The case $r(I)=0$ is principal.
::: {.proof}
If $r(I)=0$, then $I$ contains a unit. Hence
$$
I=R=(1),
$$
which is principal.
:::

<1>4. In the inductive step, a minimal factorization forces $I\subseteq(p_1)$.
::: {.proof}
Assume $r(I)=n>0$ and the result known for ideals with smaller value of $r$.
Choose
$$
u p_1\cdots p_n\in I
$$
with $u$ a unit and each $p_i$ irreducible. Multiplying by $u^{-1}$, we may
assume
$$
p_1\cdots p_n\in I.
$$

In a UFD, the irreducible $p_1$ is prime, so $(p_1)$ is a nonzero prime ideal.
By step <1>1, $(p_1)$ is maximal.

Suppose $I\nsubseteq(p_1)$. Choose
$$
b\in I\setminus(p_1).
$$
Then the ideal $(p_1)+(b)$ strictly contains the maximal ideal $(p_1)$, hence
is all of $R$. Thus there exist $c,d\in R$ such that
$$
bc+dp_1=1.
$$
Multiply by $p_2\cdots p_n$ (interpreted as $1$ when $n=1$):
$$
bc\,p_2\cdots p_n+d\,p_1p_2\cdots p_n=p_2\cdots p_n.
$$
Both terms on the left lie in $I$, so
$$
p_2\cdots p_n\in I.
$$
This is a product of only $n-1$ irreducibles, contradicting the minimality of
$r(I)=n$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I\subseteq(p_1).}
$$
:::

<1>5. Reduce to an ideal with smaller factorization length.
::: {.proof}
Define the colon ideal
$$
J=(I:p_1)=\{x\in R:xp_1\in I\}.
$$
It is an ideal: closure under subtraction and multiplication by arbitrary
ring elements follows immediately from the definition.

Since $I\subseteq(p_1)$, every $y\in I$ can be written
$$
y=xp_1
$$
for some $x\in R$, and then $x\in J$. Hence
$$
I=p_1J.
$$
Also
$$
p_1p_2\cdots p_n\in I
$$
implies
$$
p_2\cdots p_n\in J.
$$
Therefore
$$
r(J)\le n-1<n.
$$
By the induction hypothesis, $J$ is principal, say
$$
J=(a).
$$
Then
$$
I=p_1J=(p_1a)
$$
is principal.
:::

<1>6. Conclude that $R$ is a PID.
::: {.proof}
The zero ideal is principal, and steps <1>2--<1>5 show that every nonzero ideal
is principal. Since $R$ is a UFD, it is in particular an integral domain.
Therefore
$$
\boxed{R\text{ is a PID}.}
$$
:::
:::
