::: {.exercise}
Prove that $G$ is solvable if and only if some repeated commutator subgroup $G^{(n)} = \ts{e}$.

[The commutator of $x$ and $y$ is $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$; note $x$ and $y$ commute iff their commutator is 1. The commutator subgroup $G'$ is generated by all commutators, and the commutator of the commutator is denoted $G^{(2)}$, and so on.]
:::

::: {.solution}
Write
$$
G^{(0)}=G,
\qquad
G^{(k+1)}=(G^{(k)})'.
$$

<1>1. If the derived series reaches the identity, then $G$ is solvable.
::: {.proof}
Assume
$$
G^{(n)}=1
$$
for some $n$. For every $k$, the commutator subgroup
$G^{(k+1)}=(G^{(k)})'$ is characteristic, hence normal, in $G^{(k)}$.
Moreover the quotient
$$
G^{(k)}/G^{(k+1)}
$$
is abelian, because quotienting a group by its commutator subgroup gives an
abelian group.

Therefore
$$
G=G^{(0)}\trianglerighteq G^{(1)}\trianglerighteq\cdots
\trianglerighteq G^{(n)}=1
$$
is an abelian subnormal tower. Hence $G$ is solvable.
:::

<1>2. If $G$ is solvable, then its derived series reaches the identity.
::: {.proof}
Suppose $G$ is solvable. Then there is an abelian subnormal tower
$$
G=H_0\trianglerighteq H_1\trianglerighteq\cdots
\trianglerighteq H_r=1
$$
such that every quotient
$$
H_i/H_{i+1}
$$
is abelian.

Because $H_i/H_{i+1}$ is abelian, the commutator subgroup of $H_i$ lies in
$H_{i+1}$:
$$
H_i'\le H_{i+1}.
$$
We prove by induction that
$$
G^{(i)}\le H_i
$$
for $0\le i\le r$. The case $i=0$ is equality. If
$G^{(i)}\le H_i$, then taking commutator subgroups gives
$$
G^{(i+1)}=(G^{(i)})'
\le H_i'
\le H_{i+1}.
$$
Thus the claim holds for every $i$. In particular,
$$
G^{(r)}\le H_r=1,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{G^{(r)}=1.}
$$
:::

Combining the two directions,
$$
\boxed{G\text{ is solvable}\iff G^{(n)}=1
\text{ for some }n.}
$$
:::
