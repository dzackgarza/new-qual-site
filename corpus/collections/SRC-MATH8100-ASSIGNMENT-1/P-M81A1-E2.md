::: problem
Prove that a nonempty complete metric space cannot be a countable union of nowhere dense sets.

As part of the proof, show that a nested sequence of nonempty closed bounded sets whose diameters tend to zero has a one-point intersection.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Cantor's Intersection Theorem:
<2>1. Let $(X, d)$ be a complete metric space, and let $(F_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ be a nested sequence of non-empty closed sets ($F_1 \supseteq F_2 \supseteq \cdots$) such that $\lim_{n \to \infty} \operatorname{diam}(F_n) = 0$.
::: {.proof}
hypothesis.
:::
<2>2. For each $n \ge 1$, choose an element $x_n \in F_n$.
For any $m \ge n$, $x_m \in F_m \subseteq F_n$, so:
\[
d(x_n, x_m) \le \operatorname{diam}(F_n) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.
\]
Thus $(x_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ is a Cauchy sequence in $X$.
::: {.proof}
diameter bound on tail elements.
:::
<2>3. Since $X$ is complete, there exists $x \in X$ such that $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = x$.
For any fixed $k \ge 1$, the sequence $(x_n)_{n \ge k} \subseteq F_k$. Since $F_k$ is closed, $x = \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n \in F_k$.
Thus $x \in \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty F_k$.
::: {.proof}
closed sets contain all their limit points.
:::
<2>4. If $y \in \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty F_k$, then $d(x, y) \le \operatorname{diam}(F_k)$ for all $k$.
Taking $k \to \infty$ gives $d(x, y) = 0 \implies x = y$.
Thus $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty F_n = \{x\}$ is a unique single point.
::: {.proof}
uniqueness from vanishing diameter.
:::

<1>2. Baire Category Theorem:
<2>1. Let $(E_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ be a countable collection of nowhere dense subsets of $X$, so $\operatorname{int}(\overline{E_n}) = \emptyset$ for each $n$.
Let $B_0 = B(x_0, r_0) \subset X$ be an arbitrary non-empty open ball.
::: {.proof}
setup.
:::
<2>2. Since $\overline{E_1}$ has empty interior, the open set $B_0 \setminus \overline{E_1}$ is non-empty.
Choose a point $x_1 \in B_0 \setminus \overline{E_1}$ and a radius $r_1 \in (0, 1)$ such that the closed ball $\overline{B(x_1, r_1)} \subset B_0 \setminus \overline{E_1}$.
::: {.proof}
metric balls form a basis for the topology.
:::
<2>3. Inductively, given the open ball $B_{n-1} = B(x_{n-1}, r_{n-1})$, the open set $B_{n-1} \setminus \overline{E_n}$ is non-empty because $\operatorname{int}(\overline{E_n}) = \emptyset$.
Choose $x_n$ and $0 < r_n < 2^{-n}$ such that:
\[
\overline{B_n} = \overline{B(x_n, r_n)} \subset B_{n-1} \setminus \overline{E_n}.
\]
::: {.proof}
induction step.
:::
<2>4. The sequence of closed sets $F_n = \overline{B_n}$ satisfies $F_1 \supseteq F_2 \supseteq \cdots$ and $\operatorname{diam}(F_n) \le 2 r_n < 2^{-(n-1)} \to 0$.
By Cantor's Intersection Theorem (<1>1), there exists a point:
\[
x \in \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \overline{B_n}.
\]
::: {.proof}
application of <1>1 to $F_n = \overline{B_n}$.
:::
<2>5. For every $n \ge 1$, $x \in \overline{B_n} \subset X \setminus \overline{E_n} \subseteq X \setminus E_n$.
Therefore $x \notin \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$.
Moreover, $x \in \overline{B_1} \subset B_0$, so $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$ cannot contain $B_0$.
In particular, $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \neq X$.
::: {.proof}
non-membership in any $E_n$.
:::

<1>3. Conclusion:
A non-empty complete metric space cannot be a countable union of nowhere dense sets. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
<1>1 and <1>2.
:::
