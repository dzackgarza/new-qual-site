::: problem
For $x>0$, let
$$
g(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+n^2x}.
$$

1. Prove that the series is not uniformly convergent on $(0,\infty)$, but its sum is continuous there.

2. Determine whether $g$ is differentiable on $(0,\infty)$ and whether $g'$ is continuous there.
:::

::: {.solution}
Write
\[
u_n(x)=\frac{1}{1+n^2x}.
\]
For every fixed $x>0$,
\[
0\le u_n(x)\le \frac{1}{n^2x}
\qquad(n\ge1),
\]
so the series defining $g(x)$ converges.

<1>1. The convergence is not uniform on $(0,\infty)$, but the sum is continuous there.
::: {.proof}
Uniform convergence of a series would force its terms to converge uniformly to $0$. But for every $n\ge1$,
\[
\sup_{x>0}u_n(x)=1,
\]
because $u_n(x)\to1$ as $x\downarrow0$. Thus $u_n\not\to0$ uniformly, so the series cannot converge uniformly on $(0,\infty)$.

Now fix $a>0$. For $x\ge a$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
0\le u_n(x)
\le \frac{1}{n^2a}.
\]
Since
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2a}<\infty,
\]
the Weierstrass $M$-test gives uniform convergence on $[a,\infty)$. Each $u_n$ is continuous, hence $g$ is continuous on $[a,\infty)$. Since every $x_0>0$ lies in such an interval, $g$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.
:::

<1>2. The function is differentiable, and $g'$ is continuous.
::: {.proof}
For $n\ge1$,
\[
u_n'(x)=-\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
\]
Fix again $a>0$. For $x\ge a$,
\[
|u_n'(x)|
\le \frac{n^2}{n^4a^2}
=\frac{1}{n^2a^2}.
\]
The series
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2a^2}
\]
converges, so the derivative series converges uniformly on $[a,\infty)$. Since the original series converges at every point, the standard term-by-term differentiation theorem applies on $[a,\infty)$ and gives
\[
g'(x)
=-
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
\]
The derivative series is locally uniformly convergent and consists of continuous functions, so $g'$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.
:::
:::
