::: problem
Let $C([0,1])$ have the uniform metric $d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|f(x)-g(x)|$.

1. Prove that $d_\infty$ is a metric and that $C([0,1])$ is complete.

2. Prove that its closed unit ball is closed and bounded, but not compact.

3. Prove that $C([0,1])$ is not totally bounded.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. $d_\infty$ is a metric and $C([0,1])$ is complete.
::: {.proof}
Nonnegativity and symmetry are immediate. If
\[
d_\infty(f,g)=0,
\]
then $|f(x)-g(x)|=0$ for every $x$, so $f=g$. For the triangle inequality,
\[
|f(x)-h(x)|
\le |f(x)-g(x)|+|g(x)-h(x)|
\]
for every $x$, hence taking suprema gives
\[
d_\infty(f,h)
\le d_\infty(f,g)+d_\infty(g,h).
\]
Thus $d_\infty$ is a metric.

Now let $(f_n)$ be Cauchy in $d_\infty$. For every $x\in[0,1]$, the scalar sequence $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy, so define
\[
f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x).
\]
Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $N$ such that
\[
\|f_n-f_m\|_\infty<\varepsilon
\qquad(n,m\ge N).
\]
Letting $m\to\infty$ pointwise gives
\[
|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon
\qquad(x\in[0,1],\ n\ge N),
\]
so
\[
\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon.
\]
Hence $f_n\to f$ uniformly. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so $f\in C([0,1])$. Therefore $C([0,1])$ is complete.
:::

<1>2. The closed unit ball is closed and bounded, but not compact.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
B=\{f\in C([0,1]):\|f\|_\infty\le1\}.
\]
It is bounded by definition. It is closed because the norm is continuous:
\[
|\|f\|_\infty-\|g\|_\infty|
\le\|f-g\|_\infty.
\]

To show noncompactness, for each $n\ge1$ choose a continuous triangular bump $u_n$ supported in
\[
I_n=(2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n})
\]
with
\[
0\le u_n\le1
\]
and attaining the value $1$ at the midpoint of $I_n$. Then $u_n\in B$. The supports are pairwise disjoint, so if $m\ne n$, evaluating at the peak of $u_n$ gives
\[
\|u_n-u_m\|_\infty=1.
\]
Thus $(u_n)$ has no Cauchy subsequence, hence no convergent subsequence. Therefore $B$ is not compact.
:::

<1>3. $C([0,1])$ is not totally bounded.
::: {.proof}
The same sequence $(u_n)$ is pairwise distance $1$. If $C([0,1])$ were totally bounded, then for $\varepsilon=1/3$ it could be covered by finitely many balls of radius $1/3$. Any such ball contains at most one $u_n$, since two points in one ball are at distance less than $2/3$. This cannot cover infinitely many $u_n$. Hence $C([0,1])$ is not totally bounded.
:::
