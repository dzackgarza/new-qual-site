::: {.problem}
Let $w$ be positive and continuous on $[0,1]$, let $n$ be a positive integer, and equip the space $P_n$ of real polynomials of degree at most $n$ with
\[
\langle p,q\rangle=\int_0^1 p(t)q(t)w(t)\,dt.
\]

1. Prove that $P_n$ has an orthonormal basis $p_0,p_1,\ldots,p_n$ with $\deg p_k=k$.
2. Prove that
\[
\langle p_k,p_k'\rangle=0
\]
for every $k$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The displayed bilinear form is an inner product on $P_n$.

::: {.proof}
Symmetry and bilinearity are immediate. If $p\neq0$, then $p^2w$ is
continuous and nonnegative on $[0,1]$. Since $w(t)>0$ everywhere and a
nonzero polynomial is nonzero at some point, $p^2w$ is strictly positive on
some interval. Hence
$$
\langle p,p\rangle
=
\int_0^1p(t)^2w(t)\,dt
>0.
$$
Thus the form is positive definite.
:::

<1>2. Applying Gram--Schmidt to
$$
1,t,t^2,\ldots,t^n
$$
produces orthonormal polynomials
$$
p_0,p_1,\ldots,p_n
$$
with
$$
\deg p_k=k
$$
for every $k$.

::: {.proof}
At the $k$th Gram--Schmidt step, before normalization one obtains
$$
q_k(t)
=
t^k
-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\langle t^k,p_j\rangle p_j(t).
$$
Inductively, each $p_j$ with $j<k$ has degree $j<k$. Therefore the
subtracted sum has degree at most $k-1$, so the coefficient of $t^k$ in
$q_k$ remains $1$. Hence
$$
\deg q_k=k,
$$
in particular $q_k\neq0$. Normalizing $q_k$ multiplies it by a nonzero
scalar and therefore preserves its degree. Gram--Schmidt gives
orthonormality, so the resulting family is an orthonormal basis of the
$(n+1)$-dimensional space $P_n$.
:::

<1>3. For every $k\geq1$,
$$
p_k'\in
\operatorname{span}\{p_0,\ldots,p_{k-1}\}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>2 gives $\deg p_k=k$, so
$$
\deg p_k'\leq k-1.
$$
Thus $p_k'\in P_{k-1}$. Again by step <1>2,
$$
p_0,\ldots,p_{k-1}
$$
are $k$ linearly independent vectors in the $k$-dimensional space
$P_{k-1}$, hence form a basis of that space.
:::

<1>4. For every $0\leq k\leq n$,
$$
\langle p_k,p_k'\rangle=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
For $k=0$, $p_0$ is constant, so $p_0'=0$. For $k\geq1$, step <1>3 writes
$p_k'$ as a linear combination of $p_0,\ldots,p_{k-1}$. Since the family
from step <1>2 is orthonormal, $p_k$ is orthogonal to each of those
polynomials. Hence it is orthogonal to their linear combination $p_k'$.
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>2 proves part 1, and step <1>4 proves part 2.
:::
