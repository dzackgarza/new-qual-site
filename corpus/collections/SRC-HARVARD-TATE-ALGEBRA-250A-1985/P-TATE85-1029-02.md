::: {.problem}
(2) Consider the polynomials
$$
\begin{aligned}
A(X)&=X^5-X^3-2X^2-2X-1,\\
B(X)&=X^5-X+3,\\
C(X)&=X^5+X^4-4X^3-3X^2+3X+1,\\
D(X)&=X^5-5,\\
E(X)&=X^5+10X^3-10X^2+35X-18.
\end{aligned}
$$
Each of these five is irreducible.
Their discriminants are
$$
d_A=47^2,\qquad d_B=252869\ \text{(prime)},\qquad d_C=11^4,\qquad d_D=5^9,\qquad d_E=2^6 5^8 11^{12}.
$$
The following table, produced in about 25 hours of running time by Tate's Macintosh, gives for each polynomial the number of primes in $\mathcal P_i$ (cf.
Problem 1) among the first 360 primes.
Thus, the sum of each row is 360.

|  | 0 roots | 1 root | 2 roots | 3 roots | 5 roots |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $A$ | 147 | 180 | 0 | 1 ($p=47$) | 32 |
| $B$ | 143 | 131 | 58 | 27 | 1 |
| $C$ | 288 | 1 ($p=11$) | 0 | 0 | 71 |
| $D$ | 78 | 272 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| $E$ | 142 | 88 | 128 | 0 | 2 |

The primes among the first 360 which are in $\mathcal P_5$, i.e. for which $f(X)$ splits in $\FF_p$, are in each case as follows:

- $A$: $p=83,191,197,269,439,487,523,619,761,823,907,947,977,1193,1277,1319,1447,1481,1499,1579,1693,1709,1741,1811,1861,1867,2053,2213,2221,2273,2339,2351$.

- $B$: just $p=1609$.

- $C$: all primes $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{11}$, i.e. $p=23,43,67,89,\ldots,2287,2309,2311,2333,2377,2399$.

- $D$: $p=31,191,251,271,601,641,761,1091,1861,2381$.

- $E$: just $p=2063$ and $2213$.

Armed with all this information (you do not really need much of it), and using the simple form of $D(X)$, determine the groups $G_B$, $G_E$, and $G_D$.
What are the only possibilities for $G_A$ and $G_C$?
Which of these possibilities do you guess is the correct one?
:::

::: {.solution}

For an irreducible quintic $f$, write $G_f$ for its Galois group in the natural transitive action on the five roots. Write $F_{20}$ for the affine group $x\mapsto ax+b$ on $\FF_5$, $D_{10}$ for its subgroup with $a\in\{\pm1\}$, and $C_5$ for its translation subgroup.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Among the five possible transitive groups,
$$
G_f\subseteq A_5
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
G_f\in\{A_5,D_{10},C_5\},
$$
and this occurs exactly when the discriminant of $f$ is a square in $\QQ$.


::: pf-proof

Let $r_1,\ldots,r_5$ be the roots of $f$ and put
$$
\Delta=\prod_{i<j}(r_i-r_j).
$$
Then $\Delta^2=\operatorname{disc}(f)$, and every $\sigma\in G_f$ satisfies
$$
\sigma(\Delta)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\Delta.
$$
Hence $\operatorname{disc}(f)$ is a square in $\QQ$ exactly when $\Delta$ is fixed by $G_f$, equivalently when every element of $G_f$ is even.

The groups $A_5$, $D_{10}$, and $C_5$ consist of even permutations in their degree-five actions: a nontrivial translation is a $5$-cycle, and an element $x\mapsto -x+b$ has cycle type $1\,2^2$. By contrast, $F_{20}$ contains $x\mapsto2x$, which has cycle type $1\,4$ and is odd. Thus the square-discriminant groups among the five possibilities are exactly $A_5,D_{10},C_5$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

$\boxed{G_B=S_5}$.


::: pf-proof

The discriminant $d_B=252869$ is not a square, so step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} leaves only $S_5$ and $F_{20}$. The table records primes for which $B$ has two roots modulo $p$ and primes for which it has three roots modulo $p$. These primes are unramified because the only ramified prime divides $252869$, which is larger than every prime in the displayed sample. For an unramified prime, the number of roots modulo $p$ is the number of fixed points of a Frobenius element. The group $F_{20}$ has only $0$, $1$, or $5$ fixed points in its degree-five action, so $G_B\ne F_{20}$. Therefore $G_B=S_5$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

$\boxed{G_E=A_5}$.


::: pf-proof

The discriminant $d_E=2^6 5^8 11^{12}$ is a square, so step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} leaves $A_5,D_{10},C_5$. The table records $128$ primes for which $E$ has exactly two roots modulo $p$, while only the three primes $2,5,11$ can ramify. Hence at least one of those $128$ primes is unramified, and its Frobenius element has exactly two fixed points. Neither $D_{10}$ nor $C_5$ has an element with exactly two fixed points, whereas $A_5$ does. Thus $G_E=A_5$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

$\boxed{G_D=F_{20}}$.


::: pf-proof

Let $\alpha=5^{1/5}$ and let $\zeta_5$ be a primitive fifth root of unity. The roots of $D(X)=X^5-5$ are $\alpha,\zeta_5\alpha,\ldots,\zeta_5^4\alpha$, so its splitting field is
$$
L=\QQ(\alpha,\zeta_5).
$$
The polynomial $X^5-5$ is Eisenstein at $5$, so $[\QQ(\alpha):\QQ]=5$, while $[\QQ(\zeta_5):\QQ]=4$. The degree of the intersection $\QQ(\alpha)\cap\QQ(\zeta_5)$ divides both $5$ and $4$, hence the intersection is $\QQ$. Therefore
$$
[L:\QQ]=5\cdot4=20.
$$
Thus $|G_D|=20$, and among the five possible transitive quintic groups the unique group of order $20$ is $F_{20}$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The exact possibilities left for the other two polynomials are
$$
\boxed{G_A\in\{A_5,D_{10}\}},
\qquad
\boxed{G_C\in\{A_5,D_{10},C_5\}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

Both $d_A=47^2$ and $d_C=11^4$ are squares, so step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} initially leaves $A_5,D_{10},C_5$ in both cases. For $A$, the table contains many primes with exactly one root modulo $p$, and the only ramified prime is $47$, which is listed in the three-root column. Hence an unramified Frobenius element of $G_A$ has exactly one fixed point. The group $C_5$ has no such element, so only $A_5$ and $D_{10}$ remain.

For $C$, the only prime in the one-root column is $11$, which is ramified, and the displayed unramified primes exhibit only zero or five roots. Those observations occur in all three groups $A_5,D_{10},C_5$, so the finite table does not eliminate any of them.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The data suggest
$$
\boxed{G_A=D_{10}},
\qquad
\boxed{G_C=C_5}.
$$


::: pf-proof

By the fixed-point counts from Problem 1, the theoretical density vectors
$$
(\delta(\mathcal P_0),\delta(\mathcal P_1),\delta(\mathcal P_2),\delta(\mathcal P_3),\delta(\mathcal P_5))
$$
for $D_{10}$ and $C_5$ are respectively
$$
\left(\frac25,\frac12,0,0,\frac1{10}\right),
\qquad
\left(\frac45,0,0,0,\frac15\right).
$$
After removing the single ramified prime $47$, the observed row for $A$ has proportions
$$
\left(\frac{147}{359},\frac{180}{359},0,0,\frac{32}{359}\right)
\approx(0.409,0.501,0,0,0.089),
$$
which is close to the $D_{10}$ vector and very far from the $A_5$ vector $(2/5,1/4,1/3,0,1/60)$. After removing the ramified prime $11$, the row for $C$ has proportions
$$
\left(\frac{288}{359},0,0,0,\frac{71}{359}\right)
\approx(0.802,0,0,0,0.198),
$$
which is close to the $C_5$ vector. These comparisons justify the requested guesses; they are not deductions from a finite sample.

:::
