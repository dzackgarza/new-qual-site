::: {.problem}
Suppose $f(X)$ is a monic cubic with coefficients in a finite field $k$, and suppose the discriminant of $f$ is not a square in $k$. Prove that $f(X)$ is the product of a linear polynomial and an irreducible quadratic polynomial in $k[X]$. Now explain why the congruences of [[P-TATE85-1119-02]] are not given mod $p = 2, 3, 5, 7, 11$ and $23$, and how this answers the "Why?" of that problem.
:::

::: {.solution}

Write $k=\FF_q$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

The hypothesis forces $q$ to be odd and $f$ to be separable.


::: pf-proof

If $q$ were even, the Frobenius map
$$
k\longrightarrow k,
\qquad
x\longmapsto x^2
$$
would be an automorphism of the finite field $k$, so every element of $k$ would be a square. Thus the discriminant could not be a nonsquare.

Hence $q$ is odd. Also, a polynomial has discriminant $0$ exactly when it has a repeated root, and $0$ is a square. Since the discriminant of $f$ is a nonsquare, it is nonzero, so $f$ is separable.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

Let $r_1,r_2,r_3$ be the roots of $f$ in a splitting field and put
$$
\Delta=(r_1-r_2)(r_1-r_3)(r_2-r_3).
$$
Then
$$
\Delta^2=\operatorname{disc}(f),
$$
and the $q$-power Frobenius satisfies
$$
\Delta^q=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\Delta,
$$
where $\pi$ is the permutation induced by Frobenius on the three roots.


::: pf-proof

The first identity is the definition of the discriminant for a monic separable cubic. Frobenius permutes the roots because their elementary symmetric functions lie in $k$. The Vandermonde product $\Delta$ is alternating in the three roots, so applying the root permutation $\pi$ multiplies it by $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$. Since Frobenius acts by $x\mapsto x^q$, this gives the second identity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The Frobenius permutation $\pi$ is odd.


::: pf-proof

Because $q$ is odd, an element $d\in k^\times$ is a square in $k$ if and only if a square root of $d$ in an algebraic closure already lies in $k$. Indeed, if $d=c^2$ with $c\in k$, then the two roots of $X^2-d$ are $\pm c$; conversely a square root lying in $k$ exhibits $d$ as a square.

Apply this to
$$
d=\operatorname{disc}(f)=\Delta^2.
$$
The hypothesis says that $d$ is not a square in $k$, so $\Delta\notin k$. Hence Frobenius does not fix $\Delta$. By step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref},
$$
\Delta^q=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\Delta,
$$
and the only possibilities for the sign are $\pm1$. Therefore $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=-1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

The factorization of $f$ over $k$ is a linear factor times an irreducible quadratic factor.


::: pf-proof

For a separable polynomial over a finite field, the degrees of the irreducible factors are the cycle lengths of Frobenius on its roots. An odd permutation of three letters is a transposition, whose cycle type is
$$
1+2.
$$
Thus Frobenius fixes one root and interchanges the other two. Equivalently, $f$ has one root in $k$ and its remaining quadratic factor is irreducible over $k$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

For each
$$
p\in\{3,5,7,11,23\},
$$
the common discriminant $-4027$ from Problem 2 is a nonsquare modulo $p$.


::: pf-proof

The residues are
$$
-4027\equiv
2\pmod3,
\quad
3\pmod5,
\quad
5\pmod7,
\quad
10\pmod{11},
\quad
21\pmod{23}.
$$
The nonzero squares in the first four fields are respectively
$$
\{1\},
\quad
\{1,4\},
\quad
\{1,2,4\},
\quad
\{1,3,4,5,9\},
$$
so the displayed residues are nonsquares. The nonzero squares modulo $23$ are
$$
\{1,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,13,16,18\},
$$
which does not contain $21$. Hence $-4027$ is a nonsquare for all five odd primes.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

None of the odd primes $3,5,7,11,23$ could give one of the split-or-inert congruences used in Problem 2.


::: pf-proof

Each polynomial $f_i$ in Problem 2 has discriminant $-4027$. Since none of the five primes in step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} divides $4027$, reduction modulo such a prime remains separable and has the same nonzero discriminant residue. By steps [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-4){.pf-ref}, every reduced cubic therefore factors as one linear factor times an irreducible quadratic factor.

Problem 2 distinguishes the four cubic fields by primes at which one cubic splits into three linear factors while comparison cubics are irreducible. A forced factorization of type $1+2$ can be neither of those two types, so these five primes cannot supply the desired congruences.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

Modulo $2$, all four cubics from Problem 2 also factor as a linear polynomial times the same irreducible quadratic polynomial.


::: pf-proof

Reducing the four displayed cubics from Problem 2 modulo $2$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_1(X)&\equiv X^3+X^2+X
=X(X^2+X+1),\\
f_2(X)&\equiv X^3+1
=(X+1)(X^2+X+1),\\
f_3(X)&\equiv X^3+X^2+X
=X(X^2+X+1),\\
f_4(X)&\equiv X^3+1
=(X+1)(X^2+X+1).
\end{aligned}
$$
The polynomial $X^2+X+1$ has no root in $\FF_2$, so it is irreducible. Thus $p=2$ also gives factorization type $1+2$ for every $f_i$, and likewise cannot distinguish the fields by split-versus-inert behavior.

:::
