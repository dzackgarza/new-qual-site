::: {.problem}
(a) Show that an order $B$ in a quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ is of the form $B = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha] = \mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{Z}\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is a root of an irreducible monic quadratic polynomial $f(X) = X^2 + rX + s \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$.

(b) For each such polynomial $f$, let $d_f = r^2 - 4s$ and $B_f = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha] = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha, \beta]$ where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the complex (or real) roots of $f$.
Let $g$ be another irreducible monic quadratic polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
Show
$$
B_g \subset B_f \iff d_f \trianglelefteq d_g,
$$
where $a \trianglelefteq b$ means by definition that $b = m^2 a$, for some $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, and when that is the case, show that the additive group $B_f/B_g$ is *cyclic* of order $m$, where $d_g = m^2 d_f$.

(c) Thus, $B_g = B_f \iff d_f = d_g$.
Show that the integers $d$ which occur as discriminants of quadaratic orders, i.e., the integers $d$ of the form $d_f$ for some $f$ as above, are those $d \equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 4$ such that $d$ is not a perfect square.

(d) Show that $B_f$ is integrally closed if and only if
$$
d \trianglelefteq d_f,\ d \equiv 0 \text{ or } 1 \bmod 4 \Rightarrow d = d_f,
$$
and then the other orders in $\mathbb{Q}(B_f)$ are the $B_g$'s such that $d_f \trianglelefteq d_g$.

(e) Suppose $f$ and $g$ are as in (d), say $d_g = m^2 d_f$.
Show for each prime number $p$ such that $p \mid d_g$ that there is a unique prime ideal $P$ of $B_g$ such that $p \in P$, and that $B_g = P + \mathbb{Z}$, i.e., $B_g/P \cong \mathbb{F}_p$.
Show $P^2 = pB_g$ if $p \nmid m$, $P^2 = pP$ if $p \mid m$.
:::

For a monic irreducible quadratic
\[
u(X)=X^2+r_uX+s_u\in\mathbb Z[X]
\]
with roots \(\alpha_u,\beta_u\), write
\[
B_u=\mathbb Z[\alpha_u]=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha_u,
\qquad
d_u=r_u^2-4s_u=(\alpha_u-\beta_u)^2.
\]

::: pf

::: pf-step

Every order \(B\) in a quadratic field \(K\) has the form required in part (a).


::: pf-proof

By definition, \(B\) is a subring of \(K\) containing \(1\), is a free abelian group of rank \(2\), and spans \(K\) over \(\mathbb Q\). Every element of \(B\) is integral over \(\mathbb Z\): multiplication by an element \(b\in B\) is an endomorphism of the finite free \(\mathbb Z\)-module \(B\), so Cayley--Hamilton gives a monic polynomial in \(\mathbb Z[X]\) satisfied by \(b\).

In particular,
\[
B\cap\mathbb Q=\mathbb Z,
\]
because the rational numbers integral over \(\mathbb Z\) are exactly the integers. Hence \(1\) is primitive in the free abelian group \(B\): if \(1=nb\) with \(n\in\mathbb Z\) and \(b\in B\), then \(b=1/n\in B\cap\mathbb Q\), so \(n=\pm1\). Therefore \(1\) extends to a \(\mathbb Z\)-basis
\[
B=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha.
\]
Since \(B\) spans the quadratic field \(K\), one has \(\alpha\notin\mathbb Q\). The element \(\alpha\) is integral over \(\mathbb Z\), so its minimal polynomial is an irreducible monic quadratic \(f\in\mathbb Z[X]\). Its quadratic relation shows that every power of \(\alpha\) lies in \(\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha\), and consequently
\[
B=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha=\mathbb Z[\alpha].
\]

:::

:::

::: pf-step

For two quadratic orders,
\[
B_g\subset B_f
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d_g=m^2d_f
\]
for some nonzero \(m\in\mathbb Z\); in that case
\[
B_f/B_g\cong\mathbb Z/|m|\mathbb Z.
\]


::: pf-proof

Let \(\alpha,\beta\) be the roots of
\[
f(X)=X^2+rX+s,
\]
and let \(\gamma,\delta\) be the roots of
\[
g(X)=X^2+r'X+s'.
\]
First suppose \(B_g\subset B_f\). Since \(\{1,\alpha\}\) is a \(\mathbb Z\)-basis of \(B_f\), write
\[
\gamma=a+m\alpha
\]
with \(a,m\in\mathbb Z\). Here \(m\ne0\), because \(B_g\) has rank \(2\). Applying the nontrivial conjugation of the common quadratic field gives
\[
\delta=a+m\beta.
\]
Therefore
\[
d_g=(\gamma-\delta)^2
=m^2(\alpha-\beta)^2
=m^2d_f.
\]

Conversely, suppose \(d_g=m^2d_f\). Replacing \(m\) by \(-m\) if necessary only interchanges the two roots, so take \(m>0\). The equality of discriminants implies
\[
\mathbb Q(\sqrt{d_g})=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{d_f}).
\]
Choose roots compatibly as
\[
\alpha=\frac{-r+\sqrt{d_f}}2,
\qquad
\gamma=\frac{-r'+m\sqrt{d_f}}2.
\]
Since \(d_f\equiv r^2\pmod4\) and \(d_g\equiv r'^2\pmod4\), the equality \(d_g=m^2d_f\) gives
\[
r'\equiv mr\pmod2.
\]
Thus
\[
a:=\frac{mr-r'}2\in\mathbb Z,
\qquad
\gamma=a+m\alpha.
\]
It follows that
\[
B_g=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\gamma
\subset
\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha=B_f.
\]

Finally, modulo \(B_g\), the class of \(\alpha\) generates \(B_f/B_g\), because \(B_f=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha\). The relation \(\gamma=a+m\alpha\in B_g\) gives \(m[\alpha]=0\). If \(k[\alpha]=0\), then
\[
k\alpha=u+v(a+m\alpha)
\]
for some \(u,v\in\mathbb Z\); comparison of the \(\alpha\)-coefficients gives \(k=vm\). Hence \([\alpha]\) has order exactly \(|m|\), proving the asserted cyclic quotient.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Part (c) holds: \(B_g=B_f\) if and only if \(d_g=d_f\), and the discriminants of quadratic orders are exactly the nonsquare integers congruent to \(0\) or \(1\) modulo \(4\).


::: pf-proof

By step <1>2, an inclusion \(B_g\subset B_f\) has index \(|m|\) precisely when \(d_g=m^2d_f\). Thus equality of the orders is equivalent to \(|m|=1\), which is equivalent to \(d_g=d_f\).

If
\[
f(X)=X^2+rX+s
\]
is irreducible and monic over \(\mathbb Z\), then
\[
d_f=r^2-4s\equiv r^2\equiv0\text{ or }1\pmod4.
\]
Moreover \(d_f\) is not a square: a quadratic polynomial over \(\mathbb Q\) is reducible exactly when its discriminant is a square in \(\mathbb Q\), and an integer which is a square in \(\mathbb Q\) is a square in \(\mathbb Z\).

Conversely, let \(d\) be a nonsquare integer with \(d\equiv0\) or \(1\pmod4\). If \(d\equiv0\pmod4\), then
\[
X^2-\frac d4
\]
is a monic polynomial in \(\mathbb Z[X]\) with discriminant \(d\). If \(d\equiv1\pmod4\), then
\[
X^2+X+\frac{1-d}{4}
\]
is a monic polynomial in \(\mathbb Z[X]\) with discriminant \(d\). In either case the polynomial is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\), because \(d\) is not a square. Step <1>1 therefore produces a quadratic order with discriminant \(d\).

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Part (d) holds: \(B_f\) is integrally closed precisely when
\[
d\trianglelefteq d_f,\qquad d\equiv0\text{ or }1\pmod4
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d=d_f.
\]
When this holds, every order in \(\mathbb Q(B_f)\) is a \(B_g\) with \(d_f\trianglelefteq d_g\).


::: pf-proof

Suppose first that \(B_f\) is integrally closed, and let \(d\trianglelefteq d_f\) with \(d\equiv0\) or \(1\pmod4\). Thus
\[
d_f=m^2d
\]
for some nonzero integer \(m\). Since \(d_f\) is not a square, neither is \(d\). By step <1>3, \(d\) is the discriminant of a quadratic order \(B_h\), and step <1>2 gives
\[
B_f\subset B_h.
\]
Every element of \(B_h\) is integral over \(\mathbb Z\), hence integral over \(B_f\). Since \(B_f\) is integrally closed in its fraction field, this forces \(B_h=B_f\). Step <1>3 then gives \(d=d_f\).

Conversely, assume the displayed condition and suppose, for contradiction, that \(B_f\) is not integrally closed in its fraction field \(K\). Then there is an element \(x\in K\setminus B_f\) integral over \(B_f\). The ring
\[
C=B_f[x]
\]
is finite as a \(B_f\)-module because \(x\) is integral. Hence \(C\) is finite over \(\mathbb Z\); being a subgroup of the field \(K\), it is torsion-free, and because it contains the rank-\(2\) lattice \(B_f\), it has rank \(2\). Thus \(C\) is a quadratic order strictly containing \(B_f\).

By step <1>1, write \(C=B_h\), with discriminant \(d_h\). Step <1>2 gives
\[
d_h\trianglelefteq d_f.
\]
By step <1>3, \(d_h\equiv0\) or \(1\pmod4\). The assumed condition therefore gives \(d_h=d_f\), and step <1>3 then gives \(B_h=B_f\), contradicting \(x\notin B_f\). Hence \(B_f\) is integrally closed.

Finally assume \(B_f\) is integrally closed and let \(C\) be any order in \(K\). Every element of \(C\) is integral over \(\mathbb Z\), hence over \(B_f\), so \(C\subset B_f\). By step <1>1 write \(C=B_g\); step <1>2 then gives \(d_f\trianglelefteq d_g\). Conversely, every \(B_g\) satisfying \(d_f\trianglelefteq d_g\) is contained in \(B_f\) by step <1>2, so these are exactly the other orders in \(K\).

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let \(B_f\) be integrally closed, let \(d_g=m^2d_f\), and let \(p\mid d_g\). There is a unique prime ideal \(P\subset B_g\) containing \(p\), one has
\[
B_g/P\cong\mathbb F_p
\quad\text{and}\quad
B_g=P+\mathbb Z,
\]
and
\[
P^2=
\begin{cases}
pB_g,&p\nmid m,\\
pP,&p\mid m.
\end{cases}
\]


::: pf-proof

By step <1>2, after replacing the generator of \(B_g\) by an integral translate, one may write
\[
B_f=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha,
\qquad
B_g=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\theta,
\qquad
\theta=m\alpha.
\]
If
\[
f(X)=X^2+rX+s,
\]
then \(\theta\) satisfies
\[
H(X)=X^2+mrX+m^2s,
\]
whose discriminant is
\[
(mr)^2-4m^2s=m^2d_f=d_g.
\]
Consequently
\[
B_g/pB_g\cong
\mathbb F_p[X]/(\overline H).
\]

Because \(p\mid d_g\), the quadratic \(\overline H\) has a unique repeated root \(\lambda\in\mathbb F_p\). For odd \(p\) this is the usual discriminant criterion. If \(p=2\), then \(2\mid d_g\) forces \(d_g\equiv0\pmod4\), hence \(mr\) is even; thus \(\overline H=X^2+c=(X+c)^2\) in \(\mathbb F_2[X]\). Therefore
\[
B_g/pB_g
\cong
\mathbb F_p[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
\]
This ring has exactly one prime ideal, namely \((\varepsilon)\). Its inverse image \(P\) is consequently the unique prime ideal of \(B_g\) containing \(p\), and
\[
B_g/P\cong\mathbb F_p.
\]
The image of \(\mathbb Z\) already surjects onto this quotient, so \(B_g=P+\mathbb Z\).

Suppose first that \(p\mid m\). Then \(\overline H=X^2\), so the repeated root is \(0\) and
\[
P=(p,\theta).
\]
Write \(m=pk\). The relation \(H(\theta)=0\) gives
\[
\theta^2=-mr\theta-m^2s
=-pkr\theta-p^2k^2s
\in pP.
\]
Since
\[
P^2=(p^2,p\theta,\theta^2),
\]
this shows \(P^2\subset pP\). The reverse inclusion follows from \(p^2,p\theta\in P^2\), so
\[
P^2=pP.
\]

Now suppose \(p\nmid m\). Put \(O=B_f\) and \(B=B_g\). Since \(m\) is a unit in \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}\) and \(B=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Zm\alpha\),
\[
B\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z_{(p)}
=
O\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z_{(p)}.
\]
The quotient \(B/pB\) has only the prime \(P/pB\), so the left-hand side is the local ring \(B_P\); the common ring is likewise the localization of \(O\) at the corresponding prime over \(p\). The order \(O\) is finite over the Noetherian ring \(\mathbb Z\), hence is Noetherian; it is integrally closed and one-dimensional, so it is a Dedekind domain. Its localization at a nonzero prime is therefore a discrete valuation ring. Thus \(B_P\) is a discrete valuation ring with maximal ideal \(P_P\).

The quotient
\[
B/pB\cong\mathbb F_p[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)
\]
is local of length \(2\), so after localizing at \(P\), the quotient \(B_P/pB_P\) still has length \(2\). If \(\pi\) is a uniformizer of the discrete valuation ring \(B_P\) and
\[
pB_P=(\pi^e),
\]
then \(B_P/pB_P\) has length \(e\). Hence \(e=2\), so
\[
P_P^2=pB_P.
\]
At every other maximal ideal \(Q\ne P\), the element \(p\) is a unit because \(P\) is the unique prime containing \(p\). Hence
\[
P_Q=B_Q=(pB)_Q.
\]
The ideals \(P^2\) and \(pB\) therefore have equal localizations at every maximal ideal of \(B\), so
\[
P^2=pB.
\]
This proves both cases.

:::

Step <1>1 proves part (a), step <1>2 proves part (b), step <1>3 proves part (c), step <1>4 proves part (d), and step <1>5 proves part (e).
