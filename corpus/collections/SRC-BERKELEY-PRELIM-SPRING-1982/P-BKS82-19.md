::: {.problem}
Show that the initial-value problem
$$
y'(x)=2+3\sin(y(x)),
\qquad
y(0)=4,
$$
has a solution defined for
$$
-\infty<x<\infty.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
f(u)\coloneqq2+3\sin u.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #maximal-solution-exists}
There is a unique maximal solution
$$
y:(\alpha,\beta)\longrightarrow\mathbb R
$$
of
$$
y'=f(y),
\qquad
y(0)=4,
$$
where
$$
\alpha<0<\beta.
$$

::: pf-proof
The function $f$ is continuously differentiable on $\mathbb R$, hence
locally Lipschitz. The Picard--Lindelöf theorem therefore gives a unique
local solution through $(0,4)$, and the standard maximal-extension theorem
gives a unique maximal interval $(\alpha,\beta)$ containing $0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #derivative-bound}
Along this solution,
$$
\abs{y'(x)}\le5
$$
for every $x\in(\alpha,\beta)$.

::: pf-proof
Since
$$
-1\le\sin(y(x))\le1,
$$
one has
$$
-1
\le
2+3\sin(y(x))
\le
5.
$$
Thus
$$
\abs{y'(x)}
=
\abs{2+3\sin(y(x))}
\le5.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #right-endpoint-infinite}
The right endpoint satisfies
$$
\beta=+\infty.
$$

::: pf-proof
Suppose instead that $\beta<\infty$. By step [](#derivative-bound){.pf-ref} and the mean value
theorem,
$$
\abs{y(t)-y(s)}
\le
5\abs{t-s}
$$
for all $s,t\in[0,\beta)$. Hence $y(t)$ is Cauchy as
$t\uparrow\beta$, so there is an $L\in\mathbb R$ such that
$$
\lim_{t\uparrow\beta}y(t)=L.
$$

Apply Picard--Lindelöf at the point $(\beta,L)$. It gives a solution of
the same differential equation on an interval
$$
(\beta-\varepsilon,\beta+\varepsilon)
$$
with value $L$ at $\beta$. By uniqueness, this local solution agrees with
$y$ on the overlap $(\beta-\varepsilon,\beta)$, so it extends $y$
beyond $\beta$. This contradicts maximality. Therefore
$\beta=+\infty$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #left-endpoint-infinite}
The left endpoint satisfies
$$
\alpha=-\infty.
$$

::: pf-proof
If $\alpha> -\infty$, the same estimate from step [](#derivative-bound){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\abs{y(t)-y(s)}
\le
5\abs{t-s}
$$
for $s,t\in(\alpha,0]$. Thus $y(t)$ has a finite limit as
$t\downarrow\alpha$. Local existence and uniqueness at that limiting
point extend the solution to times smaller than $\alpha$, again
contradicting maximality. Hence $\alpha=-\infty$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #solution-on-all-reals}
The solution is defined on all of $\mathbb R$.

::: pf-proof
By steps [](#right-endpoint-infinite){.pf-ref} and [](#left-endpoint-infinite){.pf-ref}, the maximal interval is
$$
(\alpha,\beta)=(-\infty,\infty).
$$
Thus the solution through $y(0)=4$ exists for every real $x$.
:::

:::

::: pf-qed
Step [](#solution-on-all-reals){.pf-ref} is exactly the required global-existence statement.
:::
