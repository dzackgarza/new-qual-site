::: {.problem}
Let $(a_n)$ be a real sequence.

(a) Prove that $(a_n)$ is unbounded above if and only if it has a subsequence tending to $+\infty$.

(b) Prove that $(a_n)$ is unbounded below if and only if it has a subsequence tending to $-\infty$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $(a_n)$ has a subsequence tending to $+\infty$, then $(a_n)$ is
unbounded above.

::: {.proof}
Let $(a_{n_k})$ satisfy
$$
a_{n_k}\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$
Given any $M\in\RR$, there exists $K$ such that
$$
a_{n_k}>M
$$
for every $k\geq K$. Thus some term of the original sequence exceeds $M$.
Since $M$ was arbitrary, $(a_n)$ is unbounded above.
:::

<1>2. If $(a_n)$ is unbounded above, then every tail
$$
(a_n)_{n\geq N}
$$
is unbounded above.

::: {.proof}
Suppose some tail were bounded above by $M$. The finitely many terms
$$
a_1,\ldots,a_{N-1}
$$
have a finite maximum $M_0$. Then
$$
\max\{M,M_0\}
$$
would be an upper bound for the entire sequence, contradicting unboundedness.
:::

<1>3. If $(a_n)$ is unbounded above, there is a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ such
that
$$
a_{n_k}>k
$$
for every $k$.

::: {.proof}
Choose $n_1$ with $a_{n_1}>1$. Having chosen $n_k$, apply step <1>2 to the
tail beginning at $n_k+1$. Since that tail is unbounded above, choose
$$
n_{k+1}>n_k
$$
such that
$$
a_{n_{k+1}}>k+1.
$$
This recursively defines the required subsequence.
:::

<1>4. The subsequence in step <1>3 tends to $+\infty$.

::: {.proof}
Let $M\in\RR$. Choose $K\in\NN$ with $K>M$. Then for every $k\geq K$,
$$
a_{n_k}>k\geq K>M.
$$
Hence $a_{n_k}\to+\infty$.
:::

<1>5. Part (a) follows.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 proves one implication, and steps <1>2--<1>4 prove the converse.
:::

<1>6. If $(a_n)$ has a subsequence tending to $-\infty$, then $(a_n)$ is
unbounded below.

::: {.proof}
Let $(a_{n_k})$ satisfy
$$
a_{n_k}\longrightarrow-\infty.
$$
Given any $M\in\RR$, there exists $K$ such that
$$
a_{n_k}<M
$$
for every $k\geq K$. Thus the original sequence has a term below every real
number $M$, so it is unbounded below.
:::

<1>7. If $(a_n)$ is unbounded below, then every tail is unbounded below.

::: {.proof}
If some tail beginning at $N$ were bounded below by $M$, then the finitely
many terms $a_1,\ldots,a_{N-1}$ would have a finite minimum $M_0$, and
$$
\min\{M,M_0\}
$$
would be a lower bound for the entire sequence. This contradicts unboundedness
below.
:::

<1>8. If $(a_n)$ is unbounded below, there is a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ such
that
$$
a_{n_k}<-k
$$
for every $k$.

::: {.proof}
Choose $n_1$ with $a_{n_1}<-1$. Having chosen $n_k$, apply step <1>7 to the
tail beginning at $n_k+1$ and choose
$$
n_{k+1}>n_k
$$
with
$$
a_{n_{k+1}}<-(k+1).
$$
:::

<1>9. The subsequence in step <1>8 tends to $-\infty$.

::: {.proof}
Let $M\in\RR$. Choose $K\in\NN$ with $K>-M$. Then for every $k\geq K$,
$$
a_{n_k}<-k\leq-K<M.
$$
Hence $a_{n_k}\to-\infty$.
:::

<1>10. Part (b) follows.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>6 proves one implication, and steps <1>7--<1>9 prove the converse.
:::

<1>11. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>5 and <1>10 establish parts (a) and (b), respectively.
:::
