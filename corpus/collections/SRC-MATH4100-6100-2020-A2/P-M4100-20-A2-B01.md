::: {.problem}
Let $(a_n)$ be a bounded sequence of positive real numbers.

(a) Prove
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{a_n}
\le
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{a_n}
\le
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}.
\]

(b) Construct a sequence for which all three inequalities are strict.

(c) Use part (a) to prove
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{n}=1.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
$$
r_n=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n},
\qquad
u_n=\sqrt[n]{a_n}.
$$

<1>1. One has
$$
\liminf r_n\leq\liminf u_n.
$$

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
L=\liminf r_n.
$$
Since $r_n>0$, one has $L\geq0$. Moreover, $L<+\infty$: if $L=+\infty$,
then eventually $r_n>2$, which would force $a_n$ to grow at least
geometrically and contradict boundedness of $(a_n)$. If $L=0$, then the claimed inequality is
immediate because $u_n>0$.

Assume $L>0$, and fix any number $c$ with
$$
0<c<L.
$$
By the defining eventual lower-bound property of $\liminf$, there exists
$N$ such that
$$
r_n>c
$$
for every $n\geq N$. Hence for $n>N$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_n
&=
a_N
\prod_{k=N}^{n-1}\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\\
&=
a_N\prod_{k=N}^{n-1}r_k\\
&>
a_N c^{n-N}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Taking $n$th roots gives
$$
u_n
>
a_N^{1/n}c^{(n-N)/n}.
$$
The right-hand side tends to $c$, so
$$
\liminf u_n\geq c.
$$
Since this holds for every $c<L$, it follows that
$$
\liminf u_n\geq L.
$$
:::

<1>2. One has
$$
\limsup u_n\leq\limsup r_n.
$$

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
R=\limsup r_n.
$$
If $R=+\infty$, the inequality is automatic. Suppose $R<+\infty$, and fix
$c>R$. By the defining eventual upper-bound property of $\limsup$, there
exists $N$ such that
$$
r_n<c
$$
for every $n\geq N$. Thus for $n>N$,
$$
a_n
=
a_N\prod_{k=N}^{n-1}r_k
<
a_N c^{n-N}.
$$
Taking $n$th roots yields
$$
u_n
<
a_N^{1/n}c^{(n-N)/n}.
$$
The right-hand side tends to $c$, so
$$
\limsup u_n\leq c.
$$
Since this holds for every $c>R$,
$$
\limsup u_n\leq R.
$$
:::

<1>3. The full chain is
$$
\boxed{
\liminf r_n
\leq
\liminf u_n
\leq
\limsup u_n
\leq
\limsup r_n.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
The first and last inequalities are steps <1>1 and <1>2. The middle inequality
$$
\liminf u_n\leq\limsup u_n
$$
holds for every bounded real sequence $(u_n)$.
:::

<1>4. Part (a) follows.

::: {.proof}
Substituting the definitions of $r_n$ and $u_n$ into step <1>3 gives exactly
the displayed chain in part (a).
:::

<1>5. Define
$$
a_n=
\begin{cases}
(1/4)^n,&n\text{ odd},\\
(1/2)^n,&n\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
$$
Then $(a_n)$ is a bounded sequence of positive real numbers.

::: {.proof}
Every term is positive, and
$$
0<a_n\leq1
$$
for every $n$.
:::

<1>6. For the sequence in step <1>5,
$$
\liminf\sqrt[n]{a_n}=\frac14,
\qquad
\limsup\sqrt[n]{a_n}=\frac12.
$$

::: {.proof}
If $n$ is odd, then
$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n}=\frac14,
$$
while if $n$ is even, then
$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n}=\frac12.
$$
Both parities occur infinitely often, so the stated liminf and limsup follow.
:::

<1>7. For odd $n$,
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=2^{n-1},
$$
while for even $n$,
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=2^{-n-2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
If $n$ is odd, then $n+1$ is even and
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=
\frac{(1/2)^{n+1}}{(1/4)^n}
=
2^{n-1}.
$$
If $n$ is even, then $n+1$ is odd and
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=
\frac{(1/4)^{n+1}}{(1/2)^n}
=
2^{-n-2}.
$$
:::

<1>8. For the sequence in step <1>5,
$$
\liminf\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=0,
\qquad
\limsup\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=+\infty.
$$

::: {.proof}
Along even $n$, step <1>7 gives
$$
2^{-n-2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Along odd $n$, it gives
$$
2^{n-1}\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$
Since all ratios are positive, the liminf is $0$, while the unbounded odd
subsequence forces the limsup to be $+\infty$.
:::

<1>9. All three inequalities in part (a) are strict for the sequence in
step <1>5:
$$
\boxed{
0
<
\frac14
<
\frac12
<
+\infty.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Combine steps <1>6 and <1>8.
:::

<1>10. Part (b) follows.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>9 gives an explicit sequence for which every inequality in the chain
is strict.
:::

<1>11. Apply part (a) to
$$
a_n=\frac1n.
$$
Then
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=
\frac{n}{n+1}
\longrightarrow1.
$$

::: {.proof}
The sequence $(1/n)$ is positive and bounded, so part (a) applies. The displayed
ratio computation is immediate.
:::

<1>12. One has
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1/n}=1.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>11,
$$
\liminf\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=
\limsup\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=1.
$$
Part (a) therefore gives
$$
1
\leq
\liminf n^{-1/n}
\leq
\limsup n^{-1/n}
\leq
1.
$$
Hence the liminf and limsup are both $1$, so
$$
n^{-1/n}\longrightarrow1.
$$
:::

<1>13. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{n}=1.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Since
$$
\sqrt[n]{n}
=
\frac{1}{n^{-1/n}},
$$
step <1>12 and the quotient limit law give the limit $1$.
:::

<1>14. Part (c) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>13.
:::

<1>15. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>4, <1>10, and <1>14 establish parts (a), (b), and (c), respectively.
:::
