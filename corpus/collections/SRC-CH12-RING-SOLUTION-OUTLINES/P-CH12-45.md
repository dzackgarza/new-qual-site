::: {.problem}
Let R be a ring with unity 1. Show that $S = \{ n \cdot 1 \st n \in \ZZ \}$ is a subring of R.
:::

::: {.solution}
Write $n\cdot1$ for the $n$-fold sum of $1$ when $n>0$, for $0$ when $n=0$, and for $-\bigl(\abs n\cdot1\bigr)$ when $n<0$. Then $S$ contains $0=0\cdot1$, is closed under subtraction because $x\cdot1-y\cdot1=(x-y)\cdot1$, and is closed under multiplication because $(x\cdot1)(y\cdot1)=(xy)\cdot1$; that is exactly Gallian's criterion for a subring. The collection retains its solution outline as `assets/attachments/Ch12Sltns.pdf`, whose entry "# 45" on PDF p. 3 is this exercise: it checks non-emptiness, closure under subtraction and closure under multiplication, as the card does. The card supplies the induction behind $(m\cdot1)r=m\cdot r$ and the negative case; the wording is its own.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #definition}
For $n\in\ZZ$ the element $n\cdot1$ is defined by $0\cdot1=0$, $(n+1)\cdot1=n\cdot1+1$ for $n\ge0$, and $(-n)\cdot1=-(n\cdot1)$ for $n>0$; these are the elements of $S$, so $S=\{n\cdot1:n\in\ZZ\}$.

::: pf-proof
This is the statement's definition of $S$ together with the standard meaning of an integer multiple in an additive group: $n\cdot r$ is $r$ added to itself $n$ times for $n>0$, is $0$ for $n=0$, and is $-\bigl(\abs n\cdot r\bigr)$ for $n<0$. Taking $r=1$ gives the elements of $S$, and $0=0\cdot1\in S$, $1=1\cdot1\in S$.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #subtraction}
For $x,y\in\ZZ$, $x\cdot1-y\cdot1=(x-y)\cdot1$; hence $S$ is closed under subtraction.

::: pf-proof
Integer multiples satisfy $(m+n)\cdot1=m\cdot1+n\cdot1$ for all $m,n\in\ZZ$: for $m,n\ge0$ this is the associativity of addition read inductively, $m\cdot1+(-m)\cdot1=0$ follows from the definition of additive inverses in the group $(R,+)$, and the mixed cases follow by cancelling. Taking $m=x$ and $n=-y$ gives $x\cdot1-y\cdot1=(x-y)\cdot1$. In particular, for $a=x\cdot1$ and $b=y\cdot1$ in $S$, $a-b=(x-y)\cdot1\in S$; and $S\ne\emptyset$ since $0\in S$.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #multiplication}
For $x,y\in\ZZ$, $(x\cdot1)(y\cdot1)=(xy)\cdot1$; hence $S$ is closed under multiplication.

::: pf-proof
First, $(m\cdot1)r=m\cdot r$ for every $r\in R$ and $m\ge0$: this is proved by induction on $m$, the case $m=0$ being $0\cdot r=0$ and the step being $((m+1)\cdot1)r=(m\cdot1+1)r=(m\cdot1)r+r=m\cdot r+r=(m+1)\cdot r$ by distributivity. Taking $r=y\cdot1$ gives $(x\cdot1)(y\cdot1)=x\cdot(y\cdot1)=(xy)\cdot1$ for $x\ge0$; and for $x<0$ one has $x\cdot1=-\bigl(\abs x\cdot1\bigr)$, so $(x\cdot1)(y\cdot1)=-\bigl((\abs x\cdot1)(y\cdot1)\bigr)=-\bigl(\abs x\,y\bigr)\cdot1=(xy)\cdot1$.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #conclusion}
$S$ is a subring of $R$, and it contains $1$.

::: pf-proof
$S$ is a nonempty subset of $R$ closed under subtraction and multiplication by steps [](#subtraction){.pf-ref} and [](#multiplication){.pf-ref}, which is Gallian's definition of a subring; and $1=1\cdot1\in S$. Equivalently, $S$ is the image of the ring homomorphism $\ZZ\to R$, $n\mapsto n\cdot1$, which is another way to see that it is a subring.
:::
