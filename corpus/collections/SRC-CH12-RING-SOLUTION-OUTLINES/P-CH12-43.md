::: {.problem}
Let $R = \ZZ \oplus \ZZ \oplus \ZZ$ and $S = \{ ( a , b , c ) \in R \st a + b = c \}$ . Prove or disprove that S is a subring of R.
:::

::: {.solution}

No solution set for this chapter is retained in `tmp/sources`, so the counterexample is the one this collection's outline records and the verification below is the card's own. The statement was checked against Gallian, *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* (7th edition), Chapter 12 Exercises,

Let $R=\ZZ\oplus\ZZ\oplus\ZZ$ with coordinatewise addition and multiplication, so $(a,b,c)+(a',b',c')=(a+a',b+b',c+c')$ and $(a,b,c)(a',b',c')=(aa',bb',cc')$, and let $S=\{(a,b,c)\in R\mid a+b=c\}$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #subgroup}
$S$ is an additive subgroup of $R$.

::: pf-proof
$(0,0,0)\in S$ since $0+0=0$. If $x=(a,b,c)$ and $y=(d,f,g)$ lie in $S$, then $a+b=c$ and $d+f=g$, so $(a-d)+(b-f)=(a+b)-(d+f)=c-g$, which says $x-y=(a-d,b-f,c-g)\in S$. A nonempty subset closed under subtraction is a subgroup.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #product}
For $x=(a,b,c)$ and $y=(d,f,g)$ in $S$, $xy=(ad,bf,cg)$ lies in $S$ if and only if $af+bd=0$.

::: pf-proof
$xy\in S$ means $ad+bf=cg$. Since $c=a+b$ and $g=d+f$,
$$
cg=(a+b)(d+f)=ad+af+bd+bf,
$$
so $ad+bf=cg$ is equivalent to $ad+bf=ad+af+bd+bf$, that is to $af+bd=0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #failure}
There are $x,y\in S$ with $xy\notin S$.

::: pf-proof
Take $x=(1,2,3)$ and $y=(4,5,9)$; both lie in $S$ because $1+2=3$ and $4+5=9$. Their product is $xy=(4,10,27)$, and $4+10=14\neq27$, so $xy\notin S$. Equivalently, in the notation of step [](#product){.pf-ref}, $af+bd=1\cdot5+2\cdot4=13\neq0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$S$ is not a subring of $R$: the claim is disproved.

::: pf-proof
A subring is a subset which is a ring under the operations of $R$, in particular closed under multiplication. Step [](#subgroup){.pf-ref} shows $S$ is an additive subgroup, but step [](#failure){.pf-ref} exhibits a product of two elements of $S$ outside $S$, so $S$ is not closed under multiplication and therefore not a subring.
:::
