::: {.problem}
Give an example of ring elements a and b with the properties that $ab = 0$ but $ba \neq 0$ .
:::

::: {.solution}
The argument is the retained solution outline for this chapter, **item 31, PDF p. 2** (`assets/attachments/Ch12Sltns.pdf`), restructured into steps.

Throughout, $R=M_2(\ZZ)$ is the ring of $2\times2$ matrices with integer entries under entrywise addition and matrix multiplication, and $E_{ij}$ is the matrix with $1$ in position $(i,j)$ and $0$ elsewhere.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #setting}
$R$ is a ring and $a=E_{12}$, $b=E_{11}$ are elements of it with $a\neq b$.

::: pf-proof
$M_2(\ZZ)$ is a ring: entrywise addition is an abelian group law with identity the zero matrix, matrix multiplication is associative and distributes over addition, and both operations take integer matrices to integer matrices. The matrices $a=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $b=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ have integer entries and differ in position $(1,1)$, so they are distinct elements of $R$, and the zero of $R$ is the zero matrix.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #ab}
$ab=0$.

::: pf-proof
For matrices, $(ab)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}a_{ik}b_{kj}$. Here $a_{ik}$ is $1$ only for $(i,k)=(1,2)$ and $b_{kj}$ is $1$ only for $(k,j)=(1,1)$, so every product $a_{ik}b_{kj}$ has $k=2$ in the first factor and $k=1$ in the second, which cannot both hold; each of the four sums is therefore $0$. Hence $ab$ is the zero matrix.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #ba}
$ba=a\neq0$.

::: pf-proof
Now $(ba)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}b_{ik}a_{kj}$: $b_{ik}$ is $1$ only for $(i,k)=(1,1)$ and $a_{kj}$ is $1$ only for $(k,j)=(1,2)$, so the only nonzero summand is the one with $k=1$, $i=1$, $j=2$. Hence $(ba)_{12}=1$ and all other entries are $0$, that is $ba=a$, which is not the zero matrix by step [](#setting){.pf-ref}.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #example}
$a$ and $b$ are ring elements with $ab=0$ and $ba\neq0$.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#setting){.pf-ref}, [](#ab){.pf-ref} and [](#ba){.pf-ref} give $a,b\in M_2(\ZZ)$ with $ab=0$ and $ba=a\neq0$. So in the noncommutative ring $M_2(\ZZ)$ a product can vanish in one order and not in the other, which is what the exercise asks for.
:::
