::: {.problem}
Describe $\mathbb P^2$ and the standard embedding $\mathbb A^2\hookrightarrow\mathbb P^2$.
Let
\[
Z:=V(y^2-x^3)\subset\mathbb A^2.
\]
Answer the following questions about $Z$.

1. What is $A(Z)$?
   Is $Z$ irreducible?

2. What is the projective closure $\overline Z$ of $Z$, and what is $\overline Z-Z$?
   Depict the result.

3. Does the morphism
   \[
   f:Z-\{0\}\longrightarrow\mathbb P^1,\qquad f(\alpha,\beta)=[\alpha,\beta]
   \]
   extend to a morphism $Z\to\mathbb P^1$?

4. Is $f$ bijective?
   Is it an isomorphism?
   Is it a birational equivalence?

5. What is the function field of $Z$?

6. Compute the blow-up of $Z$ at $\{0\}$.

7. Where is $Z$ nonsingular?

8. What is the integral closure of $A(Z)$?
   What is the normalization of $Z$?
:::


::: {.solution}
Use homogeneous coordinates \([X:Y:Z]\) on \(\mathbb P^2\), with the standard embedding
\[
\mathbb A^2\hookrightarrow\mathbb P^2,
\qquad
(x,y)\longmapsto[x:y:1].
\]
Recall that \(\mathbb P^2=(k^3\setminus\{0\})/k^\times\).

<1>1. The coordinate ring is \(k[t^2,t^3]\), and \(Z\) is irreducible.
::: {.proof}
By definition,
\[
A(Z)=k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3).
\]
The homomorphism
\[
k[x,y]\longrightarrow k[t],
\qquad
x\longmapsto t^2,
\quad
y\longmapsto t^3
\]
has kernel \((y^2-x^3)\). Hence
\[
A(Z)\cong k[t^2,t^3]\subseteq k[t].
\]
Since \(k[t]\) is a domain, so is \(A(Z)\). Therefore \(Z\) is irreducible.
:::

<1>2. The projective closure is the cuspidal cubic \(Y^2Z=X^3\), with one point at infinity.
::: {.proof}
Homogenizing \(y^2-x^3\) gives
\[
\overline Z=V(Y^2Z-X^3)\subseteq\mathbb P^2.
\]
On the line at infinity \(Z=0\), the equation becomes \(X^3=0\), so \(X=0\). Thus
\[
\overline Z-Z=\{[0:1:0]\}.
\]
Geometrically, \(\overline Z\) is the projective cuspidal cubic: its affine part has a cusp at \((0,0)\), and one smooth point is added at infinity.
:::

<1>3. The map \(f:Z\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb P^1\) does not extend to a morphism on \(Z\).
::: {.proof}
Every nonzero point of \(Z\) has \(x\ne0\), because \(x=0\) forces \(y=0\). Hence on \(Z\setminus\{0\}\),
\[
f(x,y)=[x:y]=[1:y/x].
\]
Under the parametrization
\[
(x,y)=(t^2,t^3),
\]
this is
\[
f(t)=[1:t]
\qquad(t\ne0).
\]
If \(f\) extended to the cusp, continuity forces the value at \(0\) to be \([1:0]\). On the affine chart of \(\mathbb P^1\) containing \([1:0]\), with coordinate \(v=Y/X\), the pullback would have to be the regular function
\[
v\circ f=\frac yx=t
\]
near the cusp.

But
\[
\mathcal O_{Z,0}=k[t^2,t^3]_{(t^2,t^3)},
\]
and \(t\notin\mathcal O_{Z,0}\). Indeed, if \(t=a/b\) with \(a,b\in k[t^2,t^3]\) and \(b(0)\ne0\), then the \(t\)-adic order of \(a=tb\) would be \(1\), whereas every nonzero element of \(k[t^2,t^3]\) has order \(0\) or at least \(2\). This is impossible. Therefore no such extension exists.
:::

<1>4. The displayed \(f\) is neither bijective nor an isomorphism onto \(\mathbb P^1\), but it is birational.
::: {.proof}
Via \(t\mapsto(t^2,t^3)\), the punctured curve is \(\mathbb G_m\), and
\[
f(t)=[1:t].
\]
Its image is
\[
\{[1:t]:t\in k^\times\}
=\mathbb P^1\setminus\{[1:0],[0:1]\}.
\]
Thus \(f\) is not surjective, hence not bijective and not an isomorphism onto \(\mathbb P^1\).

On the other hand, it is dominant and induces the function-field map generated by
\[
t=\frac yx.
\]
As shown below, \(k(Z)=k(t)=k(\mathbb P^1)\). Therefore \(f\) is a birational equivalence.
:::

<1>5. The function field is \(k(t)\).
::: {.proof}
From \(A(Z)=k[t^2,t^3]\),
\[
k(Z)=\operatorname{Frac}k[t^2,t^3].
\]
Since
\[
t=\frac{t^3}{t^2}
\]
belongs to this fraction field, it contains \(k(t)\); the reverse inclusion is immediate from \(k[t^2,t^3]\subseteq k[t]\). Hence
\[
\boxed{k(Z)=k(t)}.
\]
:::

<1>6. Blowing up the cusp produces \(\mathbb A^1\), with map \(t\mapsto(t^2,t^3)\).
::: {.proof}
View \(Z\) inside \(\mathbb A^2\) and blow up the origin. On the \(x\)-chart of the blow-up, write
\[
y=ux.
\]
Substituting into \(y^2=x^3\) gives
\[
x^2(u^2-x)=0.
\]
After removing the exceptional factor \(x^2\), the strict transform is
\[
x=u^2.
\]
Then
\[
y=ux=u^3.
\]
Thus this chart of the strict transform is simply \(\mathbb A^1_u\).

On the \(y\)-chart, writing \(x=vy\) gives
\[
y^2=v^3y^3,
\]
so the strict transform satisfies
\[
1=v^3y.
\]
This is the open subset \(u\ne0\) of the \(u\)-chart, with \(v=u^{-1}\). Hence the entire blow-up of \(Z\) at the cusp is already covered by the \(u\)-chart and is isomorphic to \(\mathbb A^1\). The blow-up morphism is
\[
u\longmapsto(u^2,u^3).
\]
The exceptional fiber over the cusp is the single point \(u=0\).
:::

<1>7. The only singular point of \(Z\) is the cusp \((0,0)\).
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
F(x,y)=y^2-x^3.
\]
The Jacobian criterion gives
\[
F_x=-3x^2,
\qquad
F_y=2y.
\]
On \(Z\), both derivatives vanish simultaneously only at \((0,0)\) (the same conclusion holds in characteristics \(2\) or \(3\) by using the nonzero remaining derivative away from the origin). Therefore
\[
Z_{\mathrm{sm}}=Z\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
\]
:::

<1>8. The integral closure is \(k[t]\), and the normalization is \(\mathbb A^1\to Z\), \(t\mapsto(t^2,t^3)\).
::: {.proof}
Inside the common function field \(k(t)\), the element \(t\) is integral over \(A(Z)=k[t^2,t^3]\), because it satisfies the monic equation
\[
T^2-t^2=0
\]
whose coefficient \(t^2=x\) lies in \(A(Z)\). Thus \(k[t]\) is integral over \(A(Z)\).

The ring \(k[t]\) is a PID, hence integrally closed, and it has the same fraction field \(k(t)\). If \(\alpha\in k(t)\) is integral over \(A(Z)\), then the same monic equation also shows that \(\alpha\) is integral over \(k[t]\), since \(A(Z)\subseteq k[t]\). Integrally closedness of \(k[t]\) gives \(\alpha\in k[t]\). Therefore \(k[t]\) is exactly the integral closure of \(A(Z)\) in \(k(Z)\).

Consequently the normalization is
\[
\nu:\mathbb A^1=\operatorname{Spec}k[t]\longrightarrow Z,
\qquad
 t\longmapsto(t^2,t^3).
\]
:::
