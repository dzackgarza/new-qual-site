::: {.problem}
Show that any group G of order 80 is solvable.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
\abs G=80=2^4\cdot5.
$$

<1>1. The number $n_5$ of Sylow $5$-subgroups satisfies
$$
n_5=1
\quad\text{or}\quad
n_5=16.
$$

::: {.proof}
By Sylow's theorem,
$$
n_5\mid16
$$
and
$$
n_5\equiv1\pmod5.
$$
The positive divisors of $16$ are
$$
1,2,4,8,16,
$$
and among these only
$$
1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
16
$$
are congruent to $1$ modulo $5$.
:::

<1>2. If
$$
n_5=1,
$$
then $G$ is solvable.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
P
$$
be the unique Sylow $5$-subgroup.
Then
$$
P\trianglelefteq G
$$
and
$$
\abs P=5.
$$
Thus $P$ is cyclic and hence solvable.

The quotient has order
$$
\abs{G/P}
=
\frac{80}{5}
=
16.
$$
Every finite $2$-group is solvable.
Therefore both
$$
P
$$
and
$$
G/P
$$
are solvable.
A group with solvable normal subgroup and solvable quotient is solvable.
Hence $G$ is solvable.
:::

<1>3. Assume now that
$$
n_5=16.
$$
Distinct Sylow $5$-subgroups intersect trivially.

::: {.proof}
Each Sylow $5$-subgroup has prime order $5$.
If two such subgroups had a nonidentity element in common, that common element would generate both subgroups.
Hence the two subgroups would be equal.
Therefore distinct Sylow $5$-subgroups intersect only in the identity.
:::

<1>4. The $16$ Sylow $5$-subgroups contain exactly
$$
64
$$
distinct nonidentity elements.

::: {.proof}
Each subgroup of order $5$ has
$$
4
$$
nonidentity elements.
By step <1>3, the nonidentity elements of distinct Sylow $5$-subgroups are disjoint.
Thus the total number is
$$
16\cdot4
=
64.
$$
:::

<1>5. Exactly $16$ elements of $G$ are not nonidentity elements of Sylow $5$-subgroups.

::: {.proof}
The group has
$$
80
$$
elements.
Step <1>4 accounts for
$$
64
$$
distinct nonidentity elements in Sylow $5$-subgroups.
Thus
$$
80-64
=
16
$$
elements remain.
These $16$ include the identity.
:::

<1>6. Let
$$
Q
$$
be a Sylow $2$-subgroup of $G$.
Then every nonidentity element of $Q$ is among the $16$ elements counted in step <1>5.

::: {.proof}
The subgroup $Q$ has order
$$
16.
$$
Every nonidentity element of a Sylow $5$-subgroup has order $5$, whereas every element of the $2$-group $Q$ has order a power of $2$.
Thus no nonidentity element of $Q$ can be a nonidentity element of a Sylow $5$-subgroup.
Hence all elements of $Q$ lie among the $16$ elements from step <1>5.
:::

<1>7. The Sylow $2$-subgroup $Q$ consists of all $16$ elements from step <1>5.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>6,
$$
Q
$$
is contained in that set of $16$ elements.
But
$$
\abs Q=16.
$$
Therefore the containment is equality.
:::

<1>8. The Sylow $2$-subgroup of $G$ is unique.

::: {.proof}
Every Sylow $2$-subgroup has order $16$.
By the same argument as in step <1>7, every Sylow $2$-subgroup must equal the same set of $16$ elements left over after removing the nonidentity elements of the Sylow $5$-subgroups.
Thus
$$
\boxed{n_2=1}.
$$
:::

<1>9. In the case
$$
n_5=16,
$$
the group $G$ is solvable.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
Q
$$
be the unique Sylow $2$-subgroup.
By step <1>8,
$$
Q\trianglelefteq G.
$$
The subgroup $Q$ has order $16$, so it is a finite $2$-group and therefore solvable.

The quotient has order
$$
\abs{G/Q}
=
\frac{80}{16}
=
5,
$$
so it is cyclic and therefore solvable.
Thus $G$ is an extension of a solvable group by a solvable group and is itself solvable.
:::

<1>10. Therefore every group of order $80$ is solvable.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1, the only possibilities are
$$
n_5=1
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
n_5=16.
$$
Step <1>2 handles the first case, and step <1>9 handles the second.
Hence
$$
\boxed{
\abs G=80
\implies
G\text{ is solvable}.
}
$$
:::

<1>11. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>10 is the required conclusion.
:::
