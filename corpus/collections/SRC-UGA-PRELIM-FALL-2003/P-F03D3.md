::: problem
Show that the function $$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 \sin \frac{1}{x}, & \text{for } x \neq 0 \\ 0, & \text{for } x = 0 \end{cases}$$ is differentiable everywhere in $(-\infty, \infty)$, but that $f'$ is not continuous at $0$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. For $x \neq 0$, $f$ is differentiable (it is a product of differentiable functions), with $f'(x) = 2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$.
::: {.proof}
product and chain rule.
:::

<1>2. At $x = 0$, $f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^2 \sin(1/h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h \sin(1/h) = 0$.
::: {.proof}
$|h \sin(1/h)| \le |h| \to 0$.
:::

<1>3. Hence $f$ is differentiable everywhere.
::: {.proof}
<1>1 and <1>2.
:::

<1>4. $f'$ is not continuous at $0$: $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x) = \lim_{x \to 0} (2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x))$ does not exist (the term $\cos(1/x)$ oscillates and has no limit).
::: {.proof}
<1>1 (the term $-\cos(1/x)$ has no limit as $x \to 0$).
:::

<1>5. Hence $f'$ is discontinuous at $0$.
::: {.proof}
<1>4.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
<1>3 and <1>5.
:::
:::
