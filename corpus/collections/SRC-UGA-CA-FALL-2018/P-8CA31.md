::: problem
Prove or exhibit a counterexample: If $f(z)$ is an entire function that maps every unbounded sequence to an unbounded sequence, then it is a polynomial.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The statement is true: such an $f$ is a polynomial.
Proof: prove the claim.

<1>2. The hypothesis implies $|f(z)| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$.
<2>1. Suppose not; then there is $M < \infty$ and an unbounded sequence $z_n$ with $|f(z_n)| \le M$.
Proof: negating "$|f(z)| \to \infty$" gives a bounded subsequence of values along an unbounded sequence.
<2>2. This contradicts the hypothesis that $f$ maps every unbounded sequence to an unbounded sequence.
Proof: $\{z_n\}$ is unbounded but $\{f(z_n)\}$ is bounded.
<2>3. Hence $|f(z)| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$.
Proof: <2>1 and <2>2.

<1>3. An entire function with $|f(z)| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$ is a polynomial.
<2>1. Consider $g(w) = f(1/w)$ on $\CC \setminus \{0\}$.
Proof: change of variable.
<2>2. $g$ has a pole (not an essential singularity) at $w = 0$.
Proof: $|f(z)| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$ means $|g(w)| \to \infty$ as $w \to 0$, so $g$ has a pole at $0$ (by Casorati–Weierstrass, an essential singularity would make $g$ unbounded in every neighborhood but not tending to $\infty$).
<2>3. Hence $g$ has a Laurent expansion $g(w) = \sum_{n=-N}^{\infty} a_n w^n$ with finitely many negative terms.
Proof: a pole has finite order.
<2>4. Therefore $f(z) = g(1/z) = \sum_{n=-N}^{\infty} a_n z^{-n}$ is a polynomial (a finite sum of nonnegative powers of $z$).
Proof: substitute $w = 1/z$.

<1>4. Q.E.D.
Proof: <1>3.
:::
