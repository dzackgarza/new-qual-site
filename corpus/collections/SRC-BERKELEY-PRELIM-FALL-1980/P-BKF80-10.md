::: {.problem}
Show that there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that, whenever $A$ is a real $2\times2$ matrix whose entries satisfy
$$
\abs{a_{ij}}\le\varepsilon,
$$
there exists a real $2\times2$ matrix $X$ with
$$
X^2+X^{\mathsf T}=A.
$$
Is $X$ unique?
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $M=M_2(\RR)$, equip $M$ with the Frobenius norm $\norm{\cdot}_F$, and define
$$
\Phi:M\longrightarrow M,
\qquad
\Phi(X)\coloneqq X^2+X^{\mathsf T}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #d-phi-zero-isomorphism}
The derivative $D\Phi_0:M\to M$ is the transpose map $H\mapsto H^{\mathsf T}$, hence is a linear isomorphism.

::: pf-proof
For $H\in M$,
$$
\Phi(H)-\Phi(0)-H^{\mathsf T}=H^2.
$$
Since $\norm{H^2}_F\leq\norm{H}_F^2$, the remainder is $o(\norm{H}_F)$ as $H\to0$. Thus
$$
D\Phi_0(H)=H^{\mathsf T}.
$$
Transposition is its own inverse, so $D\Phi_0$ is invertible.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #phi-diffeomorphism}
There are open neighborhoods $U,V\subset M$ of $0$ such that $\Phi:U\to V$ is a diffeomorphism.

::: pf-proof
The map $\Phi$ is polynomial and therefore continuously differentiable. Step [](#d-phi-zero-isomorphism){.pf-ref} shows that its derivative at $0$ is invertible, so the inverse function theorem gives such neighborhoods $U$ and $V$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #local-existence}
There exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that every real matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ with $\abs{a_{ij}}\leq\varepsilon$ lies in $V$, and hence has a solution $X$ of $X^2+X^{\mathsf T}=A$.

::: pf-proof
Because $V$ is an open neighborhood of $0$, there is $r>0$ such that the Frobenius ball
$$
B_r(0)=\{A\in M:\norm{A}_F<r\}
$$
is contained in $V$. Set $\varepsilon=r/4$. If $\abs{a_{ij}}\leq\varepsilon$ for all four entries, then
$$
\norm{A}_F
=\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^2 a_{ij}^2\right)^{1/2}
\leq 2\varepsilon
=\frac r2<r.
$$
Thus $A\in V$. By step [](#phi-diffeomorphism){.pf-ref} there is a unique $X\in U$ with $\Phi(X)=A$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #not-unique}
The solution $X$ is not unique in general.

::: pf-proof
Take $A=0$. Besides $X=0$, the matrix
$$
Y=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
satisfies
$$
Y^2+Y^{\mathsf T}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
+\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
=0.
$$
Hence the equation can have more than one real solution, although step [](#local-existence){.pf-ref} gives a unique solution inside the neighborhood $U$.
:::

:::

::: pf-qed
Step [](#local-existence){.pf-ref} proves the asserted local existence, and step [](#not-unique){.pf-ref} answers the uniqueness question.
:::
