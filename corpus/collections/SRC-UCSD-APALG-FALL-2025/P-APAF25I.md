::: {.problem}
Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field and let $I\subseteq k[x,y]$ be an ideal.
Suppose that the variety of $I$ is $V(I)=\{(1,0),(0,1)\}\subseteq k^2$.

(a) Prove that $k[x,y]/I$ is a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space.

(b) Do we necessarily have $\dim_k(k[x,y]/I)<100$?
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step
Part (a): Finite-dimensionality of $k[x,y]/I$.

::: pf-proof
*Proof:*

::: pf-step
Let $p_1 = (1, 0)$ and $p_2 = (0, 1)$ in $k^2$, with corresponding maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m}_1 = (x - 1, y)$ and $\mathfrak{m}_2 = (x, y - 1)$ in $k[x, y]$.

::: pf-proof
These are the maximal ideals of the two points, each generated by the two coordinate functions vanishing there.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By Hilbert's Nullstellensatz, since $k$ is algebraically closed, the radical of $I$ is
$$\sqrt{I} = I(V(I)) = I(\{p_1, p_2\}) = \mathfrak{m}_1 \cap \mathfrak{m}_2.$$

::: pf-proof
The Nullstellensatz over an algebraically closed field identifies the radical of $I$ with the ideal of all polynomials vanishing on $V(I)$, and that ideal is the intersection of the maximal ideals of the two points.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since the polynomial ring $k[x, y]$ is Noetherian, the ideal $\sqrt{I}$ is finitely generated, so there exists an integer $N \ge 1$ such that $(\sqrt{I})^N \subseteq I$.

::: pf-proof
A finitely generated ideal has a power inside $I$: take the maximum of the exponents needed for a finite generating set.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Note that $\mathfrak{m}_1 + \mathfrak{m}_2 = k[x, y]$ because $-(x - 1) + x = 1 \in \mathfrak{m}_1 + \mathfrak{m}_2$.

::: pf-proof
The displayed combination exhibits $1$ in the sum of the two ideals.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Comaximality implies that for any integer $N \ge 1$, the power ideals $\mathfrak{m}_1^N$ and $\mathfrak{m}_2^N$ are comaximal: $\mathfrak{m}_1^N + \mathfrak{m}_2^N = k[x, y]$.

::: pf-proof
Powers of comaximal ideals are comaximal: if $a+b=1$ with $a\in\mathfrak{m}_1$ and $b\in\mathfrak{m}_2$, then $(a+b)^{2N-1}=1$ expands into terms each having a factor from $\mathfrak{m}_1^N$ or from $\mathfrak{m}_2^N$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Therefore $\mathfrak{m}_1^N \cap \mathfrak{m}_2^N = \mathfrak{m}_1^N \mathfrak{m}_2^N \subseteq (\mathfrak{m}_1 \cap \mathfrak{m}_2)^N \subseteq I$.

::: pf-proof
Comaximal ideals have intersection equal to their product, and $\mathfrak{m}_1\mathfrak{m}_2\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_1\cap\mathfrak{m}_2$ gives the inclusion of the powers.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By the Chinese Remainder Theorem:
$$k[x, y] / (\mathfrak{m}_1^N \cap \mathfrak{m}_2^N) \cong k[x, y] / \mathfrak{m}_1^N \oplus k[x, y] / \mathfrak{m}_2^N.$$

::: pf-proof
The Chinese remainder theorem applies to comaximal ideals.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Translating coordinates to the origin shows that $k[x, y]/\mathfrak{m}_1^N \cong k[u, v]/(u, v)^N$, which has a $k$-basis given by the monomials $\{u^i v^j : i + j < N\}$ of dimension $\binom{N+1}{2} < \infty$.

::: pf-proof
The substitution $u=x-1$, $v=y$ is an isomorphism of $k$-algebras carrying $\mathfrak{m}_1$ onto $(u,v)$, and the monomials with $i+j<N$ form a basis of the quotient by the $N$-th power.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #quotient-dimension-formula}
Thus $k[x, y] / (\mathfrak{m}_1^N \cap \mathfrak{m}_2^N)$ is a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space of dimension $2 \binom{N+1}{2} = N(N+1)$.

::: pf-proof
The two summands have equal dimension $\binom{N+1}{2}$, and twice that is $N(N+1)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since $k[x, y]/I$ is a vector space quotient of $k[x, y] / (\mathfrak{m}_1^N \cap \mathfrak{m}_2^N)$, we have $\dim_k(k[x, y]/I) \le N(N+1) < \infty$.

::: pf-proof
A quotient of a vector space has dimension at most that of the space, and $I$ contains the ideal being quotiented by.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
Part (b): Arbitrarily large vector space dimension.

::: pf-proof
*Proof:*

::: pf-step
For any positive integer $N \ge 1$, define the ideal $I_N = \mathfrak{m}_1^N \cap \mathfrak{m}_2^N = \mathfrak{m}_1^N \mathfrak{m}_2^N$.

::: pf-proof
This is a definition of a family of ideals indexed by $N$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The variety of $I_N$ is $V(I_N) = V(\mathfrak{m}_1^N) \cup V(\mathfrak{m}_2^N) = V(\mathfrak{m}_1) \cup V(\mathfrak{m}_2) = \{(1, 0), (0, 1)\} = V(I)$.

::: pf-proof
The variety of an intersection is the union of the varieties, and the variety of a power of a maximal ideal is the single corresponding point.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By step [](#quotient-dimension-formula){.pf-ref}, the dimension of the quotient ring is exactly
$$\dim_k(k[x, y]/I_N) = N(N + 1).$$

::: pf-proof
This is the computation of the formula above with the two exponents equal to $N$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Setting $N = 10$ yields $\dim_k(k[x, y]/I_{10}) = 10(11) = 110 \ge 100$.

::: pf-proof
Substituting $N=10$ into $N(N+1)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Therefore it is not necessarily true that $\dim_k(k[x,y]/I) < 100$.

::: pf-proof
An ideal with the required variety and dimension $110$ has been exhibited.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
Conclusion:

::: pf-proof
*Proof:*

$k[x, y]/I$ is always finite-dimensional over $k$, but its dimension can be arbitrarily large (so $\dim_k(k[x, y]/I) < 100$ does not necessarily hold).
:::
