::: problem
(a) Define what it means for a topological space $X$ to be:

1. **Connected**

2. **Locally connected**

(b) Give, with proof, an example of a space that is connected but not locally connected.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. A topological space $X$ is connected if it cannot be written as
\[
X=U\cup V
\]
with $U,V\subseteq X$ disjoint, nonempty, and open.
::: {.proof}
This is the definition of connectedness.
Equivalently, the only subsets of $X$ that are both open and closed are $\varnothing$ and $X$.
:::

<1>2. A topological space $X$ is locally connected if for every $x\in X$ and every open neighborhood $U$ of $x$, there is a connected open set $V$ such that
\[
x\in V\subseteq U.
\]
::: {.proof}
This is the neighborhood-basis definition of local connectedness: every point has a basis of connected open neighborhoods.
:::

<1>3. Consider the comb space
\[
C
=
([0,1]\times\{0\})
\cup
(\{0\}\times[0,1])
\cup
\bigcup_{n\ge1}\left(\left\{\frac1n\right\}\times[0,1]\right)
\subset\mathbb R^2.
\]
Then $C$ is path connected, hence connected.
::: {.proof}
Let $(x,y)\in C$.
If $y>0$, then the definition of $C$ forces either
\[
x=0
\]
or
\[
x=\frac1n
\]
for some $n\ge1$.
In either case the vertical segment from $(x,y)$ to $(x,0)$ lies entirely in $C$.
The horizontal segment from $(x,0)$ to $(0,0)$ lies in
\[
[0,1]\times\{0\}\subset C.
\]
If $y=0$, only the horizontal segment is needed.
Thus every point of $C$ can be joined by a path to $(0,0)$, so $C$ is path connected.
Every path-connected space is connected.
:::

<1>4. The comb space $C$ is not locally connected at
\[
p=\left(0,\frac12\right).
\]
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
U=C\cap B\left(p,\frac14\right).
\]
This is an open neighborhood of $p$ in the subspace $C$.
Suppose, contrary to <1>4, that there is a connected open set $V\subseteq C$ with
\[
p\in V\subseteq U.
\]

Because $V$ is open in $C$, there is some $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
C\cap B(p,\varepsilon)\subseteq V.
\]
Choose $n$ sufficiently large that
\[
\frac1n<\varepsilon.
\]
Then
\[
q=\left(\frac1n,\frac12\right)
\in C\cap B(p,\varepsilon)
\subseteq V.
\]

Every point of $U$ has second coordinate strictly between $1/4$ and $3/4$.
Hence $U$ does not meet the horizontal base $[0,1]\times\{0\}$.
Therefore every point of $V\subseteq U$ has first coordinate in
\[
D=\{0\}\cup\left\{\frac1m:m\ge1\right\}.
\]
The continuous projection
\[
\operatorname{pr}_1:V\to\mathbb R
\]
has image contained in $D$ and contains both
\[
0=\operatorname{pr}_1(p)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac1n=\operatorname{pr}_1(q).
\]
If $V$ were connected, its continuous image $\operatorname{pr}_1(V)$ would be connected in $\mathbb R$.
Any connected subset of $\mathbb R$ containing $0$ and $1/n$ contains the whole interval $[0,1/n]$.
But $D$ contains no nontrivial interval, a contradiction.

Thus no connected open neighborhood of $p$ is contained in $U$.
By <1>2, $C$ is not locally connected at $p$.
:::

<1>5. Therefore $C$ is a connected space that is not locally connected.
::: {.proof}
Connectedness follows from <1>3 and failure of local connectedness from <1>4.
:::
