::: problem
(a) Define what it means for a topological space $X$ to be:
1. **Connected**
2. **Locally connected**

(b) Give, with proof, an example of a space that is connected but not locally connected.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Define connectedness and local connectedness in (a), and prove that the topologist's sine curve is connected but not locally connected in (b).

<1>1. Part (a): Definitions.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. **Connectedness:** A topological space $X$ is *connected* if it cannot be written as the union of two disjoint, non-empty open subsets of $X$ (equivalently, the only subsets of $X$ that are both open and closed are $\emptyset$ and $X$).
    <2>2. **Local connectedness:** A topological space $X$ is *locally connected at $x \in X$* if for every open neighborhood $U$ of $x$, there exists a connected open neighborhood $V$ of $x$ such that $V \subseteq U$. The space $X$ is *locally connected* if it is locally connected at every point $x \in X$.

<1>2. Part (b): The topologist's sine curve is connected.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Define the subspace $T = S \cup L \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ with the subspace topology, where
    $$S = \left\{\left(x, \sin\frac{1}{x}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \;\middle|\; 0 < x \le 1\right\}, \quad L = \{0\} \times [-1, 1] = \{(0, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid -1 \le y \le 1\}.$$
    <2>2. The map $g: (0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $g(x) = (x, \sin(1/x))$ is continuous.
    <2>3. Since the interval $(0, 1]$ is connected, its continuous image $S = g((0, 1])$ is connected.
    <2>4. $L \subseteq \overline{S}$:
        - For any $(0, y_0) \in L$ with $y_0 \in [-1, 1]$, choose $\theta_0 \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ such that $\sin(\theta_0) = y_0$.
        - For each $n \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$, define $x_n = \frac{1}{\theta_0 + 2\pi n} \in (0, 1]$.
        - Then $(x_n, \sin(1/x_n)) = (x_n, y_0) \in S$.
        - As $n \to \infty$, $x_n \to 0$, so $(x_n, y_0) \to (0, y_0)$.
        - Thus every point of $L$ is a limit point of $S$, so $L \subseteq \overline{S}$.
    <2>5. Therefore $S \subseteq T \subseteq \overline{S}$.
    <2>6. Since $S$ is connected and $S \subseteq T \subseteq \overline{S}$, $T$ is connected.

<1>3. Part (b): $T$ is not locally connected at $(0, 0)$.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Consider the open neighborhood $U = B_{1/2}((0, 0)) \cap T$ of $(0, 0)$ in $T$.
    <2>2. Suppose for contradiction that there exists a connected open neighborhood $V$ of $(0, 0)$ in $T$ such that $V \subseteq U$.
    <2>3. Since $V$ is open in $T$, there exists $\delta \in (0, 1/2)$ such that $B_\delta((0, 0)) \cap T \subseteq V$.
    <2>4. Choose an integer $N \in \mathbb{N}$ large enough that $\frac{1}{2\pi N} < \delta$.
    <2>5. For each $n \ge N$, the arc of $S$ given by $A_n = \left\{\left(x, \sin\frac{1}{x}\right) \;\middle|\; \frac{1}{(2n+1)\pi} < x < \frac{1}{2n\pi}\right\}$ passes through $(0, \delta)$ and contains points with $y$-coordinates ranging between 0 and 1.
    <2>6. In $U \subset \{|y| < 1/2\}$, each component of $V \cap S$ is an open sub-arc that cannot reach $y = 1$ and is bounded away from $L$.
    <2>7. Thus $V$ contains a point $(x_0, \sin(1/x_0)) \in S$ on an isolated arc that is disconnected from $L \cap V$ within $U$.
    <2>8. This contradicts the assumption that $V$ is connected.
    <2>9. Therefore $T$ is not locally connected at $(0, 0)$.

<1>4. Conclusion:
    *Proof:*
    $T = S \cup L$ is connected because $S \subseteq T \subseteq \overline{S}$ for connected $S$, but fails to be locally connected at $(0, 0)$ due to infinite oscillation.
:::
