::: {.problem}
Let $G$ be a finite simple group of order $n$.
Determine the number of normal subgroups of
\[
G\times G.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $G$ is abelian, then $n=p$ is prime and $G\cong C_p$.

::: {.proof}
Choose $g\neq e$. Since $G$ is abelian, the nontrivial subgroup $\langle g\rangle$ is normal, so simplicity forces
\[
\langle g\rangle=G.
\]
Thus $G$ is cyclic. A finite cyclic group is simple exactly when its order is prime, so $n=p$ is prime and $G\cong C_p$.
:::

<1>2. If $G$ is abelian, then $G\times G$ has exactly
\[
\boxed{n+3}
\]
normal subgroups.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1,
\[
G\times G\cong C_p\times C_p\cong\mathbb F_p^2.
\]
Its subgroups are exactly the vector subspaces of the two-dimensional vector space $\mathbb F_p^2$. There is one zero subspace, one whole space, and
\[
\frac{p^2-1}{p-1}=p+1
\]
one-dimensional subspaces. Since the group is abelian, every subgroup is normal. Hence the total number is
\[
1+(p+1)+1=p+3=n+3.
\]
:::

<1>3. Suppose $G$ is nonabelian and $N\triangleleft G\times G$. Each projection
\[
\pi_i(N)\subseteq G
\]
is either $\{e\}$ or $G$.

::: {.proof}
The image of a normal subgroup under either projection
\[
\pi_i:G\times G\to G
\]
is normal in $G$. Since $G$ is simple, each image is therefore either trivial or all of $G$.
:::

<1>4. If one projection of $N$ is trivial, then
\[
N\in\bigl\{\{(e,e)\},\,G\times\{e\},\,\{e\}\times G\bigr\}.
\]

::: {.proof}
If both projections are trivial, then $N$ is trivial. If, for example,
\[
\pi_2(N)=\{e\},
\]
then $N\subseteq G\times\{e\}$. Under the identification $G\times\{e\}\cong G$, the subgroup $N$ is normal in $G$, so simplicity gives either
\[
N=\{(e,e)\}
\quad\text{or}\quad
N=G\times\{e\}.
\]
The case $\pi_1(N)=\{e\}$ is symmetric.
:::

<1>5. If both projections of $N$ are surjective, then $N=G\times G$.

::: {.proof}
Consider
\[
K_1:=N\cap(G\times\{e\}).
\]
Under $G\times\{e\}\cong G$, this is a normal subgroup of $G$, so it is either trivial or all of $G\times\{e\}$.

If $K_1=G\times\{e\}$, then surjectivity of $\pi_2|_N$ gives, for every $b\in G$, some $(a,b)\in N$. Multiplying by $(a^{-1},e)\in N$ gives $(e,b)\in N$. Hence both direct factors lie in $N$, so $N=G\times G$.

It remains to rule out $K_1=\{(e,e)\}$. In that case, because $\pi_2|_N$ is surjective and its kernel is $K_1$, the map
\[
\pi_2|_N:N\to G
\]
is an isomorphism. Likewise $\pi_1|_N$ is an isomorphism. Thus $N$ is the graph of an automorphism $\varphi:G\to G$:
\[
N=\{(g,\varphi(g)):g\in G\}.
\]
Normality under conjugation by $(x,e)$ gives
\[
(xgx^{-1},\varphi(g))\in N
\]
for all $g,x\in G$. Since this point lies in the graph,
\[
\varphi(xgx^{-1})=\varphi(g).
\]
Injectivity of $\varphi$ yields
\[
xgx^{-1}=g
\]
for all $g,x$, so $G$ is abelian, contradicting the present hypothesis. Therefore $K_1$ cannot be trivial, and the first case forces $N=G\times G$.
:::

<1>6. If $G$ is nonabelian, then $G\times G$ has exactly
\[
\boxed{4}
\]
normal subgroups.

::: {.proof}
By steps <1>3 through <1>5, the only possibilities are
\[
\{(e,e)\},
\qquad
G\times\{e\},
\qquad
\{e\}\times G,
\qquad
G\times G.
\]
All four are visibly normal.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Thus the number of normal subgroups is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{cases}
n+3,&G\text{ is abelian, equivalently }n\text{ is prime},\\
4,&G\text{ is nonabelian}.
\end{cases}}
\]
Steps <1>2 and <1>6 prove the two cases.
:::
:::
