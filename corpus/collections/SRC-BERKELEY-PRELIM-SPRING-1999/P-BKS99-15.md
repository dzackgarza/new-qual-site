::: {.problem}
Let $M$ be a square complex matrix and let
$$
S=\{XMX^{-1}:X\text{ is nonsingular}\}
$$
be its similarity class.
Show that $M$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of the identity if and only if no matrix in $S$ has a zero anywhere on its diagonal.
:::

::: {.solution}

Regard $M$ as the matrix of a linear operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional complex vector space $V$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

If $M=\lambda I$ with $\lambda\neq0$, then no matrix in $S$ has a zero on its diagonal.


::: pf-proof

For every nonsingular $X$,
$$
X(\lambda I)X^{-1}=\lambda I.
$$
Thus $S=\{\lambda I\}$, and every diagonal entry of its only matrix is the nonzero number $\lambda$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

If $T(v)\in\operatorname{span}\{v\}$ for every $v\in V$, then $T$ is a scalar operator.


::: pf-proof

Choose a nonzero vector $u$ and write $T(u)=\alpha u$. If $v$ is linearly independent from $u$, write
$$
T(v)=\beta v,
\qquad
T(u+v)=\gamma(u+v).
$$
By linearity,
$$
\alpha u+\beta v
=T(u)+T(v)
=T(u+v)
=\gamma u+\gamma v.
$$
Independence of $u,v$ gives $\alpha=\beta=\gamma$. Thus every vector independent from $u$ is multiplied by $\alpha$, and every vector proportional to $u$ is also multiplied by $\alpha$. Hence $T=\alpha I$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

If $M$ is not a scalar matrix, then some matrix in $S$ has a zero diagonal entry.


::: pf-proof

By the contrapositive of step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}, there is a nonzero $v\in V$ such that $T(v)$ is not a scalar multiple of $v$. Hence
$$
v,\ T(v)
$$
are linearly independent. Extend them to an ordered basis
$$
\mathcal B=(v,T(v),v_3,\ldots,v_n).
$$
In this basis, the first column of the matrix of $T$ is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\1\\0\\ \vdots\\0
\end{pmatrix},
$$
because the image of the first basis vector is the second basis vector. Therefore the first diagonal entry of the matrix of $T$ in $\mathcal B$ is $0$. That matrix is similar to $M$, so it belongs to $S$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

If $M=0$, then a matrix in $S$ has a zero diagonal entry.


::: pf-proof

Indeed every matrix in $S$ equals $0$, whose entire diagonal is zero.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

If no matrix in $S$ has a zero anywhere on its diagonal, then $M$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of the identity.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref} shows that $M$ must be scalar. Step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} excludes the zero scalar. Hence
$$
M=\lambda I
$$
for some $\lambda\neq0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} proves one implication and step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} proves the converse.

:::
