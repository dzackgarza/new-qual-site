::: {.problem}
Let $k$ be a positive integer. Determine all real numbers $c$ such that every real sequence $(x_n)$ satisfying
\[
\frac12(x_{n+1}+x_{n-1})=cx_n
\]
has period $k$, i.e.
\[
x_{n+k}=x_n
\]
for every $n$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
$$
A_c\coloneqq
\begin{pmatrix}
2c&-1\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

If
$$
v_n\coloneqq
\begin{pmatrix}
x_n\\x_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix},
$$
then the recurrence is equivalent to
$$
v_{n+1}=A_cv_n.
$$

::: pf-proof

The recurrence may be rewritten as
$$
x_{n+1}=2cx_n-x_{n-1}.
$$
Thus
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
x_{n+1}\\x_n
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2c&-1\\1&0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_n\\x_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Every solution of the recurrence satisfies
$$
x_{n+k}=x_n
$$
for every $n$ if and only if
$$
A_c^k=I.
$$

::: pf-proof

If $A_c^k=I$, then step [](#s1){.pf-ref} gives
$$
v_{n+k}=A_c^kv_n=v_n,
$$
so in particular $x_{n+k}=x_n$.

Conversely, the initial pair $(x_1,x_0)$ may be chosen arbitrarily in $\RR^2$. If every resulting solution satisfies the period condition, then
$$
A_c^k
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\x_0
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_{k+1}\\x_k
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\x_0
\end{pmatrix}
$$
for every vector in $\RR^2$. Hence $A_c^k=I$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

The characteristic polynomial of $A_c$ is
$$
q_c(\lambda)=\lambda^2-2c\lambda+1.
$$

::: pf-proof

Directly,
$$
\det(\lambda I-A_c)
=
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda-2c&1\\
-1&\lambda
\end{pmatrix}
=
\lambda^2-2c\lambda+1.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

If $A_c^k=I$, then
$$
c=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{k}\right)
$$
for some integer $m$ with
$$
1\le m<\frac{k}{2}.
$$

::: pf-proof

If $A_c^k=I$, every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A_c$ satisfies $\lambda^k=1$. Since
$$
\det A_c=1,
$$
the two eigenvalues are $\lambda$ and $\lambda^{-1}$. Thus for some integer $m$,
$$
\lambda=e^{2\pi i m/k},
$$
and step [](#s3){.pf-ref} gives
$$
2c
=
\lambda+\lambda^{-1}
=
2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{k}\right).
$$

The cases $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=-1$ cannot occur. Indeed, $A_c^k=I$ implies that the minimal polynomial of $A_c$ divides $t^k-1$, which has no repeated roots over $\CC$, so $A_c$ is diagonalizable. If $\lambda=1$ were the only eigenvalue, diagonalizability would force $A_c=I$, impossible because the lower-left entry of $A_c$ is $1$. Similarly, if $\lambda=-1$ were the only eigenvalue, diagonalizability would force $A_c=-I$, again impossible.

Replacing $m$ by $k-m$ does not change the cosine, so every admissible value has a representative with
$$
1\le m<k/2.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

Conversely, if
$$
c=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{k}\right)
$$
for an integer $m$ with $1\le m<k/2$, then
$$
A_c^k=I.
$$

::: pf-proof

Put
$$
\theta=\frac{2\pi m}{k}.
$$
Since $0<\theta<\pi$, the two roots of the characteristic polynomial in step [](#s3){.pf-ref} are distinct:
$$
e^{i\theta}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
e^{-i\theta}.
$$
Hence $A_c$ is diagonalizable over $\CC$. Both eigenvalues have $k$th power $1$, so after diagonalization the $k$th power of $A_c$ is the identity. Therefore $A_c^k=I$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

The required real numbers are exactly
$$
\boxed{
c=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{k}\right),
\qquad
m=1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor}.
$$
For $k=1$ or $k=2$, this set is empty.

::: pf-proof

Step [](#s4){.pf-ref} gives necessity, and step [](#s5){.pf-ref} gives sufficiency. The integers satisfying $1\le m<k/2$ are exactly
$$
1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s6){.pf-ref} is the requested classification.
