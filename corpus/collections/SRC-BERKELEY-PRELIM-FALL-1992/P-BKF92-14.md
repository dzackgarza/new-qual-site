::: {.problem}
Let $f,g:\mathbb R\to(0,\infty)$ be continuous with $g\le f$. Suppose the initial-value problem
\[
x'=f(x),
\qquad
x(0)=0,
\]
has a solution defined on all of $\mathbb R$. Prove that
\[
x'=g(x),
\qquad
x(0)=0,
\]
also has a solution defined on all of $\mathbb R$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Define
$$
F(u)\coloneqq\int_0^u\frac{ds}{f(s)}.
$$

<1>1. The function $F$ is strictly increasing and satisfies
$$
F'(u)=\frac1{f(u)}>0.
$$

::: {.proof}
The function $f$ is positive and continuous, so $1/f$ is positive and continuous. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives the derivative formula, and positivity of the derivative gives strict increase.
:::

<1>2. If $x:\RR\to\RR$ is the assumed global solution of
$$
x'=f(x),
\qquad
x(0)=0,
$$
then
$$
F(x(t))=t
$$
for every $t\in\RR$.

::: {.proof}
By the chain rule and step <1>1,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}F(x(t))
=
F'(x(t))x'(t)
=
\frac{1}{f(x(t))}f(x(t))
=1.
$$
At $t=0$,
$$
F(x(0))=F(0)=0.
$$
Therefore $F(x(t))=t$.
:::

<1>3. The function $F$ maps $\RR$ onto $\RR$, and hence
$$
\lim_{u\to\infty}F(u)=\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{u\to-\infty}F(u)=-\infty.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>2 shows that every $t\in\RR$ is a value of $F$, namely $F(x(t))$. Thus $F$ is onto. Since $F$ is strictly increasing by step <1>1, surjectivity forces the stated endpoint limits.
:::

<1>4. Define
$$
G(u)\coloneqq\int_0^u\frac{ds}{g(s)}.
$$
Then
$$
\lim_{u\to\infty}G(u)=\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{u\to-\infty}G(u)=-\infty.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since $0<g\le f$,
$$
\frac1g\ge\frac1f.
$$
For $u>0$,
$$
G(u)
=
\int_0^u\frac{ds}{g(s)}
\ge
\int_0^u\frac{ds}{f(s)}
=F(u),
$$
so step <1>3 gives $G(u)\to\infty$.

For $u<0$,
$$
G(u)
=
-\int_u^0\frac{ds}{g(s)}
\le
-\int_u^0\frac{ds}{f(s)}
=F(u),
$$
so step <1>3 gives $G(u)\to-\infty$.
:::

<1>5. The function $G:\RR\to\RR$ is a $C^1$ bijection with $G'(u)>0$ everywhere.

::: {.proof}
As in step <1>1,
$$
G'(u)=\frac1{g(u)}>0,
$$
so $G$ is strictly increasing. Step <1>4 gives both endpoint limits, hence $G$ is onto. Strict increase gives injectivity.
:::

<1>6. Define
$$
y(t)\coloneqq G^{-1}(t).
$$
Then $y$ is defined for every $t\in\RR$ and satisfies
$$
y'=g(y),
\qquad
y(0)=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>5, the inverse is defined on all of $\RR$. Since $G'$ is everywhere nonzero, the inverse function theorem gives
$$
y'(t)
=
\frac{1}{G'(y(t))}
=
g(y(t)).
$$
Also $G(0)=0$, so $y(0)=G^{-1}(0)=0$.
:::

<1>7. Hence the initial-value problem
$$
x'=g(x),
\qquad
x(0)=0
$$
has a solution defined on all of $\RR$.

::: {.proof}
The function $y$ constructed in step <1>6 is such a solution.
:::

<1>8. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>7 is the required conclusion.
:::
