::: {.problem}
Let $f$ be analytic in a region $D$ and $\gamma$ a rectifiable curve in $D$ with interior in $D$.

Prove that if $f(z)$ is real for all $z\in \gamma$, then $f$ is constant.
:::

::: {.solution}
The statement's word "interior" is what makes $\gamma$ a closed curve bounding a region $\Omega\subset D$: on an arc there is no interior and the claim would be false, as $f(z)=z$ on a segment shows. With that reading, $\operatorname{Im}f$ is harmonic on $\Omega$ and vanishes on $\partial\Omega$, so it vanishes on $\Omega$; $f(\Omega)$ is then contained in the real axis, and the open mapping theorem forces $f$ constant there, whence on $D$ by the identity theorem. The argument is this card's own: no retained solution set covers this problem.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #reading}
$\gamma$ is a closed curve in $D$ whose interior is a bounded region $\Omega$ with $\overline\Omega\subset D$ and $\partial\Omega\subseteq\gamma$, and the hypothesis is that $f$ is real on $\partial\Omega$.

::: pf-proof
The statement speaks of the interior of $\gamma$, which for a rectifiable closed curve $\gamma$ means the bounded components of $\CC\setminus\gamma^*$; let $\Omega$ be one such component, a region, and assume it lies in $D$ as the statement requires. Its boundary is contained in the trace $\gamma^*$ of $\gamma$, and $\overline\Omega=\Omega\cup\partial\Omega$ is compact, hence contained in the open set $D$. The hypothesis that $f$ is real on $\gamma$ gives that $f$ is real on $\partial\Omega$. Some reading of this kind is necessary: for an arc, or for a curve with no interior, the claim is false, as $f(z)=z$ on the segment $[-1,1]\subset\CC$ shows.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #harmonic}
$v=\operatorname{Im}f$ is harmonic on $\Omega$ and continuous on $\overline\Omega$, and $v=0$ on $\partial\Omega$.

::: pf-proof
$f$ is analytic on $D$ and $\overline\Omega\subset D$, so $u=\Re f$ and $v=\Im f$ are harmonic on a neighbourhood of $\overline\Omega$: they are $C^2$ there with $\Delta u=\Delta v=0$, as the real and imaginary parts of an analytic function. In particular $v$ is harmonic on $\Omega$ and continuous on the compact set $\overline\Omega$. By step [](#reading){.pf-ref} $f$ is real on $\partial\Omega$, so $v=0$ there.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #zero}
$v\equiv0$ on $\Omega$.

::: pf-proof
$v$ is continuous on $\overline\Omega$, which is compact, so $v$ attains its maximum and minimum on $\overline\Omega$; each is attained either at a boundary point, where $v=0$, or at an interior point of $\Omega$, where the maximum principle for harmonic functions makes $v$ constant. In either case $\max_{\overline\Omega}v\le0$ and $\min_{\overline\Omega}v\ge0$, so $v=0$ on $\Omega$.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #real}
$f(\Omega)\subseteq\RR$.

::: pf-proof
For $z\in\Omega$ one has $\Im f(z)=v(z)=0$ by step [](#zero){.pf-ref}, so $f(z)=\Re f(z)\in\RR$.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #constant}
$f$ is constant on $\Omega$, and hence on $D$.

::: pf-proof
If $f$ were not constant on the region $\Omega$, the open mapping theorem would make $f(\Omega)$ an open subset of $\CC$; but $f(\Omega)\subseteq\RR$ by step [](#real){.pf-ref}, and $\RR$ has empty interior in $\CC$, so $f(\Omega)$ contains no disc and is not open — a contradiction. Hence $f$ is constant on $\Omega$. Since $D$ is a region and $f$ is analytic on $D$, the identity theorem applied to $f$ and to the constant function with that value on the nonempty open set $\Omega$ gives that $f$ is constant on $D$.
:::

:::


::: {.pf-step #conclusion}
$f$ is constant on $D$, as asserted.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#reading){.pf-ref} to [](#constant){.pf-ref} carry the hypothesis through to the conclusion: $f$ real on the boundary of the interior region forces $\operatorname{Im}f=0$ there, the open mapping theorem makes $f$ constant on that region, and connectedness of $D$ spreads the constant.
:::
