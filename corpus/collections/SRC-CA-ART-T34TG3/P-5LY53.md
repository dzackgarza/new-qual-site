::: {.problem}
Show that if $\xi\in \RR$, then
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-2 \pi i x \xi}}{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{2}} d x=\frac{\pi}{2}(1+2 \pi|\xi|) e^{-2 \pi|\xi|}
.\]
:::

::: {.solution}
Write $I(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{-2\pi i x \xi}}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx$ and $a = 2\pi \abs{\xi} \ge 0$. Let $f(z) = \frac{e^{i a z}}{(z^2 + 1)^2}$, and for $R > 1$ let $\Gamma_R$ consist of $[-R, R]$ and the arc $C_R$: $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $\theta \in [0, \pi]$.

<1>1. $I(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{i a x}}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx$.

<2>1. $I$ is an even function of $\xi$.

::: {.proof}
The substitution $u = -x$ gives
$$I(-\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{2\pi i x \xi}}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{-2\pi i u \xi}}{(1+u^2)^2}\,du = I(\xi).$$
:::

<2>2. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
By step <2>1 we may assume $\xi \le 0$. Then $-2\pi\xi = a$, so $e^{-2\pi i x\xi} = e^{iax}$.
:::

<1>2. In the upper half-plane, $f$ has exactly one singularity, a pole of order $2$ at $z = i$.

::: {.proof}
$f(z) = \frac{e^{i a z}}{(z - i)^2 (z + i)^2}$, the numerator is entire and nonvanishing, and $z=-i$ lies in the lower half-plane.
:::

<1>3. $\operatorname{Res}(f, i) = \dfrac{(1 + a) e^{-a}}{4i}$.

::: {.proof}
For a pole of order $2$,
$$\operatorname{Res}(f, i) = \lim_{z \to i} \frac{d}{dz}\left[\frac{e^{i a z}}{(z + i)^2}\right]
= \lim_{z \to i}\frac{i a (z + i) - 2}{(z + i)^3} e^{i a z}.$$
At $z=i$: $z + i = 2i$, $(z + i)^3 = -8i$, $ia(2i) - 2 = -2(1 + a)$, and $e^{i a i} = e^{-a}$. Hence $\operatorname{Res}(f, i) = \frac{-2(1 + a) e^{-a}}{-8i}$.
:::

<1>4. $\displaystyle\int_{C_R} f(z)\,dz \to 0$ as $R\to\infty$.

::: {.proof}
For $z = x + i y \in C_R$, $y \ge 0$ and $a\ge0$, so $\abs{e^{i a z}} = e^{-a y} \le 1$. By the reverse triangle inequality, $\abs{z^2 + 1} \ge R^2 - 1 > 0$, so $\abs{f(z)} \le (R^2 - 1)^{-2}$. The arc has length $\pi R$, so
$$\left|\int_{C_R} f(z)\,dz\right| \le \frac{\pi R}{(R^2 - 1)^2} \longrightarrow 0.$$
:::

<1>5. $I(\xi) = \boxed{\dfrac{\pi}{2}(1 + 2\pi \abs{\xi}) e^{-2\pi \abs{\xi}}}$.

::: {.proof}
By the residue theorem and steps <1>2 and <1>3,
$$\oint_{\Gamma_R} f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{(1 + a) e^{-a}}{4i} = \frac{\pi}{2}(1 + a) e^{-a}$$
for every $R>1$. Letting $R\to\infty$, step <1>4 shows that $\int_{-R}^R f(x)\,dx \to \frac{\pi}{2}(1 + a) e^{-a}$, which is $I(\xi)$ by step <1>1. Substitute $a = 2\pi\abs{\xi}$.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 is the required identity.
:::
