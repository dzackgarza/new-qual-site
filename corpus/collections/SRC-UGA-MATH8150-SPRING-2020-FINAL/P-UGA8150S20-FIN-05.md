::: {.problem}
Consider
\[
f(z)=\frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right),\qquad z\in\CC\setminus\{0\}.
\]

(a) Show that $f$ is univalent on the punctured disk $\DD\setminus\{0\}$. What is the image of $|z|=r<1$ under this map?

(b) Show that $f$ is univalent on $\{z\in\CC:|z|>1\}$. What is the image of this domain?

(c) What is the inverse mapping
\[
f^{-1}:\CC\setminus[-1,1]\longrightarrow \DD\setminus\{0\}?
\]
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

If
\[
f(z_1)=f(z_2),
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
z_1=z_2
\quad\text{or}\quad
z_1z_2=1}.
\]


::: pf-proof

The equality
\[
z_1+\frac1{z_1}
=
z_2+\frac1{z_2}
\]
gives
\[
z_1-z_2
=
\frac1{z_2}-\frac1{z_1}
=
\frac{z_1-z_2}{z_1z_2}.
\]
Hence
\[
(z_1-z_2)
\left(
1-\frac1{z_1z_2}
\right)
=0,
\]
which yields the two alternatives.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The map $f$ is univalent on
\[
\DD\setminus\{0\}.
\]


::: pf-proof

Suppose $z_1,z_2$ lie in the punctured unit disk and
\[
f(z_1)=f(z_2).
\]
By step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}, either $z_1=z_2$ or $z_1z_2=1$. The second possibility is
impossible because
\[
|z_1z_2|<1.
\]
Hence $z_1=z_2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

If
\[
z=re^{it},
\qquad
0<r<1,
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
f(re^{it})
=
\frac12\left(r+\frac1r\right)\cos t
+
\frac{i}{2}\left(r-\frac1r\right)\sin t}.
\]


::: pf-proof

Use
\[
re^{it}
=
r(\cos t+i\sin t)
\]
and
\[
\frac1{re^{it}}
=
\frac1r(\cos t-i\sin t),
\]
then add and divide by $2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Therefore the image of the circle $|z|=r<1$ is the ellipse
\[
\boxed{
\frac{x^2}{
\left(\frac12(r+r^{-1})\right)^2
}
+
\frac{y^2}{
\left(\frac12(r^{-1}-r)\right)^2
}
=1}.
\]


::: pf-proof

From step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref},
\[
x
=
\frac12(r+r^{-1})\cos t,
\]
while
\[
y
=
\frac12(r-r^{-1})\sin t.
\]
Since the sign of the second semiaxis does not affect the ellipse equation,
squaring and adding
\[
\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1
\]
gives the displayed equation.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

The map $f$ is univalent on
\[
E
=
\{z\in\CC:|z|>1\}.
\]


::: pf-proof

If $z_1,z_2\in E$ and
\[
f(z_1)=f(z_2),
\]
step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} again gives either $z_1=z_2$ or $z_1z_2=1$. The latter is
impossible because
\[
|z_1z_2|>1.
\]
Thus $f$ is injective on $E$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

A complex number $w$ lies in the image of $f$ exactly when the
quadratic
\[
z^2-2wz+1=0
\]
has a solution in the relevant domain.


::: pf-proof

The equation
\[
w
=
\frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right)
\]
is equivalent, after multiplying by $2z$, to
\[
z^2-2wz+1=0.
\]
Its two roots, counted with multiplicity, have product $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

If $w\in[-1,1]$, both roots of
\[
z^2-2wz+1=0
\]
lie on the unit circle.


::: pf-proof

Write
\[
w=\cos t
\]
for some $t\in[0,\pi]$. Then
\[
z^2-2(\cos t)z+1
=
(z-e^{it})(z-e^{-it}),
\]
so the roots have modulus $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

If
\[
w\in\CC\setminus[-1,1],
\]
then one root of the quadratic lies in $\DD\setminus\{0\}$ and the
other lies in $E$.


::: pf-proof

The roots have product $1$ by step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref}. If one root had modulus $1$, say
\[
z=e^{it},
\]
then
\[
w=f(z)
=
\frac12(e^{it}+e^{-it})
=
\cos t
\in[-1,1],
\]
contrary to the hypothesis. Thus neither root lies on the unit circle.
Since the product of their moduli is $1$, exactly one root has modulus less
than $1$ and the other has modulus greater than $1$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
f(E)
=
\CC\setminus[-1,1]}.
\]


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} shows that no point of $[-1,1]$ is attained by a point with
$|z|>1$. Step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} shows that every point outside $[-1,1]$ has exactly one
preimage in $E$. Thus the image is precisely the slit plane.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Let
\[
S(w)=\sqrt{w^2-1}
\]
denote the holomorphic branch on
\[
\CC\setminus[-1,1]
\]
characterized by
\[
\frac{S(w)}{w}\longrightarrow1
\qquad(w\to\infty).
\]
Then the two roots of
\[
z^2-2wz+1=0
\]
are
\[
w\pm S(w).
\]


::: pf-proof

The quadratic formula gives the two formal roots. The slit plane
\[
\CC\setminus[-1,1]
\]
admits a holomorphic branch of the square root of $w^2-1$, uniquely
specified by the asymptotic condition at infinity. Substitution verifies
the roots.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

The branch
\[
w-S(w)
\]
is the root in the punctured unit disk.


::: pf-proof

As $w\to\infty$,
\[
S(w)
=
w\sqrt{1-\frac1{w^2}}
=
w-\frac1{2w}+O(w^{-3}),
\]
so
\[
w-S(w)
=
\frac1{2w}+O(w^{-3})
\longrightarrow0.
\]
Hence this root lies inside the unit disk for all sufficiently large $w$.
By step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}, the two roots never cross the unit circle as $w$ varies in the
connected domain
\[
\CC\setminus[-1,1].
\]
Therefore the same branch is the inside root throughout the domain.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

Thus the inverse map in part (c) is
\[
\boxed{
f^{-1}(w)
=
w-\sqrt{w^2-1}},
\]
where the square root is the branch specified in step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref}.


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref}, the displayed value solves
\[
f(z)=w.
\]
By step [](#s1-11){.pf-ref}, it lies in
\[
\DD\setminus\{0\},
\]
and step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} gives uniqueness there.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

In fact,
\[
f(\DD\setminus\{0\})
=
\CC\setminus[-1,1]
\]
as well.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} provides exactly one root in the punctured disk for every
$w\notin[-1,1]$, while step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref} excludes values in the segment.

:::

Steps [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} prove part (a), steps [](#s1-5){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-9){.pf-ref} prove part
(b), and steps [](#s1-10){.pf-ref} through [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} give the inverse requested in part (c).
