::: {.problem}
Let $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be $C^1$ and assume the improper integral
\[
\int_1^\infty |f'(x)|\,dx
\]
converges.
Prove that
\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n)
\]
converges if and only if
\[
\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx
\]
converges.
:::

::: {.solution}
For $n\ge1$, define
$$
d_n\coloneqq
f(n)-\int_n^{n+1}f(x)\,dx.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

For every $n\ge1$,
$$
\abs{d_n}
\le
\int_n^{n+1}\abs{f'(t)}\,dt.
$$

::: pf-proof

One has
$$
\begin{aligned}
d_n
&=\int_n^{n+1}\bigl(f(n)-f(x)\bigr)\,dx\\
&=-\int_n^{n+1}\int_n^x f'(t)\,dt\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\abs{d_n}
&\le
\int_n^{n+1}\int_n^x\abs{f'(t)}\,dt\,dx\\
&\le
\int_n^{n+1}\abs{f'(t)}\,dt,
\end{aligned}
$$
because the inner integration region has length at most $1$ in the $x$-direction.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

The series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_n
$$
converges absolutely.

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1){.pf-ref},
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\abs{d_n}
\le
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\int_n^{n+1}\abs{f'(t)}\,dt
=
\int_1^{\infty}\abs{f'(t)}\,dt
<\infty.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

For every positive integer $N$,
$$
\sum_{n=1}^N f(n)
-
\int_1^{N+1}f(x)\,dx
=
\sum_{n=1}^N d_n.
$$

::: pf-proof

By definition of $d_n$,
$$
f(n)
=
d_n+\int_n^{n+1}f(x)\,dx.
$$
Summing from $n=1$ to $N$ makes the integrals telescope over adjacent intervals to $\int_1^{N+1}f$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

If the improper integral
$$
\int_1^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
$$
converges, then
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)
$$
converges.

::: pf-proof

Convergence of the improper integral implies that
$$
\int_1^{N+1}f(x)\,dx
$$
has a finite limit as $N\to\infty$. By step [](#s2){.pf-ref}, the right-hand side of step [](#s3){.pf-ref} also has a finite limit. Hence the partial sums $\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n)$ converge.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

Suppose instead that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)
$$
converges. Then the sequence
$$
\int_1^{N+1}f(x)\,dx
$$
converges as $N\to\infty$.

::: pf-proof

By hypothesis, the first term on the left-hand side of step [](#s3){.pf-ref} has a finite limit. By step [](#s2){.pf-ref}, the right-hand side has a finite limit. Rearranging step [](#s3){.pf-ref} therefore shows that the displayed sequence of integrals has a finite limit.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

Under the hypothesis of step [](#s5){.pf-ref},
$$
\sup_{N\le x\le N+1}\abs{f(x)}\longrightarrow0.
$$

::: pf-proof

Convergence of the series implies $f(N)\to0$. For $x\in[N,N+1]$,
$$
\abs{f(x)}
\le
\abs{f(N)}+\int_N^{N+1}\abs{f'(t)}\,dt.
$$
The first term tends to $0$, and the second tends to $0$ because it is bounded by the tail
$$
\int_N^{\infty}\abs{f'(t)}\,dt.
$$
The bound is uniform in $x\in[N,N+1]$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s7}

Under the hypothesis of step [](#s5){.pf-ref}, the improper integral
$$
\int_1^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
$$
converges.

::: pf-proof

Let $R\to\infty$ and choose the integer $N$ with
$$
N\le R<N+1.
$$
Then
$$
\int_1^R f(x)\,dx
=
\int_1^N f(x)\,dx
+\int_N^R f(x)\,dx.
$$
The first term has a finite limit by step [](#s5){.pf-ref}, with the index shifted by one. The second satisfies
$$
\left|\int_N^R f(x)\,dx\right|
\le
\sup_{N\le x\le N+1}\abs{f(x)},
$$
which tends to $0$ by step [](#s6){.pf-ref}. Thus the full improper integral converges.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s8}

Therefore
$$
\boxed{
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)\text{ converges}
\iff
\int_1^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\text{ converges}}.
$$

::: pf-proof

Step [](#s4){.pf-ref} proves one implication, and step [](#s7){.pf-ref} proves the converse.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s8){.pf-ref} is the required equivalence.

:::
