::: {.problem}
Let $A$ be a real $n\times n$ matrix.

1. Must $A$ commute with $A^T$?
2. If the columns of $A$ form an orthonormal set, must the rows also form an orthonormal set?
:::

::: {.solution}
::: pf

::: {.pf-step #counterexample}
A real square matrix need not commute with its transpose.

::: pf-proof
Take
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then
$$
AA^T
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix},
$$
whereas
$$
A^TA
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus
$$
AA^T\neq A^TA.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #orthonormal-columns}
If the columns of $A$ are orthonormal, then
$$
A^TA=I.
$$

::: pf-proof
Let the columns of $A$ be
$$
v_1,\ldots,v_n.
$$
The $(i,j)$ entry of $A^TA$ is
$$
v_i^Tv_j.
$$
Orthonormality gives
$$
v_i^Tv_j=\delta_{ij},
$$
so $A^TA=I$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #transpose-is-inverse}
Under the hypothesis of [](#orthonormal-columns){.pf-ref},
$$
A^{-1}=A^T.
$$

::: pf-proof
The equation
$$
A^TA=I
$$
shows that $A^T$ is a left inverse of the square matrix $A$. Hence $A$ is injective as a linear map $\RR^n\to\RR^n$, and therefore invertible. The left inverse of an invertible matrix is its inverse, so
$$
A^T=A^{-1}.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #orthonormal-rows}
The rows of $A$ are orthonormal.

::: pf-proof
By [](#transpose-is-inverse){.pf-ref},
$$
AA^T=AA^{-1}=I.
$$
If the rows of $A$ are
$$
r_1,\ldots,r_n,
$$
then the $(i,j)$ entry of $AA^T$ is
$$
r_ir_j^T.
$$
Thus
$$
r_ir_j^T=\delta_{ij},
$$
which says exactly that the rows form an orthonormal set.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Therefore the answers are
$$
\boxed{\text{1. No.}\qquad\text{2. Yes.}}
$$

::: pf-proof
[](#counterexample){.pf-ref} gives the counterexample for part 1, and [](#orthonormal-columns){.pf-ref}, [](#transpose-is-inverse){.pf-ref}, and [](#orthonormal-rows){.pf-ref} prove part 2.
:::
