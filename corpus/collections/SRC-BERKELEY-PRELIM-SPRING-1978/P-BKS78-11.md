::: {.problem}
Let $U=\{z\in\CC:\abs{z}>4\}$.

1. Show that there is a holomorphic function on $U$ whose derivative is
   $$
   \frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)}.
   $$
2. Is there a holomorphic function on $U$ whose derivative is
   $$
   \frac{z^2}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)}?
   $$
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
$$
r_1(z)\coloneqq
\frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #taylor-expansion}
The function
$$
H(w)\coloneqq
\frac1{(1-w)(1-2w)(1-3w)}
$$
has a Taylor expansion
$$
H(w)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_mw^m
$$
for $\abs{w}<1/3$.

::: pf-proof
The nearest singularity of $H$ to $0$ is at $w=1/3$, so $H$ is holomorphic on the disk $\abs{w}<1/3$. Every holomorphic function has a Taylor expansion throughout the largest disk centered at the expansion point containing no singularity.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #laurent-expansion}
For $\abs{z}>3$,
$$
r_1(z)
=
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_mz^{-m-2}.
$$

::: pf-proof
Factor $z^3$ from the denominator:
$$
\begin{aligned}
r_1(z)
&=
\frac{z}
{z^3(1-z^{-1})(1-2z^{-1})(1-3z^{-1})}\\
&=
z^{-2}H(z^{-1}).
\end{aligned}
$$
If $\abs{z}>3$, then $\abs{z^{-1}}<1/3$, so [](#taylor-expansion){.pf-ref} applies and gives the displayed Laurent series.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The function
$$
\boxed{
F(z)
\coloneqq
-
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
\frac{c_m}{m+1}z^{-m-1}
}
$$
is holomorphic on $U$ and satisfies
$$
F'(z)=r_1(z).
$$

::: pf-proof
The series in [](#laurent-expansion){.pf-ref} is a Laurent series converging on $\abs{z}>3$. Its termwise antiderivative displayed above converges normally on every compact subset of $\abs{z}>3$, and hence defines a holomorphic function there. Termwise differentiation gives
$$
F'(z)
=
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_mz^{-m-2}
=
r_1(z)
$$
by [](#laurent-expansion){.pf-ref}. Since $U\subset\{\abs{z}>3\}$, this proves part 1.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #residues}
Put
$$
r_2(z)\coloneqq
\frac{z^2}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)}.
$$
The residues of $r_2$ at $1,2,3$ are respectively
$$
\frac12,\qquad -4,\qquad \frac92.
$$

::: pf-proof
All three poles are simple. Thus
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=1}r_2
=
\frac{1^2}{(1-2)(1-3)}
=
\frac12,
$$
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=2}r_2
=
\frac{2^2}{(2-1)(2-3)}
=
-4,
$$
and
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=3}r_2
=
\frac{3^2}{(3-1)(3-2)}
=
\frac92.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #nonzero-integral}
For every $R>4$,
$$
\int_{\abs{z}=R}r_2(z)\,dz=2\pi i.
$$

::: pf-proof
The positively oriented circle $\abs{z}=R$ encloses all three poles. By the residue theorem and [](#residues){.pf-ref},
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\abs{z}=R}r_2(z)\,dz
&=
2\pi i
\left(
\frac12-4+\frac92
\right)\\
&=
2\pi i.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #no-primitive}
There is no holomorphic function $G$ on $U$ with
$$
G'=r_2.
$$

::: pf-proof
Suppose such a function existed. For any $R>4$, the circle
$$
\gamma(t)=Re^{it},
\qquad
0\leq t\leq2\pi,
$$
lies in $U$. By the chain rule,
$$
\int_\gamma G'(z)\,dz
=
\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{d}{dt}G(\gamma(t))\,dt
=
G(\gamma(2\pi))-G(\gamma(0))
=0.
$$
This contradicts [](#nonzero-integral){.pf-ref}.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Therefore the answer to part 2 is
$$
\boxed{\text{No}.}
$$

::: pf-proof
This is exactly [](#no-primitive){.pf-ref}.
:::
