::: {.problem}
Consider
\[
3x+y-z+u^4=0,
\qquad
x-y+2z+u=0,
\qquad
2x+2y-3z+2u=0.
\]

1. Prove that for some $\varepsilon>0$ the system can be solved for $(x,y,u)$ as functions of $z\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$, with $x(0)=y(0)=u(0)=0$. Are these functions continuous, differentiable, and unique?
2. Show that the system cannot be solved for $(x,y,z)$ as functions of $u\in[-\delta,\delta]$ for every $\delta>0$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Define $F:\RR^4\to\RR^3$ by
$$
F(x,y,z,u)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
3x+y-z+u^4\\
x-y+2z+u\\
2x+2y-3z+2u
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #invertible-jacobian}
One has $F(0,0,0,0)=0$, and the derivative of $F$ with respect to $(x,y,u)$ at the origin is invertible.

::: pf-proof
The first assertion follows by substitution. The relevant Jacobian is
$$
D_{(x,y,u)}F(0,0,0,0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&1&0\\
1&-1&1\\
2&2&2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its determinant is
$$
3((-1)2-2)-\bigl(2-2\cdot2\bigr)
=-12+2
=-10\neq0.
$$
Thus the matrix is invertible.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #implicit-functions}
There is an $\varepsilon>0$ and unique smooth functions
$$
x,y,u:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\RR
$$
near the zero solution such that
$$
F(x(z),y(z),z,u(z))=0
$$
and
$$
x(0)=y(0)=u(0)=0.
$$

::: pf-proof
The map $F$ is polynomial and hence smooth. [](#invertible-jacobian){.pf-ref} verifies the invertibility hypothesis of the implicit function theorem for the dependent variables $(x,y,u)$ and independent variable $z$. The theorem therefore supplies a unique smooth local solution map
$$
z\longmapsto(x(z),y(z),u(z))
$$
through the origin. Shrinking its domain if necessary gives an interval $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The functions in Part 1 are continuous and differentiable, and the local solution through the origin is unique.

::: pf-proof
By [](#implicit-functions){.pf-ref} the functions are smooth, so in particular they are continuous and differentiable. The uniqueness clause is also part of the implicit function theorem: after restricting to sufficiently small neighborhoods of the origin, every solution of the system is given by this solution map.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #necessary-condition}
Every solution $(x,y,z,u)$ of the three equations satisfies
$$
u(3-u^3)=0.
$$

::: pf-proof
Subtract the first equation from the sum of the second and third equations. The coefficients of $x,y,z$ cancel:
$$
-(3x+y-z)+(x-y+2z)+(2x+2y-3z)=0.
$$
The remaining terms give
$$
-u^4+u+2u=0,
$$
equivalently
$$
u(3-u^3)=0.
$$
:::

:::

::: pf-step
For every $\delta>0$, the system cannot be solved for $(x,y,z)$ as functions of every $u\in[-\delta,\delta]$.

::: pf-proof
Fix $\delta>0$. Choose a nonzero $u$ satisfying
$$
|u|<\min\{\delta,\sqrt[3]{3}\}.
$$
Then $u\neq0$ and $u^3\neq3$, so
$$
u(3-u^3)\neq0.
$$
By [](#necessary-condition){.pf-ref} there is no triple $(x,y,z)$ at all satisfying the system for this value of $u$. Hence no functions $(x(u),y(u),z(u))$ can solve the system for every $u\in[-\delta,\delta]$.
:::
