::: {.problem}
Let $f(x)$ be Lebesgue measurable on $\RR$.
Suppose that $f(x)$ and $xf(x)$ are both integrable over the whole of $\RR$.
Define
$$
F(t):=\int_{\RR}f(x)\sin(tx)\,dx.
$$
Prove that $F(t)$ is differentiable at every $t$, and find a formula for $F'(t)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this problem — the collection's provenance is the UGA real-analysis qualifying examination paper, Spring 2010 — so the argument is the card's own.

Throughout, $f$ is Lebesgue measurable on $\RR$ with $f\in L^{1}(\RR)$ and $xf(x)\in L^{1}(\RR)$, $t\in\RR$ is fixed, and $h\neq0$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #welldefined}
$F(t)$ is defined by an absolutely convergent integral for every $t$, and $\abs{F(t)}\le\int_{\RR}\abs f$.

::: pf-proof
For every $x$, $\abs{f(x)\sin(tx)}\le\abs{f(x)}$ because $\abs{\sin}\le1$, and $\abs f$ is integrable by hypothesis; so $x\mapsto f(x)\sin(tx)$ is integrable and the estimate on $\abs{F(t)}$ follows by monotonicity of the integral.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #quotient}
For $h\neq0$, $\frac{F(t+h)-F(t)}{h}=\int_{\RR}f(x)\frac{\sin((t+h)x)-\sin(tx)}{h}\,dx$.

::: pf-proof
Both $F(t+h)$ and $F(t)$ are finite by step [](#welldefined){.pf-ref}, and the integrand of the right-hand side is measurable. It is also integrable: by the mean value theorem applied to $\sin$ on the interval with endpoints $tx$ and $(t+h)x$ there is $\xi$ between them with $\sin((t+h)x)-\sin(tx)=\cos(\xi)\,hx$, so $\big|\frac{\sin((t+h)x)-\sin(tx)}{h}\big|=\abs{x}\abs{\cos\xi}\le\abs x$ and the integrand is dominated by $\abs{xf(x)}$, which is integrable. Linearity of the integral over integrable functions then gives the displayed identity.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #pointwise}
For each fixed $x$, $\frac{\sin((t+h)x)-\sin(tx)}{h}\to x\cos(tx)$ as $h\to0$.

::: pf-proof
Write $\sin((t+h)x)-\sin(tx)=\sin(tx+hx)-\sin(tx)$ and use the differentiability of $\sin$ at $tx$: $\frac{\sin(tx+hx)-\sin(tx)}{hx}\to\cos(tx)$ as $hx\to0$, and multiplying by $x$ gives the claim. For $x=0$ both sides are $0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #dominated}
For all $x$ and all $h\neq0$, $\big|f(x)\frac{\sin((t+h)x)-\sin(tx)}{h}\big|\le\abs{x f(x)}$, and $xf\in L^{1}(\RR)$.

::: pf-proof
The bound on the quotient is the mean value estimate obtained in step [](#quotient){.pf-ref}, valid for every $h\neq0$ with no dependence on $h$; multiplying by $\abs{f(x)}$ gives the display. The function $xf(x)$ is integrable by hypothesis.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #derivative}
$F$ is differentiable at $t$ with $F'(t)=\int_{\RR}xf(x)\cos(tx)\,dx$.

::: pf-proof
By steps [](#quotient){.pf-ref}, [](#pointwise){.pf-ref} and [](#dominated){.pf-ref} the difference quotients are integrable functions converging pointwise to $xf(x)\cos(tx)$ and dominated in absolute value by the fixed integrable function $\abs{xf(x)}$. The dominated convergence theorem then gives
$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{F(t+h)-F(t)}{h}=\int_{\RR}xf(x)\cos(tx)\,dx$,
so the limit of the difference quotients exists and equals that integral, which is the definition of $F'(t)$. The integral is finite because $\abs{xf(x)\cos(tx)}\le\abs{xf(x)}$ and $xf\in L^{1}(\RR)$. Since $t$ was arbitrary, $F$ is differentiable at every $t$, with the displayed formula.
:::
