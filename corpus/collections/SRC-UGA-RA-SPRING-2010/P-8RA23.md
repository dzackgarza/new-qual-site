::: problem
Prove that there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
\cos(\sin x)>\frac12\bigl(1+\cos^2 x\bigr)
\]
whenever $0<|x|<\varepsilon$.
:::

::: solution
Define
\[
h(x):=\cos(\sin x)-\frac12\bigl(1+\cos^2x\bigr).
\]
The function $h$ is smooth. Direct differentiation at $0$ gives
\[
h(0)=h'(0)=h''(0)=h'''(0)=0,
\qquad
h^{(4)}(0)=1.
\]
Since $h^{(4)}$ is continuous, there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
h^{(4)}(t)>0
\qquad\text{whenever }|t|<\varepsilon.
\]

Fix $x$ with $0<|x|<\varepsilon$. Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder, expanded at $0$ to order three, gives some $\xi$ between $0$ and $x$ such that
\[
h(x)
=\frac{h^{(4)}(\xi)}{4!}x^4.
\]
Because $|\xi|<\varepsilon$, we have $h^{(4)}(\xi)>0$, and because $x\ne0$, $x^4>0$. Hence
\[
h(x)>0.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{
\cos(\sin x)>rac12\bigl(1+\cos^2x\bigr)
}
\]
for all sufficiently small nonzero $x$.
:::
