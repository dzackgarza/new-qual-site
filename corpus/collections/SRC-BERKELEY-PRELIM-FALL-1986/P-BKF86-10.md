::: {.problem}
For $f$ a real-valued function on the real line, define the function $\Delta f$ by $\Delta f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)$.
For $n\ge2$, define $\Delta^n f$ recursively by $\Delta^n f=\Delta(\Delta^{n-1}f)$.
Prove that $\Delta^n f=0$ if and only if $f$ has the form
\[
f(x)=a_0(x)+a_1(x)x+\cdots+a_{n-1}(x)x^{n-1},
\]
where $a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$ are periodic functions of period $1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
For $k\geq0$, write
$$
B_k(x)=\binom{x}{k},
$$
where $B_0(x)=1$ and
$$
B_k(x)=\frac{x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)}{k!}
$$
for $k\geq1$.

<1>1. If $a$ is periodic of period $1$, then
$$
\Delta\bigl(aB_0\bigr)=0
$$
and, for every $k\geq1$,
$$
\Delta\bigl(aB_k\bigr)=aB_{k-1}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since $a(x+1)=a(x)$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\bigl(aB_k\bigr)(x)
&=
a(x+1)B_k(x+1)-a(x)B_k(x)\\
&=
a(x)\bigl(B_k(x+1)-B_k(x)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
For $k=0$, the last difference is $0$. For $k\geq1$, Pascal's polynomial identity gives
$$
B_k(x+1)-B_k(x)=B_{k-1}(x).
$$
This proves both formulas.
:::

<1>2. If
$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k(x)B_k(x)
$$
with each $a_k$ periodic of period $1$, then $\Delta^n f=0$.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1, each application of $\Delta$ lowers the index of every nonzero binomial term by one and annihilates the $B_0$ term. Hence
$$
\Delta^{k+1}\bigl(a_kB_k\bigr)=0
$$
for every $0\leq k\leq n-1$. Since $k+1\leq n$, one has
$$
\Delta^n\bigl(a_kB_k\bigr)=0
$$
for every term. Linearity of $\Delta^n$ therefore gives $\Delta^n f=0$.
:::

<1>3. Conversely, if $\Delta^n f=0$, then there are period-one functions $a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}$ such that
$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k(x)B_k(x).
$$

::: {.proof}
Proceed by induction on $n$, beginning with the auxiliary case $n=1$.

If $\Delta f=0$, then
$$
f(x+1)=f(x)
$$
for every $x$, so $f$ itself is period one and
$$
f(x)=f(x)B_0(x).
$$

Now suppose the assertion holds for $n-1$, and assume $\Delta^n f=0$. Put
$$
g=\Delta f.
$$
Then
$$
\Delta^{n-1}g=0.
$$
By the induction hypothesis, there are period-one functions $b_0,\ldots,b_{n-2}$ such that
$$
g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}b_k(x)B_k(x).
$$
Define
$$
h(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}b_k(x)B_{k+1}(x).
$$
Step <1>1 gives
$$
\Delta h=g=\Delta f.
$$
Therefore
$$
\Delta(f-h)=0,
$$
so
$$
a_0=f-h
$$
is period one. Taking
$$
a_{k+1}=b_k
$$
for $0\leq k\leq n-2$ yields
$$
f(x)=a_0(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}a_{k+1}(x)B_{k+1}(x),
$$
which is the required representation.
:::

<1>4. A representation
$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k(x)B_k(x)
$$
with period-one coefficients exists if and only if a representation
$$
f(x)=a_0'(x)+a_1'(x)x+\cdots+a_{n-1}'(x)x^{n-1}
$$
with period-one coefficients exists.

::: {.proof}
The polynomials
$$
B_0(x),B_1(x),\ldots,B_{n-1}(x)
$$
form a basis of the real polynomials of degree less than $n$: each $B_k$ has degree $k$ and leading coefficient $1/k!$, so the change-of-basis matrix from $(B_0,\ldots,B_{n-1})$ to $(1,x,\ldots,x^{n-1})$ is triangular with nonzero diagonal.

Consequently each $B_k$ is a constant linear combination of $1,x,\ldots,x^k$, and each $x^k$ is a constant linear combination of $B_0,\ldots,B_k$. Passing from either representation to the other therefore replaces the coefficient functions only by constant linear combinations of them. Constant linear combinations of period-one functions are again period one.
:::

<1>5. The stated characterization holds:
$$
\Delta^n f=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(x)=a_0(x)+a_1(x)x+\cdots+a_{n-1}(x)x^{n-1}
$$
for period-one functions $a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}$.

::: {.proof}
If $\Delta^n f=0$, step <1>3 gives a binomial-basis representation and step <1>4 converts it to the power-basis representation in the statement. Conversely, a power-basis representation converts by step <1>4 to a binomial-basis representation, and step <1>2 then gives $\Delta^n f=0$.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 proves both implications.
:::
