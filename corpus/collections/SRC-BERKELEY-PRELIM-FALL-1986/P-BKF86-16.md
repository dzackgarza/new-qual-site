::: {.problem}
Discuss the solvability of the differential equation
\[
(e^x\sin y)(y')^3+(e^x\cos y)y'+e^y\tan x=0
\]
with the initial condition $y(0)=0$.
Does a solution exist in some interval about $0$?
If so, is it unique?
:::

::: {.solution}
Define
$$
F(x,y,p)
=
e^x\sin y\,p^3+e^x\cos y\,p+e^y\tan x.
$$

<1>1. Any classical solution with $y(0)=0$ must satisfy
$$
y'(0)=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
At $x=0$ and $y=0$,
$$
F(0,0,p)=p.
$$
Thus the differential equation $F(x,y,y')=0$ at the initial point gives
$$
0=F(0,0,y'(0))=y'(0).
$$
:::

<1>2. Near $(x,y,p)=(0,0,0)$, the equation $F(x,y,p)=0$ is equivalent to an explicit equation
$$
p=\phi(x,y)
$$
for a uniquely determined smooth function $\phi$ with $\phi(0,0)=0$.

::: {.proof}
The function $F$ is smooth in a neighborhood of $(0,0,0)$, since $\tan x$ is smooth near $x=0$. Moreover,
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}(x,y,p)
=
3e^x\sin y\,p^2+e^x\cos y,
$$
so
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}(0,0,0)=1\neq0.
$$
The implicit-function theorem therefore gives neighborhoods of $(0,0)$ and $0$ and a unique smooth function $\phi$ on the first neighborhood such that, within these neighborhoods,
$$
F(x,y,p)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p=\phi(x,y).
$$
Since $F(0,0,0)=0$, one has $\phi(0,0)=0$.
:::

<1>3. There exists a solution of the original differential equation on some interval about $0$ with $y(0)=0$.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>2, it is enough near the initial point to solve
$$
y'=\phi(x,y),
\qquad
y(0)=0.
$$
The function $\phi$ is smooth, hence continuous and locally Lipschitz in $y$. The local existence theorem for first-order ordinary differential equations therefore gives a solution on some interval $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$ about $0$. By construction, this solution also satisfies $F(x,y,y')=0$, which is the original equation.
:::

<1>4. The solution in step <1>3 is locally unique among classical solutions satisfying $y(0)=0$.

::: {.proof}
Let $\widetilde y$ be any classical solution of the original equation with $\widetilde y(0)=0$. By step <1>1,
$$
\widetilde y'(0)=0.
$$
Since $\widetilde y$ and $\widetilde y'$ are continuous, after shrinking the interval if necessary the triple
$$
(x,\widetilde y(x),\widetilde y'(x))
$$
stays in the neighborhood where step <1>2 applies. Hence
$$
\widetilde y'(x)=\phi(x,\widetilde y(x)).
$$
The local uniqueness theorem for
$$
y'=\phi(x,y),
\qquad
y(0)=0
$$
then implies that $\widetilde y$ agrees with the solution from step <1>3 on a neighborhood of $0$.
:::

<1>5. Therefore the initial-value problem has
$$
\boxed{\text{a locally existing and locally unique solution about }0}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Existence is step <1>3 and uniqueness is step <1>4.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 answers both questions in the problem.
:::
:::
