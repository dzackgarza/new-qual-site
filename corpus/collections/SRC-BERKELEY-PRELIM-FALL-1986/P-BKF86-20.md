::: {.problem}
Let $f$ be a real-valued continuous function on $[0,\infty)$ such that
\[
\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(f(x)+\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\right)
\]
exists.
Prove that
\[
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
Define
$$
F(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt
$$
and
$$
g(x)=f(x)+F(x).
$$
Let
$$
L=\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x).
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

The function $F$ satisfies
$$
F'(x)+F(x)=g(x),
\qquad
F(0)=0.
$$

::: pf-proof

Since $f$ is continuous, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
$$
F'(x)=f(x).
$$
Therefore the definition of $g$ gives
$$
F'(x)+F(x)=f(x)+F(x)=g(x),
$$
and the defining integral gives $F(0)=0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

For every $x\geq0$,
$$
F(x)=e^{-x}\int_0^x e^t g(t)\,dt.
$$

::: pf-proof

Multiplying the equation in step [](#s1){.pf-ref} by $e^x$ gives
$$
\bigl(e^xF(x)\bigr)'=e^xg(x).
$$
Integrating from $0$ to $x$ and using $F(0)=0$ yields
$$
e^xF(x)=\int_0^x e^t g(t)\,dt,
$$
which is the asserted formula.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

One has
$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=L.
$$

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s2){.pf-ref},
$$
\begin{aligned}
F(x)-L
&=
e^{-x}\int_0^x e^t\bigl(g(t)-L\bigr)\,dt
-Le^{-x}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $g(t)\to L$, choose $T$ such that
$$
\abs{g(t)-L}<\varepsilon
$$
for all $t\geq T$. For $x\geq T$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\abs{F(x)-L}
&\leq
e^{-x}\int_0^T e^t\abs{g(t)-L}\,dt
+
e^{-x}\int_T^x e^t\varepsilon\,dt
+
\abs L e^{-x}\\
&\leq
C_Te^{-x}+\varepsilon+\abs L e^{-x},
\end{aligned}
$$
where
$$
C_T=\int_0^T e^t\abs{g(t)-L}\,dt<\infty.
$$
Taking the upper limit as $x\to\infty$ gives
$$
\limsup_{x\to\infty}\abs{F(x)-L}\leq\varepsilon.
$$
Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $F(x)\to L$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

One has
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0}.
$$

::: pf-proof

By definition,
$$
f(x)=g(x)-F(x).
$$
The hypothesis gives $g(x)\to L$, and step [](#s3){.pf-ref} gives $F(x)\to L$. Therefore
$$
f(x)\longrightarrow L-L=0.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s4){.pf-ref} is the required conclusion.

:::
