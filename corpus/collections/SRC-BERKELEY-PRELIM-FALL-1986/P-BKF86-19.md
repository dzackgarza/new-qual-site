::: {.problem}
Prove that if six people are riding together in an Evans Hall elevator, there is either a three-person subset of mutual friends (each knows the other two) or a three-person subset of mutual strangers (each knows neither of the other two).
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Among any five people distinct from a fixed person $P$, there are three who are all friends of $P$, or three who are all strangers to $P$.

::: {.proof}
Each of the other five people is either a friend of $P$ or a stranger to $P$. If at most two were friends and at most two were strangers, there would be at most four other people. Hence at least three belong to one of the two classes.
:::

<1>2. If $P$ has three friends $A,B,C$, then the six people contain either three mutual friends or three mutual strangers.

::: {.proof}
If some two of $A,B,C$, say $A$ and $B$, are friends, then
$$
\{P,A,B\}
$$
are three mutual friends, since both $A$ and $B$ are friends of $P$.

Otherwise no two of $A,B,C$ are friends. Then each pair among $A,B,C$ consists of strangers, so
$$
\{A,B,C\}
$$
are three mutual strangers.
:::

<1>3. If $P$ has three strangers $A,B,C$, then the six people contain either three mutual friends or three mutual strangers.

::: {.proof}
If some two of $A,B,C$, say $A$ and $B$, are strangers, then
$$
\{P,A,B\}
$$
are three mutual strangers, since both $A$ and $B$ are strangers to $P$.

Otherwise no two of $A,B,C$ are strangers. Then each pair among $A,B,C$ consists of friends, so
$$
\{A,B,C\}
$$
are three mutual friends.
:::

<1>4. Every set of six people contains three mutual friends or three mutual strangers.

::: {.proof}
Fix any one of the six people as $P$. By step <1>1, either $P$ has three friends among the other five or $P$ has three strangers among them. Apply step <1>2 in the first case and step <1>3 in the second.
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 is exactly the required conclusion.
:::
