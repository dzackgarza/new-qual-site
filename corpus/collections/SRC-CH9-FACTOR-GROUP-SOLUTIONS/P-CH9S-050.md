::: {.problem}
If $\cardinality{G} = pq$ where $p$ and $q$ are primes that are not necessarily distinct, prove that $\cardinality{Z(G)} = 1$ or $pq$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

If $Z(G)$ is nontrivial and proper, then $G/Z(G)$ has prime order and is cyclic.


::: pf-proof

Assume
$$
1<\cardinality{Z(G)}<pq.
$$
By Lagrange's theorem, $\cardinality{Z(G)}$ is a proper nontrivial divisor of
$pq$. Since $p$ and $q$ are prime, even when $p=q$ this forces
$\cardinality{Z(G)}$ to be one of the prime factors of $pq$. Therefore
$$
\cardinality{G/Z(G)}=\frac{pq}{\cardinality{Z(G)}}
$$
is prime. Every group of prime order is cyclic, so $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is Abelian.


::: pf-proof

Suppose
$$
G/Z(G)=\langle gZ(G)\rangle.
$$
For arbitrary $x,y\in G$, there exist integers $m,n$ and elements
$z_1,z_2\in Z(G)$ such that
$$
x=g^m z_1,
\qquad
y=g^n z_2.
$$
Since $z_1$ and $z_2$ are central,
$$
xy=g^{m+n}z_1z_2=g^{n+m}z_2z_1=yx.
$$
Hence $G$ is Abelian.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

The center cannot be both nontrivial and proper.


::: pf-proof

If it were, step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref} would make $G/Z(G)$ cyclic, and step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref}
would make $G$ Abelian. But an Abelian group satisfies $Z(G)=G$,
contradicting the assumption that $Z(G)$ is proper.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

Thus
$$
\cardinality{Z(G)}\in\boxed{\{1,pq\}}.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}, either $Z(G)=\{e\}$ or $Z(G)=G$. These alternatives have
orders $1$ and $pq$, respectively.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref} gives exactly the two possible orders required.

:::
