::: {.problem}
Let
\[
E=\left\{\frac{(-1)^n n}{n+1}:n\in\mathbb N\right\}.
\]

(a) Find the limit points of $E$.

(b) Is $E$ closed? Is it open?

(c) Does $E$ contain isolated points?

(d) Find the closure $\overline E$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Write
$$
x_n=\frac{(-1)^n n}{n+1}.
$$

<1>1. The even-indexed terms converge to $1$:
$$
x_{2k}=\frac{2k}{2k+1}\longrightarrow1.
$$

::: {.proof}
One has
$$
1-x_{2k}
=
\frac{1}{2k+1}
\longrightarrow0.
$$
:::

<1>2. The odd-indexed terms converge to $-1$:
$$
x_{2k-1}
=
-\frac{2k-1}{2k}
\longrightarrow-1.
$$

::: {.proof}
One has
$$
x_{2k-1}+1
=
\frac1{2k}
\longrightarrow0.
$$
:::

<1>3. Both $1$ and $-1$ are limit points of $E$.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>2 exhibit sequences of distinct points of $E$ converging to
$1$ and $-1$, respectively.
:::

<1>4. No point other than $1$ and $-1$ is a limit point of $E$.

::: {.proof}
Let $y$ be a limit point of $E$. Recursively choose distinct points
$$
e_j\in E\setminus\{y\}
$$
such that
$$
|e_j-y|<\frac1j,
$$
which is possible because every punctured neighborhood of a limit point
contains infinitely many points of $E$. Write $e_j=x_{n_j}$. Since the points
$e_j$ are distinct, the indices $n_j$ are distinct and therefore unbounded.
Passing to a subsequence if necessary, arrange that $n_j$ is strictly
increasing. Thus
$$
e_j=x_{n_j}\longrightarrow y
$$
with $n_j\to\infty$.

Among the integers $n_j$, at least one parity occurs infinitely often. Passing
to that parity subsequence, step <1>1 or step <1>2 shows that the resulting
subsequence converges to $1$ or $-1$. Since every subsequence of a convergent
sequence has the same limit $y$, one must have
$$
y\in\{-1,1\}.
$$
:::

<1>5. The set of limit points of $E$ is
$$
\boxed{\{-1,1\}}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>3 gives both points, and step <1>4 excludes all others.
:::

<1>6. Part (a) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>5.
:::

<1>7. Neither $1$ nor $-1$ belongs to $E$.

::: {.proof}
For every positive integer $n$,
$$
\left|\frac{(-1)^n n}{n+1}\right|
=
\frac{n}{n+1}
<1.
$$
Thus no element of $E$ equals $1$ or $-1$.
:::

<1>8. The set $E$ is not closed.

::: {.proof}
A closed subset of $\RR$ contains all of its limit points. By step <1>5,
$1$ and $-1$ are limit points of $E$, while step <1>7 shows that neither
belongs to $E$. Hence $E$ is not closed.
:::

<1>9. The set $E$ is not open.

::: {.proof}
Fix any $x\in E$. Every open interval about $x$ contains uncountably many real
numbers, whereas $E$ is countable because it is the image of $\NN$. Therefore
no open interval about $x$ can be contained in $E$. Thus $x$ is not an
interior point of $E$, and $E$ is not open.
:::

<1>10. Part (b) follows.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>8 and <1>9 show that $E$ is neither closed nor open.
:::

<1>11. Every point of $E$ is isolated.

::: {.proof}
Fix $x_n\in E$. Suppose $x_n$ were not isolated. Then for each $j$ one could
choose
$$
x_{m_j}\in E\setminus\{x_n\}
$$
with
$$
|x_{m_j}-x_n|<\frac1j
$$
and with the indices $m_j$ distinct. Hence
$$
x_{m_j}\longrightarrow x_n.
$$
The distinct positive integers $m_j$ are unbounded, so after passing to a
subsequence of one parity, steps <1>1--<1>2 imply convergence to $1$ or $-1$.
Therefore
$$
x_n\in\{-1,1\},
$$
contradicting step <1>7. Hence $x_n$ is isolated.
:::

<1>12. Part (c) follows.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>11 shows that every point of $E$ is isolated.
:::

<1>13. The closure of $E$ is
$$
\boxed{\overline E=E\cup\{-1,1\}}.
$$

::: {.proof}
For any subset of $\RR$, the closure is the union of the set with all of its
limit points. Step <1>5 identifies the limit points as exactly $-1$ and $1$.
:::

<1>14. Part (d) follows.

::: {.proof}
This is step <1>13.
:::

<1>15. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>6, <1>10, <1>12, and <1>14 establish parts (a)--(d).
:::
:::
