::: problem
Exhibit the rational canonical form for

- $A\in M_6(\QQ)$ with minimal polynomial $(x-1)(x^2 + 1)^2$.

- $A\in M_{10}(\QQ)$ with minimal polynomial $(x^2+1)^2(x^3 + 1)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. For a monic polynomial $g$, write $C(g)$ for its companion matrix. A rational canonical form is a block diagonal matrix
\[
\operatorname{diag}(C(d_1),\dots,C(d_t))
\]
where the nonconstant invariant factors satisfy
\[
d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_t,
\]
their degrees sum to the matrix size, and the minimal polynomial is $d_t$.
::: {.proof}
This is the invariant-factor form of the structure theorem for finitely generated torsion $\mathbb Q[x]$-modules.
:::

<1>2. For the $6\times6$ matrix, put
\[
m_1=(x-1)(x^2+1)^2.
\]
Then $\deg m_1=5$, so the invariant factors have total degree $6$ and largest factor $m_1$.
The unique possibility is
\[
d_1=x-1,
\qquad
d_2=m_1.
\]
::: {.proof}
The invariant factors other than $m_1$ must have total degree $1$ and divide $m_1$.
The only monic degree-one divisor of $m_1$ over $\mathbb Q$ is $x-1$.
:::

<1>3. Hence the rational canonical form in the first case is uniquely determined:
\[
\operatorname{diag}\big(C(x-1),C((x-1)(x^2+1)^2)\big).
\]
::: {.proof}
The block sizes are $1$ and $5$, so the total size is $6$, and the largest invariant factor is exactly the prescribed minimal polynomial.
:::

<1>4. For the $10\times10$ matrix, put
\[
m_2=(x^2+1)^2(x^3+1)
=(x^2+1)^2(x+1)(x^2-x+1).
\]
Then $\deg m_2=7$, so the invariant factors preceding $m_2$ must have total degree $3$.
::: {.proof}
The degree sum of all invariant factors equals $10$, while the final invariant factor is $m_2$ of degree $7$.
:::

<1>5. If there is exactly one invariant factor before $m_2$, it must be a degree-three divisor of $m_2$. There are exactly two such divisors:
\[
(x+1)(x^2+1)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(x+1)(x^2-x+1)=x^3+1.
\]
::: {.proof}
The irreducible factors of $m_2$ over $\mathbb Q$ have degrees $1,2,2$, namely $x+1$, $x^2+1$, and $x^2-x+1$. A degree-three divisor must therefore be the degree-one factor times one of the two degree-two factors.
:::

<1>6. There is also a possibility with three invariant factors before $m_2$, namely
\[
x+1,\quad x+1,\quad x+1,\quad m_2.
\]
There is no possibility with exactly two invariant factors before $m_2$.
::: {.proof}
Three positive degrees summing to $3$ must all equal $1$, and $x+1$ is the only degree-one divisor of $m_2$, so the displayed chain works.
If there were exactly two preceding invariant factors, their positive degrees would have to be $1$ and $2$. The first would be $x+1$, but no degree-two divisor of $m_2$ is divisible by $x+1$ because $(x+1)^2\nmid m_2$. Hence no such divisibility chain exists.
:::

<1>7. Therefore the second problem is not uniquely determined by the given data. Its three possible rational canonical forms are
\[
\operatorname{diag}\big(C((x+1)(x^2+1)),C(m_2)\big),
\]
\[
\operatorname{diag}\big(C(x^3+1),C(m_2)\big),
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{diag}\big(C(x+1),C(x+1),C(x+1),C(m_2)\big).
\]
::: {.proof}
By <1>5--<1>6 these are all possible invariant-factor chains. Each has total degree $10$ and largest invariant factor $m_2$, so each has the required size and minimal polynomial.
:::
:::
