::: {.problem}
Let $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ be distinct complex numbers, and let $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ be nonzero complex numbers. Suppose
\[
S_p=\sum_{j=1}^n a_jz_j^p=0
\qquad(p=0,1,\ldots,m-1),
\]
but $S_m\ne0$, where $1\le m\le n-1$.

How many zeros does
\[
f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{a_j}{z-z_j}
\]
have in $\mathbb C$? Why is $m\ge n$ impossible?
:::

::: {.solution}
Define
$$
Q(z)\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^n(z-z_j).
$$

<1>1. The function $f$ can be written
$$
f(z)=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)},
$$
where
$$
P(z)
\coloneqq
\sum_{j=1}^n
a_j
\prod_{k\neq j}(z-z_k).
$$

::: {.proof}
Put the summands
$$
\frac{a_j}{z-z_j}
$$
over the common denominator $Q(z)$.
:::

<1>2. For every $j$,
$$
P(z_j)\neq0.
$$

::: {.proof}
At $z=z_j$, every term in the sum defining $P$ vanishes except the
$j$th term. Thus
$$
P(z_j)
=
a_j
\prod_{k\neq j}(z_j-z_k).
$$
The coefficient $a_j$ is nonzero and the numbers $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ are
distinct, so every factor in the product is nonzero.
:::

<1>3. For all sufficiently large $\abs z$,
$$
f(z)
=
\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}
S_pz^{-p-1}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Choose $\abs z>\max_j\abs{z_j}$. Then
$$
\frac1{z-z_j}
=
\frac1z
\frac1{1-z_j/z}
=
\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}
z_j^p z^{-p-1}.
$$
Summing over $j$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)
&=
\sum_{j=1}^n
a_j
\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}
z_j^p z^{-p-1}\\
&=
\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}
\left(
\sum_{j=1}^n a_jz_j^p
\right)
z^{-p-1}\\
&=
\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}
S_pz^{-p-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The finite sum over $j$ permits termwise rearrangement.
:::

<1>4. The hypotheses on the moments imply
$$
f(z)
=
S_mz^{-m-1}
+O(z^{-m-2})
$$
as $z\to\infty$.

::: {.proof}
By assumption,
$$
S_0=\cdots=S_{m-1}=0
$$
and
$$
S_m\neq0.
$$
Apply these facts to the Laurent expansion from step <1>3.
:::

<1>5. The polynomial $P$ has degree
$$
\deg P=n-m-1.
$$

::: {.proof}
The polynomial $Q$ is monic of degree $n$. Let
$$
d=\deg P.
$$
Since $P\neq0$ by step <1>4, the rational function
$$
\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}
$$
has leading behavior
$$
c z^{d-n}
$$
at infinity for some nonzero constant $c$. Step <1>4 says that the leading
power is instead
$$
z^{-m-1}.
$$
Therefore
$$
d-n=-m-1,
$$
so
$$
d=n-m-1.
$$
:::

<1>6. The function $f$ has exactly
$$
\boxed{n-m-1}
$$
zeros in $\CC$, counted with multiplicity.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>2, $P$ and $Q$ have no common zero, so no factor of the
denominator cancels. Thus the finite zeros of $f=P/Q$ are exactly the zeros
of $P$. By step <1>5, $P$ has degree $n-m-1$, and the fundamental theorem
of algebra gives exactly that many zeros counted with multiplicity.
:::

<1>7. If
$$
m\geq n,
$$
then the moment conditions are impossible under the assumption that all
$a_j$ are nonzero.

::: {.proof}
If $m\geq n$, then in particular
$$
S_p
=
\sum_{j=1}^n a_jz_j^p
=0
$$
for
$$
p=0,1,\ldots,n-1.
$$
This is the homogeneous linear system
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&\cdots&1\\
z_1&z_2&\cdots&z_n\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
z_1^{n-1}&z_2^{n-1}&\cdots&z_n^{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
a_1\\
a_2\\
\vdots\\
a_n
\end{pmatrix}
=0.
$$
The coefficient matrix is Vandermonde, with determinant
$$
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(z_j-z_i)\neq0
$$
because the $z_j$ are distinct. Hence
$$
a_1=\cdots=a_n=0,
$$
contrary to the hypotheses.
:::

<1>8. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>6 gives the zero count, and step <1>7 explains the impossible
range.
:::
:::
