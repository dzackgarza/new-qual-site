::: {.problem}
Let $f$ be holomorphic and zero-free on the annulus
\[
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb C:1<|z|<2\}.
\]
Show that there exist an integer $n$ and a holomorphic function $g$ on $\Omega$ such that
\[
f(z)=z^n e^{g(z)}
\]
for every $z\in\Omega$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Fix a radius $\rho$ with $1<\rho<2$, and let
$$
\gamma(t)=\rho e^{it},\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
$$

<1>1. The number
$$
n\coloneqq \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz
$$
is an integer.

::: {.proof}
Since $f$ has no zeros on $\Omega$, the curve $f\circ\gamma$ is a closed curve in $\CC^\times$. By the change-of-variables formula for line integrals,
$$
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz
=
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{f\circ\gamma}\frac{dw}{w}.
$$
The last quantity is the winding number of $f\circ\gamma$ about $0$, hence is an integer.
:::

<1>2. The holomorphic function
$$
h(z)\coloneqq \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}-\frac{n}{z}
$$
has a holomorphic primitive on $\Omega$.

::: {.proof}
The function $h$ is holomorphic on $\Omega$, so it has a Laurent expansion
$$
h(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_kz^k
$$
there. By step <1>1,
$$
\int_\gamma h(z)\,dz
=
\int_\gamma \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz
-n\int_\gamma \frac{dz}{z}
=0.
$$
For a Laurent series, this integral is $2\pi i a_{-1}$, so $a_{-1}=0$. Therefore the termwise antiderivative
$$
g_0(z)
=
\sum_{k\ne-1}\frac{a_k}{k+1}z^{k+1}
$$
converges locally uniformly on the same annulus and satisfies $g_0'=h$.
:::

<1>3. The function
$$
q(z)\coloneqq f(z)z^{-n}e^{-g_0(z)}
$$
is a nonzero constant on $\Omega$.

::: {.proof}
The function $q$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero on $\Omega$. Using step <1>2,
$$
\frac{q'(z)}{q(z)}
=
\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}-\frac{n}{z}-g_0'(z)
=0.
$$
Hence $q'=0$. Since $\Omega$ is connected, $q$ is constant, say $q\equiv c$ with $c\ne0$.
:::

<1>4. There is a holomorphic function $g$ on $\Omega$ such that
$$
\boxed{f(z)=z^ne^{g(z)}}
$$
for every $z\in\Omega$.

::: {.proof}
Choose $\lambda\in\CC$ with $e^\lambda=c$ and set
$$
g\coloneqq g_0+\lambda.
$$
By step <1>3,
$$
f(z)=cz^ne^{g_0(z)}=z^ne^{g(z)}.
$$
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 gives the required integer $n$, and step <1>4 gives the required holomorphic function $g$ and the factorization.
:::
