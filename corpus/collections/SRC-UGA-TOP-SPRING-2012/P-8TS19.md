::: problem
Let $X$ be a path-connected topological space.

(i) Prove that $X$ is connected.

(ii) Is the converse true?
Prove or give a counterexample.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Every path-connected topological space is connected.
::: {.proof}
Suppose \(X\) were path-connected but disconnected.
Then there would be disjoint nonempty open sets \(U,V\subseteq X\) with
\[
X=U\cup V.
\]
Choose
\[
u\in U,
\qquad
v\in V.
\]
Path-connectedness gives a continuous map
\[
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
\]
with \(\gamma(0)=u\) and \(\gamma(1)=v\). The inverse images
\[
\gamma^{-1}(U),
\qquad
\gamma^{-1}(V)
\]
are disjoint nonempty open subsets of \([0,1]\) whose union is all of \([0,1]\). This would disconnect the interval \([0,1]\), a contradiction.
Hence \(X\) is connected.
:::

<1>2. The converse is false.
Let
\[
G=\left\{\left(x,\sin\frac1x\right):0<x\le1\right\}
\subseteq\mathbb R^2
\]
and define
\[
Y=G\cup\{(0,0)\}.
\]
Then \(Y\) is connected.
::: {.proof}
The map
\[
(0,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb R^2,
\qquad
x\longmapsto\left(x,\sin\frac1x\right)
\]
is continuous.
Since \((0,1]\) is connected, its image \(G\) is connected.

Moreover,
\[
\left(\frac1{m\pi},0\right)\in G
\qquad(m\ge1),
\]
and these points converge to \((0,0)\). Thus
\[
(0,0)\in\overline G.
\]
The closure of a connected set is connected, so \(\overline G\) is connected.
Since
\[
G\subseteq Y\subseteq\overline G,
\]
the set \(Y\) is connected as well: if \(Y\) had a separation, its intersections with the dense connected subset \(G\) would force \(G\) into one side, while the other side could contain no point because every point of \(Y\) lies in \(\overline G\).
:::

<1>3. There is no path in \(Y\) from \((0,0)\) to a point of \(G\).
::: {.proof}
Suppose such a path existed.
Write
\[
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t)),
\qquad 0\le t\le1,
\]
with \(\gamma(0)=(0,0)\) and \(\gamma(1)\in G\). Then \(x(1)>0\). Let
\[
Z=\{t\in[0,1]:x(t)=0\}.
\]
The set \(Z\) is nonempty and closed, hence compact.
Let
\[
t_0=\max Z.
\]
Because \((0,0)\) is the only point of \(Y\) whose first coordinate is \(0\),
\[
\gamma(t_0)=(0,0).
\]
Also
\[
x(t)>0
\qquad(t_0<t\le1).
\]

For \(m\ge1\), set
\[
u_m=\frac1{\pi/2+2\pi m},
\qquad
v_m=\frac1{3\pi/2+2\pi m}.
\]
Then
\[
u_m\to0,
\qquad
v_m\to0,
\]
while
\[
\sin(1/u_m)=1,
\qquad
\sin(1/v_m)=-1.
\]
For sufficiently large \(m\), both \(u_m\) and \(v_m\) lie between \(0\) and \(x(1)\). By the intermediate value theorem there are
\[
r_m,s_m\in(t_0,1]
\]
with
\[
x(r_m)=u_m,
\qquad
x(s_m)=v_m.
\]

We claim
\[
r_m\to t_0,
\qquad
s_m\to t_0.
\]
Indeed, fix \(\delta>0\) with \(t_0+\delta\le1\). The continuous function \(x\) is strictly positive on the compact interval \([t_0+\delta,1]\), so it has a positive minimum there.
Since \(u_m,v_m\to0\), for all sufficiently large \(m\) neither value can be attained on \([t_0+\delta,1]\). Thus eventually
\[
t_0<r_m,s_m<t_0+\delta.
\]

Every point of \(Y\) with positive first coordinate lies on \(G\). Hence
\[
y(r_m)=\sin(1/u_m)=1,
\qquad
 y(s_m)=\sin(1/v_m)=-1.
\]
But \(r_m,s_m\to t_0\), while continuity of \(y\) at \(t_0\) requires both sequences to converge to
\[
y(t_0)=0.
\]
This contradiction proves that no such path exists.
:::

<1>4. Therefore connectedness does not imply path-connectedness.
::: {.proof}
By <1>2, \(Y\) is connected.
By <1>3, the point \((0,0)\) cannot be joined by a path to any point of \(G\), so \(Y\) is not path-connected.
Thus \(Y\) is the required counterexample.
:::
:::
