::: problem
Suppose $D = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ and $\mathfrak{a} = \langle 3, 1 + \sqrt{-5} \rangle$.

(a) Prove that $\mathfrak{a}$ is not a principal ideal.

(b) Prove that $\mathfrak{a}$ is a projective $D$-module.
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
\[
s=\sqrt{-5},
\qquad
D=\mathbb Z[s],
\qquad
\mathfrak a=(3,1+s).
\]

<1>1. The ideal $\mathfrak a$ is proper.
::: {.proof}
Define
\[
\varphi:D\longrightarrow\mathbb F_3,
\qquad
\varphi(m+ns)=m-n\pmod3.
\]
This is a well-defined ring homomorphism because the image $-1$ of $s$ satisfies
\[
(-1)^2=1=-5\pmod3.
\]
The map is surjective, while
\[
\varphi(3)=0,
\qquad
\varphi(1+s)=0.
\]
Hence
\[
\mathfrak a\subseteq\ker\varphi\ne D,
\]
so $\mathfrak a$ is proper.
:::

<1>2. If $\mathfrak a=(\alpha)$ were principal, then the norm of $\alpha$ would equal $3$.
::: {.proof}
Suppose
\[
\mathfrak a=(\alpha).
\]
Since $3$ and $1+s$ belong to $(\alpha)$, the element $\alpha$ divides both $3$ and $1+s$ in $D$.

For $m,n\in\mathbb Z$, the field norm is
\[
N(m+ns)=(m+ns)(m-ns)=m^2+5n^2.
\]
It is multiplicative. Therefore $N(\alpha)$ divides
\[
N(3)=9
\]
and
\[
N(1+s)=6.
\]
Thus $N(\alpha)$ divides $3$.

By <1>1, $(\alpha)$ is proper, so $\alpha$ is not a unit. An element of $D$ with norm $1$ is a unit, since its conjugate is its inverse. Hence
\[
N(\alpha)>1.
\]
It follows that
\[
N(\alpha)=3.
\]
:::

<1>3. The ideal $\mathfrak a$ is not principal.
::: {.proof}
If $\alpha=m+ns$ had norm $3$, then
\[
m^2+5n^2=3.
\]
If $n\ne0$, the left side is at least $5$; if $n=0$, it is the square $m^2$, which cannot equal $3$.
Thus no element of $D$ has norm $3$, contradicting <1>2. Therefore $\mathfrak a$ is not principal. This proves part (a).
:::

<1>4. The conjugate ideal
\[
\overline{\mathfrak a}=(3,1-s)
\]
satisfies
\[
\mathfrak a\overline{\mathfrak a}=(3).
\]
::: {.proof}
The product ideal is generated by
\[
9,
\quad
3(1-s),
\quad
3(1+s),
\quad
(1+s)(1-s)=6.
\]
Every generator is divisible by $3$, so
\[
\mathfrak a\overline{\mathfrak a}\subseteq(3).
\]
Conversely,
\[
3=9-6
\]
belongs to the product ideal. Hence $(3)$ is contained in the product, proving equality.
:::

<1>5. The fractional ideal
\[
\mathfrak a^{-1}:=\frac{1}{3}\overline{\mathfrak a}
\]
is an inverse of $\mathfrak a$.
::: {.proof}
Dividing the equality in <1>4 by $3$ gives
\[
\mathfrak a\left(\frac{1}{3}\overline{\mathfrak a}\right)=D.
\]
In particular every $y\in\mathfrak a^{-1}$ satisfies
\[
y\mathfrak a\subseteq D.
\]
:::

<1>6. The natural surjection $D^2\to\mathfrak a$ splits.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
\pi:D^2\longrightarrow\mathfrak a,
\qquad
\pi(u,v)=3u+(1+s)v.
\]
It is surjective because $3$ and $1+s$ generate $\mathfrak a$.

The identity
\[
1=3-(1+s)\frac{1-s}{3}
\]
holds because $(1+s)(1-s)=6$. Define
\[
\sigma:\mathfrak a\longrightarrow D^2,
\qquad
\sigma(z)=\left(z,-\frac{1-s}{3}z\right).
\]
The second coordinate lies in $D$ by <1>5, since
\[
\frac{1-s}{3}\in\mathfrak a^{-1}.
\]
Thus $\sigma$ is a well-defined $D$-linear map. Moreover,
\[
\pi(\sigma(z))
=
3z-(1+s)\frac{1-s}{3}z
=z.
\]
Hence
\[
\pi\circ\sigma=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak a}.
\]
:::

<1>7. The $D$-module $\mathfrak a$ is projective.
::: {.proof}
By <1>6, $\mathfrak a$ is a direct summand of the free module $D^2$. Every direct summand of a free module is projective. Therefore $\mathfrak a$ is projective, proving part (b).
:::
