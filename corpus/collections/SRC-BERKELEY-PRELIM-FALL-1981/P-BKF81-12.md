::: {.problem}
Let
\[
u(x,y)=\frac{a^2+b^2+x^2-y^2}{(a-x)^2+(b-y)^2}.
\]
Show that $u$ is harmonic. The original problem asks for an entire function whose real part is $u$, but the paper corrects this: explain why this is impossible, modify $u$ slightly, and solve the corrected problem.
:::

::: {.remark}
The problem is false as printed: the displayed $u$ is not harmonic.
On the complement of $(a,b)$,
\[
\Delta u=\frac{4\left(a^2+b^2+2ax-x^2-2by+y^2\right)}{\left((a-x)^2+(b-y)^2\right)^2},
\]
which is not identically zero; for $a=1$, $b=0$ it equals $4$ at the origin.
No entire function has real part $u$, since $u$ is unbounded near $(a,b)$ whenever $a\neq0$.
The source prints no modified formula.
The solution below replaces $+x^2$ by $-x^2$; with $c=a+ib$ and $z=x+iy$, the modified function is $\operatorname{Re}\frac{c+z}{c-z}$, which is harmonic on $\CC\setminus\{c\}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
D(x,y)\coloneqq(a-x)^2+(b-y)^2
$$
and let
$$
\Omega\coloneqq\CC\setminus\{a+ib\}.
$$

<1>1. The function printed in the source is not harmonic on its natural
domain.

::: {.proof}
For
$$
u(x,y)
=
\frac{a^2+b^2+x^2-y^2}{D(x,y)},
$$
direct differentiation gives
$$
\Delta u
=
\frac{
4\bigl(a^2+b^2+2ax-x^2-2by+y^2\bigr)
}{
D(x,y)^2
}.
$$
The numerator is not the zero polynomial: its coefficient of $x^2$ is
$-4$. Thus $\Delta u$ is not identically zero on
$\RR^2\setminus\{(a,b)\}$, so $u$ is not harmonic.
:::

<1>2. Consequently the printed $u$ cannot be the real part of an entire
function.

::: {.proof}
The real part of every holomorphic function is harmonic. In particular,
the real part of an entire function is harmonic on all of $\CC$. Step
<1>1 shows that the printed $u$ is not even harmonic on its punctured
natural domain, so no entire function can have real part $u$ there.
:::

<1>3. Make the single sign change
$$
\widetilde u(x,y)
\coloneqq
\frac{a^2+b^2-x^2-y^2}{D(x,y)}.
$$
If
$$
c\coloneqq a+ib,
$$
then on $\Omega$,
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde u(x,y)
=
\operatorname{Re}
\left(
\frac{c+z}{c-z}
\right),
\qquad z=x+iy.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Since
$$
\abs{c-z}^2=D(x,y),
$$
one has
$$
\frac{c+z}{c-z}
=
\frac{(c+z)(\overline c-\overline z)}
{\abs{c-z}^2}.
$$
The real part of the numerator is
$$
\operatorname{Re}
\bigl(
(c+z)(\overline c-\overline z)
\bigr)
=
\abs{c}^2-\abs{z}^2
=
a^2+b^2-x^2-y^2,
$$
because
$$
z\overline c-c\overline z
$$
is purely imaginary. Dividing by $D(x,y)$ gives the stated identity.
:::

<1>4. The modified function $\widetilde u$ is harmonic on
$\RR^2\setminus\{(a,b)\}$.

::: {.proof}
The function
$$
F(z)\coloneqq\frac{c+z}{c-z}
$$
is holomorphic on $\Omega$. By step <1>3,
$$
\widetilde u=\operatorname{Re}F
$$
there, and the real part of a holomorphic function is harmonic.
:::

<1>5. The corresponding holomorphic function is
$$
\boxed{
F(z)=\frac{a+ib+z}{a+ib-z}
}
$$
on $\CC\setminus\{a+ib\}$.

::: {.proof}
This is exactly the function whose real part was computed in step <1>3.
Thus it solves the corrected harmonic-conjugate problem on the natural
domain of $\widetilde u$.
:::

<1>6. If $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$, no entire function has real part
$\widetilde u$ on $\Omega$.

::: {.proof}
When $c\ne0$,
$$
F(z)
=
-1+\frac{2c}{c-z}
$$
has a simple pole at $z=c$. Equivalently,
$\widetilde u=\operatorname{Re}F$ is unbounded in every punctured
neighborhood of $c$. The real part of an entire function is continuous and
finite at $c$, so it cannot agree with $\widetilde u$ on $\Omega$.
:::

<1>7. If $(a,b)=(0,0)$, the modified function is the constant $-1$ on
$\Omega$, and it extends across the origin.

::: {.proof}
For $c=0$ and $z\ne0$,
$$
F(z)=\frac z{-z}=-1.
$$
Hence $\widetilde u=-1$, and the constant entire function
$F(z)=-1$ gives the extension.
:::

<1>8. The printed $u$ is not harmonic and is not the real part of an entire
function. The modified function satisfies
$$
\boxed{
\widetilde u
=
\operatorname{Re}
\left(
\frac{a+ib+z}{a+ib-z}
\right)
}
$$
on $\CC\setminus\{a+ib\}$, and $\widetilde u$ is the real part of an entire
function if and only if $(a,b)=(0,0)$.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>2 prove the claims about the printed $u$. Step <1>3
gives the formula for $\widetilde u$, and steps <1>6 and <1>7 decide when
$\widetilde u$ is the real part of an entire function.
:::

<1>9. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>8 answers each part of the problem.
:::
