::: {.problem}
Let $G$ have normal subgroups $N_1,N_2,N_3$ such that $N_i\cap N_j=\{e\}$ and $N_iN_j=G$ whenever $i\ne j$. Show that $G$ is abelian and the $N_i$ are pairwise isomorphic.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Distinct $N_i$ and $N_j$ commute elementwise.
::: {.proof}
Let $i\ne j$, and take
$$
x\in N_i,
\qquad
y\in N_j.
$$
Because $N_i$ is normal,
$$
[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}\in N_i.
$$
Because $N_j$ is normal, the same commutator also belongs to $N_j$. Hence
$$
[x,y]\in N_i\cap N_j=\{e\}.
$$
Thus $xy=yx$.
:::

<1>2. Identify $G$ with the direct product $N_1\times N_2$.
::: {.proof}
By hypothesis,
$$
G=N_1N_2.
$$
Step <1>1 shows that $N_1$ and $N_2$ commute elementwise, and their
intersection is trivial. Therefore the multiplication map
$$
N_1\times N_2\longrightarrow G,
\qquad
(x,y)\longmapsto xy,
$$
is an isomorphism. We henceforth use this identification.
:::

<1>3. The subgroup $N_3$ is the graph of an isomorphism $\varphi:N_1\to N_2$.
::: {.proof}
Let
$$
\pi_1:G=N_1\times N_2\to N_1
$$
be the first projection. Its restriction to $N_3$ has kernel
$$
N_3\cap N_2=\{e\},
$$
so it is injective. Also
$$
N_3N_2=G,
$$
so every first coordinate occurs among elements of $N_3$; hence
$\pi_1|_{N_3}$ is surjective. Thus it is an isomorphism.

Similarly, the second projection restricts to an isomorphism
$$
\pi_2|_{N_3}:N_3\to N_2.
$$
Define
$$
\varphi
=(\pi_2|_{N_3})\circ(\pi_1|_{N_3})^{-1}:N_1\to N_2.
$$
Then $\varphi$ is an isomorphism and
$$
N_3=\{(x,\varphi(x)):x\in N_1\}.
$$
In particular
$$
N_1\cong N_3\cong N_2.
$$
:::

<1>4. Normality of $N_3$ forces $N_1$ to be abelian.
::: {.proof}
Take $a,x\in N_1$. Since
$$
(x,\varphi(x))\in N_3
$$
and $N_3\trianglelefteq G$, conjugating by $(a,e)$ gives
$$
(a,e)(x,\varphi(x))(a,e)^{-1}
=(axa^{-1},\varphi(x))\in N_3.
$$
But $N_3$ is the graph of $\varphi$, so membership in $N_3$ implies
$$
\varphi(axa^{-1})=\varphi(x).
$$
Since $\varphi$ is injective,
$$
axa^{-1}=x.
$$
Thus every $a,x\in N_1$ commute, and $N_1$ is abelian. Since
$N_2\cong N_1$ and $N_3\cong N_1$, all three are abelian.
:::

<1>5. Conclude that $G$ is abelian.
::: {.proof}
By step <1>2,
$$
G\cong N_1\times N_2.
$$
Both factors are abelian by step <1>4, so their direct product is abelian.
Therefore
$$
\boxed{G\text{ is abelian},
\qquad N_1\cong N_2\cong N_3.}
$$
:::
