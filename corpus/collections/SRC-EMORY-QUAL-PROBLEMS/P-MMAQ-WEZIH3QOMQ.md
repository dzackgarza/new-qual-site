::: problem
An integral domain $R$ is said to be an *Euclidean domain* if there
is a function $N: R \to \{n\in\mathbb{Z} \mid n\geq 0\}$ such that
$N(0)=0$ and for each $a,b\in R$ with $b\neq 0$, there exist
elements $q,r\in R$ with
`\begin{align*}
  a = qb + r, \quad \text{and} \quad r = 0 \, \text{ or } \, N(r) < N(b).
\end{align*}`{=tex}

Prove:

1.  The ring $F[[x]]$ of power series over a field $F$ is an
    Euclidean domain.

2.  Every Euclidean domain is a PID.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. A nonzero series $f\in F[[x]]$ has a unique
factorization $f=x^k u$ with $u$ a unit and $u(0)\ne0$,
where $k$ is its least nonzero coefficient index.

::: proof
Write $f=\sum_{j\geq0}a_jx^j$ and choose the least
$k$ with $a_k\ne0$. Then $f=x^k u$ with
$u=\sum_{j\geq0}a_{k+j}x^j$. For a series
$u=\sum u_jx^j$ with $u_0\ne0$, define coefficients
of its inverse recursively by
$$
v_0=u_0^{-1},\qquad
v_n=-u_0^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_{n-i}\quad(n\geq1).
$$
The coefficient of $x^0$ in $uv$ is one and every
higher coefficient is zero. Thus $uv=1$.
Conversely, a unit must have nonzero constant term
by taking constant coefficients in $uv=1$.

If nonzero series $f,g$ have least indices $k,l$,
their product has least index $k+l$, with coefficient
$a_k b_l\ne0$. This also proves that $F[[x]]$ is
an integral domain. The index $k$ and the shifted
series $u$ are uniquely determined by the coefficients.
:::

<1>2. The least nonzero coefficient index is a
Euclidean function on the nonzero series.

::: proof
Set $N(0)=0$ and $N(f)=k$ as in step <1>1 for
$f\ne0$. Given $a\in F[[x]]$ and a nonzero
$b=x^k u$, let $r$ be the polynomial consisting
of the terms of $a$ in degrees less than $k$.
There is a series $c$ with $a=r+x^k c$.
Set $q=cu^{-1}$. Commutativity gives
$a=qb+r$. If $r\ne0$, its least nonzero degree
is less than $k=N(b)$, as required.
If $k=0$, then $r=0$ and the division is exact.
Thus the stated convention, including $N(0)=0$,
is satisfied in all cases.
:::

<1>3. Every Euclidean domain $R$ is a PID.

::: proof
Let $I$ be a nonzero ideal. The nonempty set
$\{N(a):a\in I,\ a\ne0\}$ of nonnegative integers
has a least element. Choose $b\in I\setminus\{0\}$
attaining it. For any $a\in I$, Euclidean division
gives $a=qb+r$ with $r=0$ or $N(r)<N(b)$.
The remainder belongs to $I$ since $r=a-qb$.
The strict inequality contradicts minimality if
$r\ne0$, so $r=0$ and $a\in(b)$.
Thus $I\subseteq(b)$; the reverse inclusion follows
from $b\in I$. The zero ideal is generated by zero,
so every ideal is principal. Since $R$ is an
integral domain by definition, it is a PID.
:::
:::
