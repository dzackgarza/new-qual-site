::: {.problem}
Show that the infinite series $\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n2^{-nx}$ converges uniformly on $[0,B]$ for any $B > 0$.
Does this series converge uniformly on $[0,\infty)$?
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

The terms are $x^n2^{-nx} = (x2^{-x})^n$.

::: pf-proof

For every $x\geq0$ and integer $n\geq0$, the exponent law gives $2^{-nx}=(2^{-x})^n$; multiplication by $x^n$ gives the identity.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

$\sup_{x \ge 0} x2^{-x} = \frac{1}{e\ln 2} < 1$.

::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #s2-1}

The function $h(x) = x2^{-x} = xe^{-x\ln 2}$ has its global maximum on $[0,\infty)$ at $x = 1/\ln 2$.

::: pf-proof

Differentiation gives $h'(x)=e^{-x\ln2}(1-x\ln2)$. Since $\ln2>0$, this derivative is positive for $0\leq x<1/\ln2$ and negative for $x>1/\ln2$. Thus the unique global maximum occurs at $x=1/\ln2$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2-2}

$h(1/\ln 2) = \frac{1}{e\ln 2} \approx 0.531 < 1$.

::: pf-proof

Substitution gives $h(1/\ln2)=1/(e\ln2)$. Moreover $\ln2=\int_1^2dt/t>1/2$ and $e>2$, so $e\ln2>1$. Hence $r:=1/(e\ln2)$ lies strictly between zero and one.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps [](#s2-1){.pf-ref} and [](#s2-2){.pf-ref} show that $0\leq h(x)\leq r<1$ for every $x\geq0$.

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

The series converges uniformly on $[0, B]$ for every $B > 0$.

::: pf-proof

By steps [](#s1){.pf-ref} and [](#s2){.pf-ref}, $|x^n2^{-nx}|\leq r^n$ for every $x\in[0,B]$. Since $\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n$ converges, the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

The series converges uniformly on $[0, \infty)$ as well.

::: pf-proof

The same bound holds on the entire half-line. In particular, for every $N\geq0$,
\[
\sup_{x\geq0}\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty x^n2^{-nx}\right|
\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty r^n=\frac{r^{N+1}}{1-r}\longrightarrow0.
\]
Thus convergence is uniform on $[0,\infty)$, not merely on its bounded subintervals.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Steps [](#s3){.pf-ref} and [](#s4){.pf-ref} answer both questions.
