::: {.problem}
An $n\times n$ real matrix $T$ is called positive definite if it is symmetric and
\[
\langle Tx,x\rangle>0
\]
for every nonzero $x\in\mathbb R^n$.
Let $A$ and $B$ be positive-definite real matrices.

1. Show that there is a basis $\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ of $\mathbb R^n$ and real numbers $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ such that
\[
\langle Av_i,v_j\rangle=
\begin{cases}
1,&i=j,\\
0,&i\ne j,
\end{cases}
\]
and
\[
\langle Bv_i,v_j\rangle=
\begin{cases}
\lambda_i,&i=j,\\
0,&i\ne j.
\end{cases}
\]

2. Deduce that there is an invertible real matrix $U$ such that
\[
U^TAU=I
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
U^TBU
\]
is diagonal.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The formula
$$
(x,y)_A\coloneqq\langle Ax,y\rangle
$$
defines an inner product on $\RR^n$.

::: {.proof}
Because $A$ is symmetric,
$$
(x,y)_A
=
\langle Ax,y\rangle
=
\langle x,Ay\rangle
=
(y,x)_A.
$$
Bilinearity is immediate, and positive definiteness of $A$ gives
$$
(x,x)_A=\langle Ax,x\rangle>0
$$
for every nonzero $x$.
:::

<1>2. The matrix $A$ is invertible, and the operator
$$
T\coloneqq A^{-1}B
$$
is self-adjoint with respect to $(\ ,\ )_A$.

::: {.proof}
If $Ax=0$, then
$$
\langle Ax,x\rangle=0,
$$
so positive definiteness forces $x=0$. Thus $A$ has trivial kernel and is invertible.

For $x,y\in\RR^n$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
(Tx,y)_A
&=
\langle A(A^{-1}B)x,y\rangle\\
&=
\langle Bx,y\rangle\\
&=
\langle x,By\rangle\\
&=
\langle Ax,A^{-1}By\rangle\\
&=
(x,Ty)_A,
\end{aligned}
$$
where symmetry of $B$ is used in the third equality and symmetry of $A$ in the fourth.
:::

<1>3. There is an $(\ ,\ )_A$-orthonormal basis
$$
v_1,\ldots,v_n
$$
of $\RR^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $T$:
$$
Tv_i=\lambda_i v_i
$$
for real numbers $\lambda_i$.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>2, $T$ is self-adjoint on the finite-dimensional real inner-product space $(\RR^n,(\ ,\ )_A)$. The spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators therefore gives an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues.
:::

<1>4. The basis from step <1>3 satisfies
$$
\langle Av_i,v_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}
$$
and
$$
\langle Bv_i,v_j\rangle
=
\lambda_i\delta_{ij}.
$$

::: {.proof}
The first identity is exactly the $(\ ,\ )_A$-orthonormality of the basis. Since
$$
B=AT,
$$
one has
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle Bv_i,v_j\rangle
&=
\langle ATv_i,v_j\rangle\\
&=
\lambda_i\langle Av_i,v_j\rangle\\
&=
\lambda_i\delta_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves part 1. In fact, positive definiteness of $B$ also gives $\lambda_i>0$, since
$$
\lambda_i
=
\langle Bv_i,v_i\rangle.
$$
:::

<1>5. Let $U$ be the matrix whose $i$th column is $v_i$. Then
$$
U^TAU=I
$$
and
$$
U^TBU=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).
$$

::: {.proof}
Because $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is a basis, $U$ is invertible. The $(i,j)$ entry of $U^TAU$ is
$$
v_i^TAv_j
=
\langle Av_j,v_i\rangle
=
\delta_{ij},
$$
so $U^TAU=I$. Likewise, the $(i,j)$ entry of $U^TBU$ is
$$
v_i^TBv_j
=
\langle Bv_j,v_i\rangle
=
\lambda_j\delta_{ij},
$$
which is the stated diagonal matrix.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 proves part 1, and step <1>5 proves part 2.
:::
