::: {.problem}
Let $I$ be an open interval containing $0$. Suppose $f'$ exists in a neighborhood of $0$ and $f''(0)$ exists. Show that
\[
f(x)=f(0)+f'(0)\sin x+\frac12f''(0)\sin^2x+o(x^2)
\qquad(x\to0).
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
::: pf

::: {.pf-step #first-derivative-expansion}
As $x\to0$,
$$
f'(x)
=
f'(0)+f''(0)x+o(x).
$$

::: pf-proof
The existence of $f''(0)$ means precisely that
$$
\frac{f'(x)-f'(0)}{x}
\longrightarrow
f''(0).
$$
Rearranging this limit gives the stated first-order expansion of $f'$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #peano-expansion}
The ordinary second-order Peano expansion holds:
$$
f(x)
=
f(0)+f'(0)x+\frac12f''(0)x^2+o(x^2).
$$

::: pf-proof
Set
$$
R(x)
\coloneqq
f(x)-f(0)-f'(0)x-\frac12f''(0)x^2.
$$
Then $R(0)=0$ and, by step [](#first-derivative-expansion){.pf-ref},
$$
R'(x)
=
f'(x)-f'(0)-f''(0)x
=
o(x).
$$

For $x\neq0$ sufficiently close to $0$, Cauchy's mean value theorem
applied to $R(t)$ and $t^2$ on the interval with endpoints $0$ and $x$
gives a point $\xi$ strictly between those endpoints such that
$$
\frac{R(x)}{x^2}
=
\frac{R'(\xi)}{2\xi}.
$$
As $x\to0$, also $\xi\to0$, and step [](#first-derivative-expansion){.pf-ref} gives
$$
\frac{R'(\xi)}{2\xi}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Hence $R(x)=o(x^2)$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #sine-approximations}
As $x\to0$,
$$
x
=
\sin x+o(x^2)
$$
and
$$
x^2
=
\sin^2x+o(x^2).
$$

::: pf-proof
The standard expansion
$$
\sin x=x+O(x^3)
$$
gives
$$
x-\sin x=O(x^3)=o(x^2).
$$
Moreover,
$$
x^2-\sin^2x
=
(x-\sin x)(x+\sin x)
=
O(x^4)
=
o(x^2).
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #final-expansion-boxed}
Therefore
$$
\boxed{
f(x)
=
f(0)+f'(0)\sin x
+\frac12f''(0)\sin^2x
+o(x^2)
}.
$$

::: pf-proof
Substitute the two relations from step [](#sine-approximations){.pf-ref} into the Peano expansion of
step [](#peano-expansion){.pf-ref}. Multiplication by the fixed constants $f'(0)$ and
$\frac12f''(0)$ preserves the $o(x^2)$ error, and a finite sum of
$o(x^2)$ terms is again $o(x^2)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-qed
Step [](#final-expansion-boxed){.pf-ref} is the desired expansion.
:::
