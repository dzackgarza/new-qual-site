::: {.problem}
Let $F:[0,1]\to\mathbb C$ be continuous and define
\[
f(z)=\int_0^1\frac{F(t)}{t-z}\,dt,
\qquad z\in\mathbb C\setminus[0,1].
\]

1. Prove that $f$ is analytic on $\mathbb C\setminus[0,1]$.
2. Express the coefficients of the Laurent series of $f$ at $\infty$ in terms of $F$, and use them to prove that $F$ is uniquely determined by $f$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The function $f$ is analytic on $\CC\sm[0,1]$, with
$$
f'(z)
=
\int_0^1\frac{F(t)}{(t-z)^2}\,dt.
$$

::: {.proof}
Fix $z_0\in\CC\sm[0,1]$ and let
$$
d\coloneqq\operatorname{dist}(z_0,[0,1])>0.
$$
If $\abs{z-z_0}<d/2$, then for every $t\in[0,1]$,
$$
\abs{t-z}\geq d/2,
\qquad
\abs{t-z_0}\geq d.
$$
For $z\neq z_0$,
$$
\frac{f(z)-f(z_0)}{z-z_0}
=
\int_0^1
\frac{F(t)}{(t-z)(t-z_0)}
\,dt.
$$
As $z\to z_0$, the integrand converges uniformly in $t\in[0,1]$ to
$$
\frac{F(t)}{(t-z_0)^2}.
$$
Indeed, continuity of $F$ makes it bounded, while the two denominators
are uniformly separated from zero on the indicated disk. Therefore
the limit may be passed through the integral, giving
$$
f'(z_0)
=
\int_0^1\frac{F(t)}{(t-z_0)^2}\,dt.
$$
Since $z_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is analytic on $\CC\sm[0,1]$.
:::

<1>2. For $\abs z>1$,
$$
f(z)
=
-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\left(
\int_0^1 t^nF(t)\,dt
\right)
z^{-n-1}.
$$

::: {.proof}
For $t\in[0,1]$ and $\abs z>1$,
$$
\frac1{t-z}
=
-\frac1z\frac1{1-t/z}
=
-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^n}{z^{n+1}}.
$$
Because
$$
\abs{t/z}\leq\frac1{\abs z}<1,
$$
the geometric series converges uniformly for $t\in[0,1]$. Multiplying
by the bounded function $F(t)$ and integrating term by term gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)
&=
-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac1{z^{n+1}}
\int_0^1t^nF(t)\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>3. Thus the Laurent coefficient of $z^{-n-1}$ at infinity is
$$
\boxed{
a_{-n-1}
=
-\int_0^1t^nF(t)\,dt
},
\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,
$$
and all coefficients of nonnegative powers of $z$ vanish.

::: {.proof}
This is the coefficient comparison in the Laurent expansion from step
<1>2.
:::

<1>4. If two continuous functions $F,G:[0,1]\to\CC$ determine the
same transform $f$, then for
$$
H\coloneqq F-G
$$
one has
$$
\int_0^1 t^nH(t)\,dt=0
$$
for every $n\geq0$.

::: {.proof}
The common transform has a unique Laurent expansion at infinity. Step
<1>3 applied to $F$ and $G$ therefore gives equality of all their
moments. Subtracting yields the displayed identities.
:::

<1>5. The identities in step <1>4 force $H\equiv0$.

::: {.proof}
By linearity, step <1>4 implies
$$
\int_0^1p(t)H(t)\,dt=0
$$
for every complex polynomial $p$.

By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, there are complex polynomials
$p_k$ converging uniformly on $[0,1]$ to the continuous function
$\overline{H}$. Hence
$$
0
=
\lim_{k\to\infty}
\int_0^1p_k(t)H(t)\,dt
=
\int_0^1\abs{H(t)}^2\,dt.
$$
The integrand is continuous and nonnegative, so this integral can vanish
only if $H(t)=0$ for every $t\in[0,1]$.
:::

<1>6. Therefore $F$ is uniquely determined by $f$.

::: {.proof}
If $F$ and $G$ give the same $f$, step <1>5 applied to $H=F-G$ yields
$F=G$.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 proves analyticity, steps <1>2--<1>3 give the Laurent
coefficients, and steps <1>4--<1>6 prove uniqueness.
:::
:::
