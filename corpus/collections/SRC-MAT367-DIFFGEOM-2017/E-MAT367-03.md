::: {.problem}
Identify the space of rotations of $\mathbb R^3$ with $\mathbb{RP}^3$.
The source suggests associating to $v\in\mathbb R^3$ the rotation through angle $\lVert v\rVert$ about the oriented axis determined by $v$, noting that vectors $v$ and $-v$ with $\lVert v\rVert=\pi$ determine the same rotation.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
\[
B_\pi^3=\{v\in\mathbb R^3:\lVert v\rVert\le\pi\},
\]
and let $SO(3)$ carry its usual subspace topology in $M_3(\mathbb R)$.

<1>1. The axis--angle map
\[
\Phi:B_\pi^3\longrightarrow SO(3)
\]
is surjective, and its only nontrivial fibres are the pairs $\{v,-v\}$ with $\lVert v\rVert=\pi$.
::: {.proof}
For $v\ne0$, let $\Phi(v)$ be rotation through angle $\lVert v\rVert$ about the oriented axis $\mathbb Rv$, and set $\Phi(0)=I$.

Every element $R\in SO(3)$ has an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$: a real eigenvalue of a real $3\times3$ orthogonal matrix is $\pm1$, and the determinant condition forces the product of the eigenvalues to be $1$.  On the orthogonal complement of a fixed unit eigenvector, $R$ is an orientation-preserving orthogonal map of a plane, hence a rotation through a unique angle $\theta\in[0,\pi]$.  Thus $R=\Phi(\theta u)$ for a suitable unit vector $u$, so $\Phi$ is surjective.

For $0<\theta<\pi$, the angle is determined by
\[
\operatorname{tr}(R)=1+2\cos\theta,
\]
and the fixed axis is the one-dimensional space $\ker(R-I)$.  Since $\sin\theta>0$, the orientation of that axis is determined by the skew-symmetric part $R-R^{\mathsf T}$.  Hence the representing vector $\theta u$ is unique.  The identity has only the representative $0$ in $B_\pi^3$.  For $\theta=\pi$, reversing the axis does not change the rotation, so exactly the two boundary points $\pi u$ and $-\pi u$ represent the same rotation.
:::

<1>2. The map $\Phi$ induces a homeomorphism
\[
B_\pi^3/\!\sim\;\longrightarrow SO(3),
\]
where the equivalence relation identifies $v$ with $-v$ only on $\partial B_\pi^3$.
::: {.proof}
For $v\in\mathbb R^3$, let $[v]_\times$ denote the matrix of the map $w\mapsto v\times w$.  Rodrigues' formula gives, for $r=\lVert v\rVert$,
\[
\Phi(v)
=I+\frac{\sin r}{r}[v]_\times
+\frac{1-\cos r}{r^2}[v]_\times^2,
\]
with the coefficients extended continuously at $r=0$.  Hence $\Phi$ is continuous.

By <1>1 its fibres are exactly the equivalence classes of $\sim$, so it descends to a continuous bijection
\[
\overline\Phi:B_\pi^3/\!\sim\;\longrightarrow SO(3).
\]
The source is compact because it is a quotient of the compact ball $B_\pi^3$, while $SO(3)$ is Hausdorff as a subspace of the Euclidean space $M_3(\mathbb R)$.  Therefore $\overline\Phi$ is a homeomorphism.
:::

<1>3. The quotient $B_\pi^3/\!\sim$ is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{RP}^3$.
::: {.proof}
Write
\[
S^3=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R:\lVert x\rVert^2+t^2=1\}.
\]
The closed upper hemisphere
\[
H_+=\{(x,t)\in S^3:t\ge0\}
\]
is homeomorphic to a closed $3$-ball.  Every antipodal pair $\{p,-p\}$ in $S^3$ meets the interior of $H_+$ in exactly one point, unless $p$ lies on the equator $t=0$.  On the equator, both representatives lie in $H_+$ and are antipodal boundary points.

Consequently the quotient
\[
S^3/(p\sim -p)=\mathbb{RP}^3
\]
is precisely a closed $3$-ball with antipodal boundary points identified.  Rescaling that ball to radius $\pi$ gives
\[
B_\pi^3/\!\sim\;\cong\mathbb{RP}^3.
\]
:::

<1>4. The space of rotations of $\mathbb R^3$ is
\[
\boxed{SO(3)\cong\mathbb{RP}^3}.
\]
::: {.proof}
Combine the homeomorphism of <1>2 with the quotient identification of <1>3.
:::

<1>5. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 is the required identification.
