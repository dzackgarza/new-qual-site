::: {.problem}
Let $S\subset\mathbb R$ be uncountable. Prove that there exists $t\in\mathbb R$ such that both
\[
S\cap(-\infty,t)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
S\cap(t,\infty)
\]
are uncountable.
:::

::: {.solution}
For $t\in\RR$, write
$$
S_t^- = S\cap(-\infty,t),
\qquad
S_t^+ = S\cap(t,\infty).
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

Suppose, for contradiction, that no $t\in\RR$ makes both
$S_t^-$ and $S_t^+$ uncountable. Define
$$
A=\{t\in\RR:S_t^-\text{ is countable}\},
\qquad
B=\{t\in\RR:S_t^+\text{ is countable}\}.
$$
Then
$$
A\cup B=\RR.
$$

::: pf-proof

Under the contrary assumption, for every real $t$ at least one of the
sets $S_t^-$ and $S_t^+$ is countable. This is exactly the displayed
union.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

Both $A$ and $B$ are nonempty.

::: pf-proof

If $A$ were empty, step [](#s1){.pf-ref} would give $B=\RR$. In particular,
each set
$$
S\cap(-n,\infty),
\qquad
n\geq1,
$$
would be countable. But
$$
S
=
\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty
\bigl(S\cap(-n,\infty)\bigr),
$$
so $S$ would be countable, a contradiction.

Similarly, if $B$ were empty, then $A=\RR$, and
$$
S
=
\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty
\bigl(S\cap(-\infty,n)\bigr)
$$
would be a countable union of countable sets. Hence $B$ is also
nonempty.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

Every $a\in A$ and $b\in B$ satisfy
$$
a\leq b.
$$

::: pf-proof

If instead $b<a$, then
$$
\RR=(-\infty,a)\cup(b,\infty),
$$
so
$$
S
=
S_a^-\cup S_b^+.
$$
Both sets on the right are countable by the definitions of $A$ and
$B$, contradicting that $S$ is uncountable.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

There is a real number $c$ such that
$$
S\cap(-\infty,c)
\quad\text{and}\quad
S\cap(c,\infty)
$$
are both countable.

::: pf-proof

By steps [](#s2){.pf-ref} and [](#s3){.pf-ref}, $A$ is nonempty and bounded above by every
element of $B$. Let
$$
c=\sup A.
$$
For each $n\geq1$, choose $a_n\in A$ with
$$
c-\frac1n<a_n\leq c.
$$
If $x<c$, then $x<a_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Hence
$$
S\cap(-\infty,c)
\subseteq
\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty S_{a_n}^-.
$$
Each $S_{a_n}^-$ is countable, so the left-hand set is countable.

Step [](#s3){.pf-ref} also implies that every $b\in B$ satisfies $c\leq b$.
Conversely, if $t>c$, then $t\notin A$, so step [](#s1){.pf-ref} gives $t\in B$.
Thus for every $n\geq1$,
$$
b_n=c+\frac1n\in B.
$$
If $x>c$, then $x>b_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Therefore
$$
S\cap(c,\infty)
\subseteq
\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty S_{b_n}^+,
$$
which is countable.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

The contrary assumption is impossible.

::: pf-proof

By step [](#s4){.pf-ref},
$$
S
=
\bigl(S\cap(-\infty,c)\bigr)
\cup
\bigl(S\cap\{c\}\bigr)
\cup
\bigl(S\cap(c,\infty)\bigr)
$$
is a union of three countable sets. Hence $S$ would be countable,
contrary to hypothesis.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

There exists $t\in\RR$ for which both
$$
S\cap(-\infty,t)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
S\cap(t,\infty)
$$
are uncountable.

::: pf-proof

This is the negation of the assumption contradicted in step [](#s5){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s6){.pf-ref} is the required conclusion.

:::
