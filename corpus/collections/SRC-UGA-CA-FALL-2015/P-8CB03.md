::: {.problem}
Evaluate the integral
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^4}\,dx.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}
This is a problem of the University of Georgia complex-analysis qualifying examination of Fall 2015 (`assets/attachments/Complex_Analysis_Fall_2015.docx`). No published solution set is transcribed: the argument is the card's own, and the value it reaches was checked numerically (the integral is $0.7721380044$, the closed form $0.7721380048$).

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #contour}
With $F(z)=\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^{4}}$ and $\Gamma_{R}$ the boundary of the upper half disc of radius $R>1$, $\int_{\Gamma_{R}}F\,dz=\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^{4}}\,dx+\int_{\gamma_{R}}F\,dz$.

::: pf-proof
$\Gamma_{R}$ is the union of the interval $[-R,R]$ on the real axis and the upper semicircle $\gamma_{R}$ of radius $R$. $F$ is meromorphic on $\CC$ with poles only at the fourth roots of $-1$, all of modulus $1$, so for $R>1$ no pole lies on $\Gamma_{R}$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #arc}
$\int_{\gamma_{R}}F\,dz\to0$ as $R\to\infty$.

::: pf-proof
On $\gamma_{R}$ one has $\abs{e^{iz}}=e^{-\operatorname{Im}z}\leq1$ and $\abs{1+z^{4}}\geq\abs{z}^{4}-1=R^{4}-1$, so $\abs{F}\leq\frac{1}{R^{4}-1}$ there, and the length of $\gamma_{R}$ is $\pi R$; hence $\abs{\int_{\gamma_{R}}F\,dz}\leq\frac{\pi R}{R^{4}-1}\to0$. This is the estimate behind Jordan's lemma in the case at hand, and it suffices because $F$ is already dominated by $R^{-4}$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #poles}
The poles of $F$ in the upper half plane are $z_{1}=e^{i\pi/4}$, $z_{2}=e^{3i\pi/4}$, both simple, with $\operatorname{Res}(F,z_{k})=\frac{e^{iz_{k}}}{4z_{k}^{3}}$.

::: pf-proof
$1+z^{4}=0$ exactly at the four numbers $e^{i(\pi+2\pi j)/4}$, of which those in the upper half plane are $e^{i\pi/4}$ and $e^{3i\pi/4}$. The derivative of $1+z^{4}$ is $4z^{3}$, nonzero at each of them, so each is a simple pole, and the residue of $e^{iz}/(1+z^{4})$ at a simple pole is $e^{iz_{k}}/(4z_{k}^{3})$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #sum}
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^{4}}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}e^{-a}(\cos a+\sin a)$, where $a=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$.

::: pf-proof
The residue theorem gives $\int_{\Gamma_{R}}F\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{k=1}^{2}\operatorname{Res}(F,z_{k})$ for $R>1$, and steps [](#contour){.pf-ref} and [](#arc){.pf-ref} let $R\to\infty$. Put $a=1/\sqrt2$, so $z_{1}=a(1+i)$ and $z_{2}=a(i-1)$. Then $z_{1}^{3}=a(i-1)$ and $z_{2}^{3}=a(1+i)$, so
$$\frac{1}{z_{1}^{3}}=-\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2},\qquad\frac{1}{z_{2}^{3}}=\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2},$$
and $e^{iz_{1}}=e^{-a}e^{ia}$, $e^{iz_{2}}=e^{-a}e^{-ia}$. Hence
$$\sum_{k=1}^{2}\operatorname{Res}(F,z_{k})=\frac{e^{-a}}{4\sqrt2}\Big((1-i)e^{-ia}-(1+i)e^{ia}\Big).$$
Now $(1-i)e^{-ia}-(1+i)e^{ia}=-2i(\sin a+\cos a)$, by expanding both products, so the sum is $-\frac{ie^{-a}}{2\sqrt2}(\cos a+\sin a)$ and
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^{4}}\,dx=2\pi i\cdot\left(-\frac{ie^{-a}}{2\sqrt2}(\cos a+\sin a)\right)=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}e^{-a}(\cos a+\sin a).$$
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #real}
$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{1+x^{4}}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt2}e^{-1/\sqrt2}\left(\cos\frac{1}{\sqrt2}+\sin\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$.

::: pf-proof
The imaginary part $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{1+x^{4}}\,dx$ vanishes because the integrand is odd, so the real integral equals the value of step [](#sum){.pf-ref}; and $\frac{\cos x}{1+x^{4}}$ is even, so the integral over $[0,\infty)$ is half of the integral over $\RR$.
:::
