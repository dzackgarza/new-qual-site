::: problem
Let $F$ be a field. Is the group $\operatorname{SL}_2(F)$
of $2\times2$ matrices over $F$ with determinant $1$ simple?
:::

::: remark
The answer depends on the field. The group here is
$\operatorname{SL}_2(F)$, not its quotient by scalar matrices.
In particular, the nontrivial central subgroup $\{I,-I\}$
already obstructs simplicity when the characteristic is not two.
:::

::: solution
The exact criterion is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{SL}_2(F)\text{ is simple}
\iff \operatorname{char}F=2\text{ and }|F|>2.}
$$
The proof covers infinite fields as well as finite ones.

<1>1. The group is not simple when $\operatorname{char}F\ne2$,
or when $F=\mathbb F_2$.

::: proof
If the characteristic is not two, the subgroup $\{I,-I\}$
is central, nontrivial, and proper: the matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
has determinant one and is not scalar. A central subgroup
is normal, so this disproves simplicity.

Over $\mathbb F_2$, every invertible matrix has determinant
one. Its first column can be any of the three nonzero vectors,
and its second column can be either of the two vectors not
in the span of the first. Thus the group has six elements.
Its action on the three nonzero vectors is faithful, because
fixing all vectors fixes the standard basis. It therefore
identifies the group with $S_3$. The subgroup $A_3$ is
nontrivial, proper, and normal, as the kernel of sign [@DF04].
:::

For the rest of the proof assume $\operatorname{char}F=2$
and $|F|>2$, and write $G=\operatorname{SL}_2(F)$.
Set
$$
u(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\t&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
h(a)=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
$$
for $t\in F$ and $a\in F^\times$, and put $U=\{u(t):t\in F\}$.

<1>2. The conjugates of the abelian subgroup $U$ generate $G$.

::: proof
One has $u(t)u(s)=u(t+s)$, so $U$ is abelian. Direct
multiplication, with signs retained in the formulas, gives
$$
w(a):=u(a)v(-a^{-1})u(a)
=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad w(a)w(-1)=h(a).
$$
Also $w(1)u(t)w(1)^{-1}=v(-t)$, so every $v(t)$
belongs to a conjugate of $U$.

Let $H$ be the subgroup generated by all $u(t)$ and $v(t)$.
It contains $w(a)$ and $h(a)$ by the displayed identities.
For $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
\in G$ with $a\ne0$, the determinant equation gives
$d=(1+bc)/a$, and multiplication verifies
$$
A=v(c/a)h(a)u(b/a).
$$
Hence $A\in H$. If $a=0$, then $c\ne0$, and the
upper-left entry of $w(1)A$ is $c$. Thus $w(1)A\in H$
by the preceding case and $A\in H$ as well. This proves
$H=G$, and therefore the conjugates of $U$ generate $G$.
:::

<1>3. The action of $G$ on the projective line is faithful
and doubly transitive, and its stabilizer of infinity normalizes $U$.

::: proof
The projective line $X$ is the set of one-dimensional
subspaces of $F^2$. Write
$\infty=F(1,0)$ and $z=F(z,1)$ for $z\in F$.
For a nonzero vector $(a,c)$, there are $b,d$ with
$ad-bc=1$: take $d=a^{-1},b=0$ when $a\ne0$, and
$b=-c^{-1},d=0$ otherwise. The corresponding matrix
sends $\infty$ to $F(a,c)$, proving transitivity.
The subgroup $U$ fixes infinity and acts on the other
points by $z\mapsto z+t$, transitively. Conjugating
this stabilizer property to any point proves double transitivity.

A matrix fixing every line must fix $F(1,0)$ and $F(0,1)$,
so is diagonal. Fixing $F(1,1)$ makes its diagonal entries
equal, so it is $aI$ with $a^2=1$. In characteristic two,
$(a-1)^2=0$ in the field forces $a=1$. Thus the action
has trivial kernel and is faithful.

The stabilizer $B=G_\infty$ consists exactly of the matrices
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\0&a^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
with $a\ne0$. Conjugation by such a matrix sends $u(t)$
to $u(a^2t)$. Hence $U\lhd B$.
:::

<1>4. The group $G$ is perfect: it equals its commutator subgroup.

::: proof
Choose $a\in F\setminus\{0,1\}$. Since the characteristic
is two, $a^2-1=(a-1)^2\ne0$. With
$[g,h]=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$, the matrix identity
$$
[h(a),u(t)]=u((a^2-1)t)
$$
shows that every element of $U$ is a commutator: for
$u(s)$ use $t=s/(a^2-1)$. The subgroup generated by
commutators is normal, because conjugation sends a
commutator to the commutator of the conjugates. It
therefore contains every conjugate of $U$, and step
<1>2 shows that it is all of $G$.
:::

<1>5. Every nontrivial normal subgroup $N\lhd G$ equals $G$.

::: proof
First $N$ acts transitively on $X$. If $N$ fixed infinity,
normality and the transitivity of $G$ would make it fix
every point: for $x=g\infty$ and $n\in N$,
$nx=g(g^{-1}ng)\infty=g\infty$. Faithfulness would then
give $N=1$, a contradiction. Thus the orbit $N\infty$
contains a point other than infinity. This orbit is
preserved by $B$, since $B$ fixes infinity and normalizes
$N$. As $B$ is transitive on $X\setminus\{\infty\}$,
the orbit must be all of $X$.

It follows that $G=NB$: given $g\in G$, choose $n\in N$
with $n\infty=g\infty$; then $n^{-1}g\in B$.
Let $\pi:G\to G/N$ be the quotient. For $g=nb$,
normality of $U$ in $B$ gives
$$
\pi(gUg^{-1})=\pi(bUb^{-1})=\pi(U).
$$
Thus all conjugates of $U$ have the same image in the
quotient. Since they generate $G$, the entire quotient
equals the abelian subgroup $\pi(U)$ and is abelian.
Every commutator therefore maps to one. By step <1>4,
$G$ is generated by commutators, so $G/N=1$, or $N=G$.

The matrix $u(1)\ne I$ proves that $G$ is nontrivial.
It has no nontrivial proper normal subgroup, so it is
simple. Together with step <1>1, this proves the criterion.
:::
:::
