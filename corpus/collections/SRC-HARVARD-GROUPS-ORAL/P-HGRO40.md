::: {.problem}
Prove that a group generated by two involutions is a dihedral group.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
\[
G=\langle a,b\rangle,
\qquad
a^2=b^2=1,
\]
and set
\[
r=ab.
\]

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

The group $G$ is generated by $r$ and $a$, with
\[
ara=r^{-1}.
\]

::: pf-proof

Since $r=ab$, we have $b=ar$, so $G=\langle r,a\rangle$. Moreover
\[
ara=a(ab)a=ba=(ab)^{-1}=r^{-1}.
\]

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

Every element of $G$ has the form $r^m$ or $ar^m$.

::: pf-proof

Using the relation
\[
ar=r^{-1}a,
\]
move every occurrence of $a$ in a word to the left. Since $a^2=1$, at most one
$a$ remains, giving one of the two stated forms.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If $r$ has finite order $n$, then $G\cong D_{2n}$; if $r$ has infinite
order, then $G$ is the infinite dihedral group.

::: pf-proof

The relations from step [](#s1){.pf-ref} give a surjective homomorphism from the corresponding
dihedral presentation
\[
\langle r,a\mid r^n=1,\ a^2=1,\ ara=r^{-1}\rangle
\]
in the finite case, or
\[
\langle r,a\mid a^2=1,\ ara=r^{-1}\rangle
\]
in the infinite case, onto $G$.

By step [](#s2){.pf-ref} there are at most $2n$ elements in the finite case. The elements
$r^m$ are distinct for $0\le m<n$, and $ar^m=r^k$ would give
$a\in\langle r\rangle$. That is impossible: if $a\in\langle r\rangle$, then
$a$ commutes with $r$, so $r^{-1}=ara=r$ and $r^2=1$; since $a\ne1$, this
forces $a=r$ and then $b=ar=r^2=1$, contradicting that $b$ is an involution.
Hence the $2n$ normal forms are distinct and $G\cong D_{2n}$. The same
normal-form argument gives the infinite dihedral group when $r$ has infinite
order.

:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.remark}
The smallest cases are $n=1$, when $a=b$ and $G\cong D_2\cong C_2$, and
$n=2$, when $a\ne b$ commute and $G\cong D_4\cong C_2\times C_2$.
:::
