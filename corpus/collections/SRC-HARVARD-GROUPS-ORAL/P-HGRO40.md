::: problem
Prove that a group generated by two involutions is a dihedral group.
:::

::: solution
Let
\[
G=\langle a,b\rangle,
\qquad
a^2=b^2=1,
\]
and set
\[
r=ab.
\]

<1>1. The group $G$ is generated by $r$ and $a$, with
\[
ara=r^{-1}.
\]
::: proof
Since $r=ab$, we have $b=ar$, so $G=\langle r,a\rangle$. Moreover
\[
ara=a(ab)a=ba=(ab)^{-1}=r^{-1}.
\]
:::

<1>2. Every element of $G$ has the form $r^m$ or $ar^m$.
::: proof
Using the relation
\[
ar=r^{-1}a,
\]
move every occurrence of $a$ in a word to the left. Since $a^2=1$, at most one
$a$ remains, giving one of the two stated forms.
:::

<1>3. If $r$ has finite order $n$, then $G\cong D_{2n}$; if $r$ has infinite
order, then $G$ is the infinite dihedral group.
::: proof
The relations from <1>1 give a surjective homomorphism from the corresponding
dihedral presentation
\[
\langle r,a\mid r^n=1,\ a^2=1,\ ara=r^{-1}\rangle
\]
in the finite case, or
\[
\langle r,a\mid a^2=1,\ ara=r^{-1}\rangle
\]
in the infinite case, onto $G$.

By <1>2 there are at most $2n$ elements in the finite case. The elements
$r^m$ are distinct for $0\le m<n$, and no $ar^m$ can equal an $r^k$ unless
$a\in\langle r\rangle$. If $a\in\langle r\rangle$, then also
$b=ar\in\langle r\rangle$ and $G$ is cyclic; this is only the degenerate
dihedral case. Otherwise the $2n$ normal forms are distinct. The same normal-form
argument gives the standard infinite dihedral group when $r$ has infinite order.
:::

Thus, apart from the usual degenerate cyclic case when the two involutions do not
generate distinct reflections, a group generated by two involutions is dihedral.
:::
