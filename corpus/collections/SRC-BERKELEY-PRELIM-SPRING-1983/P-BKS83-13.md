::: {.problem}
Let $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots$ be real numbers. Show that
\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{e^{i\lambda_nx}}{n^2}
\]
converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$ to a continuous function $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$, and prove that the limit
\[
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f(x)\,dx
\]
exists.
:::

::: {.solution}
::: pf

::: {.pf-step #series-converges-uniformly}
The series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{e^{i\lambda_nx}}{n^2}
$$
converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

::: pf-proof
For every $x\in\mathbb R$ and every $n$,
$$
\left|\frac{e^{i\lambda_nx}}{n^2}\right|
=
\frac1{n^2}.
$$
Since
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2}<\infty,
$$
the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence on all of $\mathbb R$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #f-is-continuous}
The sum $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$.

::: pf-proof
Every partial sum is continuous, being a finite sum of continuous
exponentials. By step [](#series-converges-uniformly){.pf-ref}, these partial sums converge uniformly to $f$.
A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #finite-mean-formula}
For $T>0$,
$$
\frac1{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f(x)\,dx
=
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{a_n(T)}{n^2},
$$
where
$$
a_n(T)
\coloneqq
\begin{cases}
1,&\lambda_n=0,\\[2mm]
\dfrac{\sin(\lambda_nT)}{\lambda_nT},&\lambda_n\ne0.
\end{cases}
$$
In every case,
$$
\abs{a_n(T)}\le1.
$$

::: pf-proof
On the compact interval $[-T,T]$, the series defining $f$ converges
uniformly by step [](#series-converges-uniformly){.pf-ref}, so it may be integrated term by term. If
$\lambda_n=0$, then
$$
\frac1{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{i\lambda_nx}\,dx=1.
$$
If $\lambda_n\ne0$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{i\lambda_nx}\,dx
&=
\frac{e^{i\lambda_nT}-e^{-i\lambda_nT}}
{2Ti\lambda_n}\\
&=
\frac{\sin(\lambda_nT)}{\lambda_nT}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Finally, $\abs{\sin u}\le\abs u$ gives
$\abs{a_n(T)}\le1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #mean-limit-boxed}
The mean value has the limit
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f(x)\,dx
=
\sum_{\{n:\lambda_n=0\}}\frac1{n^2}
}.
$$

::: pf-proof
Set
$$
L\coloneqq
\sum_{\{n:\lambda_n=0\}}\frac1{n^2},
$$
which converges absolutely. Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ such that
$$
\sum_{n>N}\frac1{n^2}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.
$$
For every index $n\le N$ with $\lambda_n\ne0$,
$$
a_n(T)=\frac{\sin(\lambda_nT)}{\lambda_nT}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Since only finitely many such indices occur, there is $T_0$ such that for
$T\ge T_0$,
$$
\left|
\sum_{\substack{n\le N\\\lambda_n\ne0}}
\frac{a_n(T)}{n^2}
\right|
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
The terms with $\lambda_n=0$ already equal their limiting values. By
step [](#finite-mean-formula){.pf-ref}, the remaining tail differs from its limiting zero-frequency
tail by at most
$$
\sum_{n>N}\frac{\abs{a_n(T)}}{n^2}
+
\sum_{\substack{n>N\\\lambda_n=0}}\frac1{n^2}
\le
2\sum_{n>N}\frac1{n^2}
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
Therefore the absolute difference between the mean and $L$ is less than
$\varepsilon$ for $T\ge T_0$.
:::

limit.
