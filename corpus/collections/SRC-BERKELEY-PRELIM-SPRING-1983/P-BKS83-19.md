::: {.problem}
Show that the initial-value problem
\[
x'=1+5\cos x,
\qquad
x(0)=7
\]
has a solution defined on all of $\mathbb R$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
$$
F(u)\coloneqq1+5\cos u.
$$

<1>1. The initial-value problem has a unique local solution, hence a unique
maximal solution
$$
x:(\alpha,\beta)\longrightarrow\mathbb R
$$
with
$$
\alpha<0<\beta.
$$

::: {.proof}
The function $F$ is continuously differentiable and
$$
\abs{F'(u)}
=
5\abs{\sin u}
\le5.
$$
Thus $F$ is globally Lipschitz. The Picard--Lindelof theorem gives a
unique local solution through $(0,7)$, which extends uniquely to a maximal
open interval $(\alpha,\beta)$.
:::

<1>2. On its maximal interval, the solution satisfies
$$
\abs{x(t)-x(s)}\le6\abs{t-s}
$$
for all $s,t\in(\alpha,\beta)$.

::: {.proof}
Since
$$
\abs{F(u)}
=
\abs{1+5\cos u}
\le6,
$$
the differential equation gives $\abs{x'(t)}\le6$. The mean value theorem
then yields the displayed estimate.
:::

<1>3. The right endpoint satisfies
$$
\beta=+\infty.
$$

::: {.proof}
Suppose instead that $\beta<\infty$. By step <1>2, the values $x(t)$ form
a Cauchy family as $t\uparrow\beta$: for $s,t$ near $\beta$,
$$
\abs{x(t)-x(s)}
\le
6\abs{t-s}.
$$
Completeness of $\mathbb R$ therefore gives a finite limit
$$
L\coloneqq\lim_{t\uparrow\beta}x(t).
$$
Applying the local existence theorem at the point $(\beta,L)$ gives a
solution on an interval containing $\beta$. By local uniqueness, it agrees
with the original solution on their common interval to the left of
$\beta$, and hence extends the maximal solution past $\beta$. This
contradicts maximality.
:::

<1>4. The left endpoint satisfies
$$
\alpha=-\infty.
$$

::: {.proof}
If $\alpha>-\infty$, the same estimate in step <1>2 shows that
$x(t)$ has a finite limit as $t\downarrow\alpha$. Local existence through
that limiting point, together with local uniqueness, then extends the
solution to times smaller than $\alpha$, again contradicting maximality.
:::

<1>5. The prescribed initial-value problem has a solution defined on all
of
$$
\boxed{\mathbb R}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>3 and <1>4 show that the maximal interval in step <1>1 is
$$
(\alpha,\beta)=\mathbb R.
$$
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 is the required global-existence statement.
:::
:::
