::: {.problem}
Let $F = \FF_p$, where $p$ is a prime number.

(a) Show that if $\pi(x) \in \FF_p[x]$ is irreducible of degree $d$, then $\pi(x)$ divides $x^{p^d} - x$ in $\FF_p[x]$.

(b) Show that if $\pi(x) \in \FF_p[x]$ is an irreducible polynomial that divides $x^{p^n} - x$, then $\deg \pi(x)$ divides $n$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

Every irreducible polynomial $\pi\in\FF_p[x]$ of degree $d$ divides $x^{p^d}-x$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step

Construct the field $K = \FF_p[x]/\langle \pi(x) \rangle$. Since $\pi(x)$ is irreducible over $\FF_p$, $\langle \pi(x) \rangle$ is a maximal ideal, so $K$ is a field.

::: pf-proof
$\FF_p[x]$ is a principal ideal domain, and in a principal ideal domain the ideal generated by a nonzero element is maximal exactly when that element is irreducible; a quotient by a maximal ideal is a field. The ideal is proper because $\pi$ is nonconstant.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

The extension degree is $[K : \FF_p] = \deg \pi(x) = d$, so $\cardinality{K} = p^d$.

::: pf-proof
The classes of $1,x,\dots,x^{d-1}$ form an $\FF_p$-basis of $K$: they span because division with remainder writes every class as a representative of degree $<d$, and they are independent because a nontrivial $\FF_p$-linear relation of degree $<d$ vanishing modulo $\pi$ would be a nonzero multiple of $\pi$ of degree $<d$. So the dimension is $d$, and a $d$-dimensional vector space over the $p$-element field has $p^d$ elements.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

The multiplicative group $K^\times = K \setminus \{0\}$ is a group of order $p^d - 1$.

::: pf-proof
$K$ is a field, so its nonzero elements are closed under multiplication and inversion and form a group under multiplication; and $K$ has $p^d$ elements of which exactly one, $0$, is excluded.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

By Lagrange's Theorem (or Euler's Theorem for finite groups), every element $\alpha \in K^\times$ satisfies $\alpha^{p^d - 1} = 1$, which implies $\alpha^{p^d} = \alpha$.

::: pf-proof
Lagrange's theorem says the order of an element divides the order of the group, so $\alpha^{p^d-1}=1$ for $\alpha\in K^\times$; multiplying by $\alpha$ gives $\alpha^{p^d}=\alpha$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

For $\alpha = 0$, $0^{p^d} - 0 = 0$. Thus $\alpha^{p^d} - \alpha = 0$ for all $\alpha \in K$.

::: pf-proof
$0^{p^d}=0$ since $p^d\ge1$, so the identity holds at $0$ as well; together with step 1.4 for the nonzero elements this covers all of $K$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Consider the residue class $\bar{x} = x + \langle \pi(x) \rangle \in K$.

::: pf-proof
$\bar x$ is the image of $x$ under the quotient map $\FF_p[x]\to K$, hence an element of $K$; it is the element that generates $K$ over $\FF_p$, since $\FF_p(\bar x)=K$ by step 1.2.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Applying the polynomial identity to $\bar{x}$ gives
    $$\bar{x}^{p^d} - \bar{x} = 0 \quad \text{in } K = \FF_p[x]/\langle \pi(x) \rangle.$$

::: pf-proof
Step 1.5 gives $\alpha^{p^d}=\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in K$; take $\alpha=\bar x$, which lies in $K$ by step 1.6. The powers are computed in $K$, and $\bar x^{p^d}$ is the class of $x^{p^d}$ because the quotient map is a ring homomorphism.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

By definition of the quotient ring, this means $x^{p^d} - x \in \langle \pi(x) \rangle$.

::: pf-proof
In $\FF_p[x]/(\pi)$ two elements have the same class exactly when their difference lies in the ideal $(\pi)$; the left side is the class of $x^{p^d}-x$ and the right side the class of $0$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore $\pi(x)$ divides $x^{p^d} - x$ in $\FF_p[x]$.

::: pf-proof
The ideal $(\pi)$ consists exactly of the multiples of $\pi$ in the principal ideal domain $\FF_p[x]$, so membership of $x^{p^d}-x$ in it is the divisibility asserted.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

If an irreducible $\pi\in\FF_p[x]$ divides $x^{p^n}-x$, then $\deg\pi\mid n$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step

Let $L = \FF_{p^n}$ be the splitting field of $x^{p^n} - x$ over $\FF_p$, which is a finite field of degree $[L : \FF_p] = n$.

::: pf-proof
The derivative of $x^{p^n}-x$ is $p^nx^{p^n-1}-1=-1$ in characteristic $p$, hence nonzero and coprime to the polynomial; so the polynomial has $p^n$ distinct roots in its splitting field $L$. A splitting field of a polynomial of degree $p^n$ over $\FF_p$ is generated by its roots, so $L$ is finite of order $p^n$, and a finite field of order $p^n$ has degree $n$ over its prime field.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

The elements of $L$ are precisely the $p^n$ distinct roots of $x^{p^n} - x$.

::: pf-proof
The roots lie in $L$ by definition of splitting field, and there are $p^n$ of them by step 2.1 while $L$ has $p^n$ elements, so the root set is all of $L$. Equivalently each $a\in L$ satisfies $a^{p^n}=a$ because $a=0$ or $a\in L^\times$ of order dividing $p^n-1$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Since $\pi(x) \mid (x^{p^n} - x)$ in $\FF_p[x]$, every root of $\pi(x)$ in an algebraic closure $\overline{\FF}_p$ is also a root of $x^{p^n} - x$, and therefore lies in $L$.

::: pf-proof
If $x^{p^n}-x=\pi(x)\cdot q(x)$ and $\alpha$ is a root of $\pi$, then substituting $\alpha$ makes the left side vanish, so $\alpha$ is a root of $x^{p^n}-x$; by step 2.2 every such root lies in $L$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let $\alpha \in L$ be a root of $\pi(x)$.

::: pf-proof
$\pi$ has a root in $\overline{\FF}_p$ because every nonconstant polynomial splits there, and step 2.3 places every root of $\pi$ in $L$; so choosing one gives an element $\alpha\in L$ with $\pi(\alpha)=0$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Since $\pi(x)$ is irreducible over $\FF_p$, it is a scalar multiple of the minimal polynomial $m_{\alpha, \FF_p}(x)$, so
    $$[\FF_p(\alpha) : \FF_p] = \deg \pi(x).$$

::: pf-proof
The minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\FF_p$ is the monic polynomial of least degree vanishing at $\alpha$, and it divides every polynomial over $\FF_p$ vanishing at $\alpha$; it is irreducible. Since $\pi(\alpha)=0$ and $\pi$ is irreducible, $\pi$ is a scalar multiple of $m_{\alpha,\FF_p}$. The degree of the extension $\FF_p(\alpha)/\FF_p$ equals the degree of the minimal polynomial.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Since $\alpha \in L$, we have the tower of subfields $\FF_p \subseteq \FF_p(\alpha) \subseteq L$.

::: pf-proof
$\FF_p\subseteq L$ because $L$ is an $\FF_p$-algebra; $\FF_p(\alpha)$ is by definition the smallest subfield of $\overline{\FF}_p$ containing $\FF_p$ and $\alpha$, and it is contained in $L$ because $L$ is a subfield of $\overline{\FF}_p$ containing both.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

By the Tower Law for field extensions:
    $$n = [L : \FF_p] = [L : \FF_p(\alpha)] \cdot [\FF_p(\alpha) : \FF_p] = [L : \FF_p(\alpha)] \cdot \deg \pi(x).$$

::: pf-proof
The tower law says that for a tower $F\subseteq E\subseteq L$ of finite extensions the degrees multiply; all three are finite here, the outer one by step 2.1 and the inner one by step 2.5. Substituting $[L:\FF_p]=n$ and $[\FF_p(\alpha):\FF_p]=\deg\pi$ gives the displayed equation.
:::

:::

::: pf-step

Since $[L : \FF_p(\alpha)]$ is an integer, $\deg \pi(x)$ divides $n$.

::: pf-proof
The displayed equation of step 2.7 exhibits $n$ as the product of the positive integer $[L:\FF_p(\alpha)]$ and $\deg\pi$, which is the definition of divisibility.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Step [](#s1){.pf-ref} proves part (a), and step [](#s2){.pf-ref} proves part (b).

::: pf-proof
Part (a) is steps 1.1 to 1.9: the quotient by $\pi$ is a field of $p^d$ elements, its elements satisfy $a^{p^d}=a$, and applying that to the class of $x$ gives the divisibility. Part (b) is steps 2.1 to 2.8: the splitting field of $x^{p^n}-x$ has $p^n$ elements and contains every root of $\pi$, and the tower law then divides $n$ by $\deg\pi$.
:::
