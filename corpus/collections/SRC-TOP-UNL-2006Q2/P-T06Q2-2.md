::: {.problem}
(a) Define what it means for a topological space $X$ to be **normal**.
(b) Prove that a closed subspace $Y \subseteq X$ of a normal space $X$ is normal.
(c) Suppose that $X$ is a normal space, $C \subseteq X$ is closed, and $C \subseteq O_1 \cup O_2$ where $O_1, O_2 \subseteq X$ are open.
Prove that there exist closed sets $C_1, C_2 \subseteq X$ with $C = C_1 \cup C_2$ and $C_i \subseteq O_i$ for $i = 1, 2$.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) A space $X$ is normal if any two disjoint closed subsets $A,B\subseteq X$ admit disjoint open neighborhoods.

For (b), let $Y\subseteq X$ be closed and let $A,B\subseteq Y$ be disjoint and closed in $Y$. Since $Y$ is closed in $X$, the sets $A$ and $B$ are closed in $X$. By normality of $X$, choose disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ with $A\subseteq U$ and $B\subseteq V$. Then $U\cap Y$ and $V\cap Y$ are disjoint open neighborhoods of $A$ and $B$ in $Y$. Hence $Y$ is normal.

For (c), set
\[
A=C\setminus O_2,
\qquad
B=C\setminus O_1.
\]
Because $C$ is closed and $O_1,O_2$ are open, $A$ and $B$ are closed in $X$. Moreover
\[
A\cap B=C\setminus(O_1\cup O_2)=\varnothing.
\]
By normality choose disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ with
\[
A\subseteq U,
\qquad
B\subseteq V.
\]
Define
\[
C_1=C\setminus V,
\qquad
C_2=C\setminus U.
\]
Both are closed in $X$. Since $C\setminus O_1=B\subseteq V$, one has $C_1\subseteq O_1$; similarly $C_2\subseteq O_2$. Finally,
\[
C_1\cup C_2
=(C\setminus V)\cup(C\setminus U)
=C\setminus(U\cap V)=C.
\]
Thus $C=C_1\cup C_2$ with $C_i\subseteq O_i$.
::: {.proof}
Explicitly, whenever $A,B\subseteq X$ are closed and
\[
A\cap B=\varnothing,
\]
there exist open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ such that
\[
A\subseteq U,
\qquad
B\subseteq V,
\qquad
U\cap V=\varnothing.
\]
This is the definition required in part (a).
:::

<1>2. If $Y\subseteq X$ is closed and $X$ is normal, then $Y$ is normal in the subspace topology.
::: {.proof}
Let $A,B\subseteq Y$ be disjoint and closed in $Y$.
Because $Y$ is closed in $X$, every subset closed in $Y$ is also closed in $X$.
Hence $A$ and $B$ are disjoint closed subsets of $X$.

By normality of $X$, there are disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ satisfying
\[
A\subseteq U,
\qquad
B\subseteq V.
\]
Then
\[
U\cap Y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
V\cap Y
\]
are open in $Y$, contain $A$ and $B$ respectively, and are disjoint because
\[
(U\cap Y)\cap(V\cap Y)=(U\cap V)\cap Y=\varnothing.
\]
Thus $Y$ is normal, proving part (b).
:::

Now suppose $X$ is normal, $C\subseteq X$ is closed, and
\[
C\subseteq O_1\cup O_2
\]
for open sets $O_1,O_2\subseteq X$.

<1>3. The sets
\[
A=C\setminus O_2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
B=C\setminus O_1
\]
are disjoint closed subsets of $X$, with
\[
A\subseteq O_1,
\qquad
B\subseteq O_2.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $C$ is closed and $X\setminus O_i$ is closed,
\[
A=C\cap(X\setminus O_2),
\qquad
B=C\cap(X\setminus O_1)
\]
are closed in $X$.
Moreover,
\[
A\cap B
=
C\setminus(O_1\cup O_2)
=
\varnothing
\]
because $C\subseteq O_1\cup O_2$.

If $x\in A$, then $x\in C$ and $x\notin O_2$; since every point of $C$ lies in $O_1\cup O_2$, necessarily $x\in O_1$.
Thus $A\subseteq O_1$.
The proof that $B\subseteq O_2$ is symmetric.
:::

<1>4. There are disjoint open sets $U,V\subseteq X$ such that
\[
A\subseteq U,
\qquad
B\subseteq V.
\]
::: {.proof}
Apply normality of $X$ to the disjoint closed sets $A$ and $B$ from <1>3.
:::

<1>5. Define
\[
C_1=C\setminus V,
\qquad
C_2=C\setminus U.
\]
Then $C_1$ and $C_2$ are closed in $X$ and satisfy
\[
C_1\subseteq O_1,
\qquad
C_2\subseteq O_2.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $U$ and $V$ are open and $C$ is closed,
\[
C_1=C\cap(X\setminus V),
\qquad
C_2=C\cap(X\setminus U)
\]
are closed in $X$.

By <1>3 and <1>4,
\[
C\setminus O_1=B\subseteq V.
\]
Therefore any point of $C\setminus V=C_1$ must lie in $O_1$, so $C_1\subseteq O_1$.
Similarly,
\[
C\setminus O_2=A\subseteq U
\]
implies $C_2=C\setminus U\subseteq O_2$.
:::

<1>6. The closed sets $C_1,C_2$ cover $C$:
\[
C=C_1\cup C_2.
\]
::: {.proof}
Since $U\cap V=\varnothing$ by <1>4,
\[
C_1\cup C_2
=(C\setminus V)\cup(C\setminus U)
=
C\setminus(U\cap V)
=
C.
\]
Together with <1>5, this proves part (c).
:::
:::
