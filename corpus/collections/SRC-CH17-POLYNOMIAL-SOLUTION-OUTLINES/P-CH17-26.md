::: {.problem}
Find all zeros of $f ( x ) = 3 x ^ { 2 } + x + 4$ over $\mathbb { Z } _ { 7 }$ by substitution.
Find all zeros of $f ( x )$ by using the quadratic formula.
Do your answers agree?
Should they?
Find all zeros of $g ( x ) = 2 x ^ { 2 } + x + 3$ over $\mathbb { Z } _ { 5 }$ by substitution.
Try the quadratic formula on $g ( x )$ . Do your answers agree?
State necessary and sufficient conditions for the quadratic formula to yield the zeros of a quadratic from $\mathbb { Z } _ { p } [ x ]$ , where p is a prime greater than 2.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The zeros of $f=3x^2+x+4$ over $\mathbb F_7$ are
\[
\boxed{x=4,\quad x=5}.
\]

::: {.proof}
Substitution at $x=0,1,\ldots,6$ gives the respective values $4,1,4,6,0,0,6$ in $\mathbb F_7$. Thus the only zeros are $4,5$. For the quadratic formula, the discriminant is $1-4(3)(4)=-47=2$ in $\mathbb F_7$, and $3^2=2$. Since the inverse of $6$ modulo $7$ is $6$, the formula gives
\[
x=\frac{-1\pm3}{6}\in\{5,4\}.
\]
The two methods agree because the formula follows from an equivalence of equations in any field of characteristic different from two with nonzero leading coefficient.
:::

<1>2. The polynomial $g=2x^2+x+3$ has no zero in $\mathbb F_5$.

::: {.proof}
Direct substitution at $0,1,2,3,4$ gives $3,1,3,4,4$, none equal to zero modulo $5$. Its discriminant is $1-4(2)(3)=-23=2$ in $\mathbb F_5$. The squares in this field are $0,1,4$, so the quadratic formula has no square root of its discriminant in $\mathbb F_5$, in agreement with substitution.
:::

<1>3. For any odd prime $p$ and $a,b,c\in\mathbb F_p$ with $a\neq0$, the quadratic $ax^2+bx+c$ has a root in $\mathbb F_p$ if and only if its discriminant $\Delta=b^2-4ac$ is a square in $\mathbb F_p$, including zero; in that case all roots are
\[
\boxed{x=(-b\pm d)(2a)^{-1}\quad\text{where }d^2=\Delta}.
\]

::: {.proof}
Because $p>2$ and $a\neq0$, both $4a$ and $2a$ are invertible. Completing the square gives the identity
\[
4a(ax^2+bx+c)=(2ax+b)^2-\Delta.
\]
Consequently $ax^2+bx+c=0$ if and only if $(2ax+b)^2=\Delta$. Such an $x$ exists precisely when $\Delta=d^2$ for some $d\in\mathbb F_p$, and the latter equation is equivalent to $2ax+b=\pm d$. Multiplication by $(2a)^{-1}$ gives exactly the displayed roots. If $\Delta=0$, the two displayed expressions coincide.
:::

<1>4. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1 and <1>2 solve the two specific equations by both methods. Step <1>3 supplies the necessary and sufficient condition for a genuine quadratic over any field $\mathbb F_p$ of odd characteristic.
:::
:::
