::: {.problem}
Find all zeros of $f(x) = 3x^{2} + x + 4$ over $\ZZ_{7}$ by substitution.
Find all zeros of $f(x)$ by using the quadratic formula.
Do your answers agree?
Should they?
Find all zeros of $g(x) = 2x^{2} + x + 3$ over $\ZZ_{5}$ by substitution.
Try the quadratic formula on $g(x)$. Do your answers agree?
State necessary and sufficient conditions for the quadratic formula to yield the zeros of a quadratic from $\ZZ_{p}[x]$, where $p$ is a prime greater than $2$.
:::

::: {.solution}

The argument is the card's own: substitution and the quadratic formula are carried out in $\ZZ_{7}$ and $\ZZ_{5}$, and the general criterion is that the discriminant be a square.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #substitution}

$f(x)=3x^{2}+x+4$ has exactly the zeros $4$ and $5$ in $\ZZ_{7}$.

::: pf-proof

Compute $f$ on all seven classes: $f(0)=4$, $f(1)=8\equiv1$, $f(2)=12+2+4=18\equiv4$, $f(3)=27+3+4=34\equiv6$, $f(4)=48+4+4=56\equiv0$, $f(5)=75+5+4=84\equiv0$, $f(6)=108+6+4=118\equiv6$. So the zeros are $4$ and $5$, and there are no others since $\ZZ_{7}$ is a field and a quadratic has at most two roots.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #formula}

The quadratic formula gives the same zeros.

::: pf-proof

Here $a=3$, $b=1$, $c=4$, so $\Delta=b^{2}-4ac=1-48=-47\equiv2\pmod 7$ and $2a=6$ is invertible with inverse $6$ (as $6\cdot6=36\equiv1$). Since $3^{2}=9\equiv2$ and $4^{2}=16\equiv2$, the square roots of $\Delta$ are $\pm3$. Then
$$
x=(-1\pm3)\cdot6^{-1}=(6\pm3)\cdot6,
$$
giving $x=(6+3)\cdot6=9\cdot6=54\equiv5$ for one sign and $x=(6-3)\cdot6=3\cdot6=18\equiv4$ for the other. So the roots are $4$ and $5$, agreeing with step [](#substitution){.pf-ref}. They should agree, because $2a\ne0$ is invertible in $\ZZ_{7}$ and completing the square is an equivalence.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #nosquare}

$g(x)=2x^{2}+x+3$ has no zeros in $\ZZ_{5}$, and the formula confirms it.

::: pf-proof

$g(0)=3$, $g(1)=6\equiv1$, $g(2)=8+2+3=13\equiv3$, $g(3)=18+3+3=24\equiv4$, $g(4)=32+4+3=39\equiv4$; no value is $0$. For the formula, $\Delta=1-4\cdot2\cdot3=1-24=-23\equiv2\pmod 5$, and $2a=4$ is invertible; the squares in $\ZZ_{5}$ are $0,1,4$, so $2$ has no square root and the formula yields no zeros. The two routes agree.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #criterion}

For $ax^{2}+bx+c$ with $a\ne0$ and $p>2$ prime, the formula yields the zeros exactly when $\Delta=b^{2}-4ac$ is a square in $\ZZ_{p}$.

::: pf-proof

$2a\ne0$ and $\ZZ_{p}$ is a field, so $2a$ is invertible and
$$
ax^{2}+bx+c=0\iff x^{2}+\tfrac ba x+\tfrac ca=0\iff\Bigl(x+\tfrac{b}{2a}\Bigr)^{2}=\frac{b^{2}-4ac}{4a^{2}},
$$
using that $4a^{2}$ is invertible. A square in $\ZZ_{p}$ equals $\Delta/(4a^{2})$ exactly when $\Delta$ is a square, since $4a^{2}$ is a nonzero square and the squares form a subgroup of $\ZZ_{p}^{\times}$. Hence a solution exists exactly when $\Delta$ has a square root, in which case $x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$ gives both roots. For $p=2$ the argument fails since $2a=0$, and for $a=0$ the equation is not quadratic:
$$
\boxed{\text{roots exist}\iff b^{2}-4ac\ \text{is a square in } \ZZ_{p}\ (p>2,\ a\ne0).}
$$

:::
