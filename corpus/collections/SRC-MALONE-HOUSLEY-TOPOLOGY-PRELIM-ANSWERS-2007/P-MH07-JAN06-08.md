::: {.problem}
State the tubular neighborhood theorem for a compact submanifold $X$. Give an outline of the proof, including details of the step showing that the constructed map is a diffeomorphism.
:::

::: {.solution}

Assume
$$
X^k
\subset
\RR^n
$$
is a compact embedded smooth submanifold.
Let
$$
\nu(X)
\coloneqq
\{
(x,v)
:
x\in X,
\ v\in(T_xX)^\perp
\}
$$
be its normal bundle.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Tubular neighborhood theorem.


::: pf-proof

There exists
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
such that the map
$$
\boxed{
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
\RR^n,
\qquad
\Phi(x,v)=x+v,
}
$$
where
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\coloneqq
\{
(x,v)\in\nu(X):
\norm{v}<\varepsilon
\},
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood
$$
U
\subset
\RR^n
$$
of $X$.

Under this diffeomorphism, the zero section
$$
x\longmapsto(x,0)
$$
maps to $X$ itself.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

The normal bundle $\nu(X)$ is a smooth vector bundle of rank
$$
n-k.
$$


::: pf-proof

The Euclidean metric identifies the normal space at $x$ as
$$
\nu_xX
\coloneqq
(T_xX)^\perp.
$$
In a local frame for $TX$, orthogonal projection onto the complement varies smoothly with $x$.
Thus the spaces
$$
\nu_xX
$$
assemble into a smooth rank-$(n-k)$ vector bundle over $X$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-3}

Define
$$
\Phi:\nu(X)\to\RR^n,
\qquad
\Phi(x,v)=x+v.
$$


::: pf-proof

The map is smooth because in local bundle coordinates it is ordinary vector addition in
$$
\RR^n.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-4}

At a point
$$
(x,0)
$$
of the zero section, there is a canonical splitting
$$
T_{(x,0)}\nu(X)
\cong
T_xX
\oplus
\nu_xX.
$$


::: pf-proof

Along the zero section of a vector bundle, tangent vectors split into:

$$
\text{base directions }T_xX,
$$
and
$$
\text{vertical fiber directions }\nu_xX.
$$

In any local vector-bundle trivialization
$$
\nu(X)\restrict_V
\cong
V\times\RR^{n-k},
$$
this is the ordinary product splitting at
$$
(x,0).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-5}

Under the splitting in step [](#s1-4){.pf-ref},
$$
\boxed{
d\Phi_{(x,0)}(\xi,\eta)
=
\xi+\eta.
}
$$


::: pf-proof

Varying the base point in the direction
$$
\xi\in T_xX
$$
changes
$$
x+v
$$
to first order by $\xi$.
Varying the fiber vector in the vertical direction
$$
\eta\in\nu_xX
$$
changes
$$
x+v
$$
to first order by $\eta$.
Thus the total derivative is
$$
(\xi,\eta)\longmapsto\xi+\eta.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-6}

The derivative
$$
d\Phi_{(x,0)}
$$
is an isomorphism for every
$$
x\in X.
$$


::: pf-proof

By definition of the normal space,
$$
\RR^n
=
T_xX
\oplus
\nu_xX
$$
as an orthogonal direct sum.
Therefore the addition map
$$
T_xX\oplus\nu_xX
\to
\RR^n,
\qquad
(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\xi+\eta
$$
is a linear isomorphism.
Apply step [](#s1-5){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-7}

For every
$$
x\in X,
$$
there is an open neighborhood
$$
W_x
\subset
\nu(X)
$$
of
$$
(x,0)
$$
such that
$$
\Phi\restrict_{W_x}
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset of
$$
\RR^n.
$$


::: pf-proof

By step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref},
$$
d\Phi_{(x,0)}
$$
is invertible.
The inverse function theorem therefore gives open neighborhoods
$$
(x,0)\in W_x
$$
and
$$
x\in U_x\subset\RR^n
$$
such that
$$
\Phi\restrict_{W_x}:W_x\to U_x
$$
is a diffeomorphism.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-8}

There exists
$$
\delta>0
$$
such that
$$
d\Phi_{(x,v)}
$$
is invertible whenever
$$
x\in X,
\qquad
\norm{v}<\delta.
$$


::: pf-proof

Invertibility of a linear map is an open condition.
Thus the set
$$
\mathcal O
\coloneqq
\{
(x,v)\in\nu(X):
d\Phi_{(x,v)}
\text{ is invertible}
\}
$$
is open in
$$
\nu(X)
$$
and contains the entire zero section by step [](#s1-6){.pf-ref}.

For each
$$
x\in X,
$$
choose
$$
\delta_x>0
$$
and a neighborhood
$$
V_x\subset X
$$
such that
$$
\{
(y,v):
y\in V_x,\ \norm{v}<\delta_x
\}
\subset
\mathcal O.
$$

The sets
$$
V_x
$$
cover the compact manifold $X$.
Choose a finite subcover
$$
V_{x_1},
\ldots,
V_{x_r}
$$
and let
$$
\delta
\coloneqq
\min_i\delta_{x_i}>0.
$$
Then every
$$
(x,v)
$$
with
$$
\norm{v}<\delta
$$
lies in $\mathcal O$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-9}

There exists
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
such that
$$
\Phi
$$
is injective on
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$


::: pf-proof

Suppose no such uniform radius existed.
Then for every positive integer $j$ there would be distinct points
$$
(x_j,v_j),
\qquad
(y_j,w_j)
$$
in
$$
\nu(X)
$$
such that
$$
\norm{v_j}<\frac1j,
\qquad
\norm{w_j}<\frac1j,
$$
but
$$
\Phi(x_j,v_j)
=
\Phi(y_j,w_j).
$$
Thus
$$
x_j+v_j
=
y_j+w_j,
$$
so
$$
\norm{x_j-y_j}
\le
\norm{v_j}+\norm{w_j}
\longrightarrow
0.
$$

Since $X$ is compact, after passing to a subsequence,
$$
x_j\to x
$$
for some
$$
x\in X.
$$
The preceding estimate then gives
$$
y_j\to x
$$
as well.
Also
$$
v_j\to0,
\qquad
w_j\to0.
$$
Hence
$$
(x_j,v_j)\to(x,0),
\qquad
(y_j,w_j)\to(x,0)
$$
in the total space of the normal bundle.

By step [](#s1-7){.pf-ref}, there is a neighborhood
$$
W_x
$$
of
$$
(x,0)
$$
on which $\Phi$ is injective.
For sufficiently large $j$, both
$$
(x_j,v_j)
$$
and
$$
(y_j,w_j)
$$
lie in
$$
W_x.
$$
Their images are equal, so injectivity on $W_x$ gives
$$
(x_j,v_j)
=
(y_j,w_j),
$$
contradicting their choice as distinct points.

Therefore some
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
exists for which $\Phi$ is injective on
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-10}

Shrink $\varepsilon$ if necessary so that
$$
0<\varepsilon<\delta,
$$
where $\delta$ is from step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref}.
Then
$$
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
\RR^n
$$
is both injective and a local diffeomorphism.


::: pf-proof

Injectivity follows from step [](#s1-9){.pf-ref}.
Because
$$
\varepsilon<\delta,
$$
step [](#s1-8){.pf-ref} says
$$
d\Phi
$$
is invertible at every point of
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
The inverse function theorem therefore makes $\Phi$ a local diffeomorphism everywhere on this disk bundle.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-11}

The image
$$
U
\coloneqq
\Phi
\left(
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\right)
$$
is open in
$$
\RR^n.
$$


::: pf-proof

A local diffeomorphism is an open map.
By step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref},
$$
\Phi
$$
is a local diffeomorphism on
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
Hence its image $U$ is open.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-12}

The open set $U$ contains $X$.


::: pf-proof

For every
$$
x\in X,
$$
the zero vector satisfies
$$
\norm{0}<\varepsilon,
$$
so
$$
(x,0)
\in
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
Moreover,
$$
\Phi(x,0)=x.
$$
Hence
$$
X\subseteq U.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-13}

The map
$$
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
U
$$
is a homeomorphism.


::: pf-proof

By definition of $U$, the map is surjective.
By step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref}, it is injective.
Also by step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref}, it is a local diffeomorphism and hence an open map.

A continuous bijective open map is a homeomorphism.
Therefore
$$
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
U
$$
is a homeomorphism.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-14}

The inverse
$$
\Phi^{-1}:U\to\nu_\varepsilon(X)
$$
is smooth.


::: pf-proof

Fix
$$
z\in U.
$$
Let
$$
(x,v)
=
\Phi^{-1}(z).
$$
By step [](#s1-10){.pf-ref},
$$
\Phi
$$
is a local diffeomorphism at
$$
(x,v).
$$
Hence there is a neighborhood
$$
W
$$
of
$$
(x,v)
$$
such that
$$
\Phi\restrict_W
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood
$$
\Phi(W)
$$
of $z$.

On
$$
\Phi(W),
$$
the global inverse
$$
\Phi^{-1}
$$
agrees with the smooth local inverse
$$
(\Phi\restrict_W)^{-1}.
$$
Thus $\Phi^{-1}$ is smooth near every point of $U$.
Therefore it is smooth globally.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-15}

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\xrightarrow{\sim}
U
}
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood of $X$.


::: pf-proof

Step [](#s1-13){.pf-ref} gives a homeomorphism.
The map $\Phi$ is smooth by step [](#s1-3){.pf-ref}, and its inverse is smooth by step [](#s1-14){.pf-ref}.
Thus $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism.
Step [](#s1-12){.pf-ref} shows that its image is a neighborhood of $X$.

:::
