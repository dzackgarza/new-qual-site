::: {.problem}
State the tubular neighborhood theorem for a compact submanifold $X$. Give an outline of the proof, including details of the step showing that the constructed map is a diffeomorphism.
:::

::: {.solution}
Assume
$$
X^k
\subset
\mathbb R^n
$$
is a compact embedded smooth submanifold.
Let
$$
\nu(X)
\coloneqq
\{
(x,v)
:
x\in X,
\ v\in(T_xX)^\perp
\}
$$
be its normal bundle.

<1>1. Tubular neighborhood theorem.

::: {.proof}
There exists
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
such that the map
$$
\boxed{
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
\mathbb R^n,
\qquad
\Phi(x,v)=x+v,
}
$$
where
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\coloneqq
\{
(x,v)\in\nu(X):
\|v\|<\varepsilon
\},
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood
$$
U
\subset
\mathbb R^n
$$
of $X$.

Under this diffeomorphism, the zero section
$$
x\longmapsto(x,0)
$$
maps to $X$ itself.
:::

<1>2. The normal bundle $\nu(X)$ is a smooth vector bundle of rank
$$
n-k.
$$

::: {.proof}
The Euclidean metric identifies the normal space at $x$ as
$$
\nu_xX
\coloneqq
(T_xX)^\perp.
$$
In a local frame for $TX$, orthogonal projection onto the complement varies smoothly with $x$.
Thus the spaces
$$
\nu_xX
$$
assemble into a smooth rank-$(n-k)$ vector bundle over $X$.
:::

<1>3. Define
$$
\Phi:\nu(X)\to\mathbb R^n,
\qquad
\Phi(x,v)=x+v.
$$

::: {.proof}
The map is smooth because in local bundle coordinates it is ordinary vector addition in
$$
\mathbb R^n.
$$
:::

<1>4. At a point
$$
(x,0)
$$
of the zero section, there is a canonical splitting
$$
T_{(x,0)}\nu(X)
\cong
T_xX
\oplus
\nu_xX.
$$

::: {.proof}
Along the zero section of a vector bundle, tangent vectors split into:

$$
\text{base directions }T_xX,
$$
and
$$
\text{vertical fiber directions }\nu_xX.
$$

In any local vector-bundle trivialization
$$
\nu(X)|_V
\cong
V\times\mathbb R^{n-k},
$$
this is the ordinary product splitting at
$$
(x,0).
$$
:::

<1>5. Under the splitting in step <1>4,
$$
\boxed{
d\Phi_{(x,0)}(\xi,\eta)
=
\xi+\eta.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Varying the base point in the direction
$$
\xi\in T_xX
$$
changes
$$
x+v
$$
to first order by $\xi$.
Varying the fiber vector in the vertical direction
$$
\eta\in\nu_xX
$$
changes
$$
x+v
$$
to first order by $\eta$.
Thus the total derivative is
$$
(\xi,\eta)\longmapsto\xi+\eta.
$$
:::

<1>6. The derivative
$$
d\Phi_{(x,0)}
$$
is an isomorphism for every
$$
x\in X.
$$

::: {.proof}
By definition of the normal space,
$$
\mathbb R^n
=
T_xX
\oplus
\nu_xX
$$
as an orthogonal direct sum.
Therefore the addition map
$$
T_xX\oplus\nu_xX
\to
\mathbb R^n,
\qquad
(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\xi+\eta
$$
is a linear isomorphism.
Apply step <1>5.
:::

<1>7. For every
$$
x\in X,
$$
there is an open neighborhood
$$
W_x
\subset
\nu(X)
$$
of
$$
(x,0)
$$
such that
$$
\Phi|_{W_x}
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset of
$$
\mathbb R^n.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>6,
$$
d\Phi_{(x,0)}
$$
is invertible.
The inverse function theorem therefore gives open neighborhoods
$$
(x,0)\in W_x
$$
and
$$
x\in U_x\subset\mathbb R^n
$$
such that
$$
\Phi|_{W_x}:W_x\to U_x
$$
is a diffeomorphism.
:::

<1>8. There exists
$$
\delta>0
$$
such that
$$
d\Phi_{(x,v)}
$$
is invertible whenever
$$
x\in X,
\qquad
\|v\|<\delta.
$$

::: {.proof}
Invertibility of a linear map is an open condition.
Thus the set
$$
\mathcal O
\coloneqq
\{
(x,v)\in\nu(X):
d\Phi_{(x,v)}
\text{ is invertible}
\}
$$
is open in
$$
\nu(X)
$$
and contains the entire zero section by step <1>6.

For each
$$
x\in X,
$$
choose
$$
\delta_x>0
$$
and a neighborhood
$$
V_x\subset X
$$
such that
$$
\{
(y,v):
y\in V_x,\ \|v\|<\delta_x
\}
\subset
\mathcal O.
$$

The sets
$$
V_x
$$
cover the compact manifold $X$.
Choose a finite subcover
$$
V_{x_1},
\ldots,
V_{x_r}
$$
and let
$$
\delta
\coloneqq
\min_i\delta_{x_i}>0.
$$
Then every
$$
(x,v)
$$
with
$$
\|v\|<\delta
$$
lies in $\mathcal O$.
:::

<1>9. There exists
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
such that
$$
\Phi
$$
is injective on
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$

::: {.proof}
Suppose no such uniform radius existed.
Then for every positive integer $j$ there would be distinct points
$$
(x_j,v_j),
\qquad
(y_j,w_j)
$$
in
$$
\nu(X)
$$
such that
$$
\|v_j\|<\frac1j,
\qquad
\|w_j\|<\frac1j,
$$
but
$$
\Phi(x_j,v_j)
=
\Phi(y_j,w_j).
$$
Thus
$$
x_j+v_j
=
y_j+w_j,
$$
so
$$
\|x_j-y_j\|
\le
\|v_j\|+\|w_j\|
\longrightarrow
0.
$$

Since $X$ is compact, after passing to a subsequence,
$$
x_j\to x
$$
for some
$$
x\in X.
$$
The preceding estimate then gives
$$
y_j\to x
$$
as well.
Also
$$
v_j\to0,
\qquad
w_j\to0.
$$
Hence
$$
(x_j,v_j)\to(x,0),
\qquad
(y_j,w_j)\to(x,0)
$$
in the total space of the normal bundle.

By step <1>7, there is a neighborhood
$$
W_x
$$
of
$$
(x,0)
$$
on which $\Phi$ is injective.
For sufficiently large $j$, both
$$
(x_j,v_j)
$$
and
$$
(y_j,w_j)
$$
lie in
$$
W_x.
$$
Their images are equal, so injectivity on $W_x$ gives
$$
(x_j,v_j)
=
(y_j,w_j),
$$
contradicting their choice as distinct points.

Therefore some
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
exists for which $\Phi$ is injective on
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
:::

<1>10. Shrink $\varepsilon$ if necessary so that
$$
0<\varepsilon<\delta,
$$
where $\delta$ is from step <1>8.
Then
$$
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
\mathbb R^n
$$
is both injective and a local diffeomorphism.

::: {.proof}
Injectivity follows from step <1>9.
Because
$$
\varepsilon<\delta,
$$
step <1>8 says
$$
d\Phi
$$
is invertible at every point of
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
The inverse function theorem therefore makes $\Phi$ a local diffeomorphism everywhere on this disk bundle.
:::

<1>11. The image
$$
U
\coloneqq
\Phi
\left(
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\right)
$$
is open in
$$
\mathbb R^n.
$$

::: {.proof}
A local diffeomorphism is an open map.
By step <1>10,
$$
\Phi
$$
is a local diffeomorphism on
$$
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
Hence its image $U$ is open.
:::

<1>12. The open set $U$ contains $X$.

::: {.proof}
For every
$$
x\in X,
$$
the zero vector satisfies
$$
\|0\|<\varepsilon,
$$
so
$$
(x,0)
\in
\nu_\varepsilon(X).
$$
Moreover,
$$
\Phi(x,0)=x.
$$
Hence
$$
X\subseteq U.
$$
:::

<1>13. The map
$$
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
U
$$
is a homeomorphism.

::: {.proof}
By definition of $U$, the map is surjective.
By step <1>10, it is injective.
Also by step <1>10, it is a local diffeomorphism and hence an open map.

A continuous bijective open map is a homeomorphism.
Therefore
$$
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\to
U
$$
is a homeomorphism.
:::

<1>14. The inverse
$$
\Phi^{-1}:U\to\nu_\varepsilon(X)
$$
is smooth.

::: {.proof}
Fix
$$
z\in U.
$$
Let
$$
(x,v)
=
\Phi^{-1}(z).
$$
By step <1>10,
$$
\Phi
$$
is a local diffeomorphism at
$$
(x,v).
$$
Hence there is a neighborhood
$$
W
$$
of
$$
(x,v)
$$
such that
$$
\Phi|_W
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood
$$
\Phi(W)
$$
of $z$.

On
$$
\Phi(W),
$$
the global inverse
$$
\Phi^{-1}
$$
agrees with the smooth local inverse
$$
(\Phi|_W)^{-1}.
$$
Thus $\Phi^{-1}$ is smooth near every point of $U$.
Therefore it is smooth globally.
:::

<1>15. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
\Phi:
\nu_\varepsilon(X)
\xrightarrow{\sim}
U
}
$$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighborhood of $X$.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>13 gives a homeomorphism.
The map $\Phi$ is smooth by step <1>3, and its inverse is smooth by step <1>14.
Thus $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism.
Step <1>12 shows that its image is a neighborhood of $X$.
:::

<1>16. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 states the theorem, and steps <1>2--<1>15 outline its proof with the diffeomorphism step established in detail.
:::
