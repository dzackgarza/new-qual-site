::: {.problem}
On
\[
\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\},
\]
consider the $2$-form
\[
\omega
=
(x^2+y^2+z^2)^\alpha
\left(
x\,dy\wedge dz
+y\,dz\wedge dx
+z\,dx\wedge dy
\right).
\]
Find all
\[
\alpha\in\mathbb R
\]
for which $\omega$ is closed. For that value of $\alpha$, show that $\omega$ is not exact.
:::

::: {.solution}
Set
$$
r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2
$$
and
$$
\beta
\coloneqq
x\,dy\wedge dz
+y\,dz\wedge dx
+z\,dx\wedge dy.
$$
Then
$$
\omega=r^{2\alpha}\beta.
$$

<1>1. One has
$$
\boxed{
d\beta
=
3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since the coordinate $1$-forms are closed,
$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x\,dy\wedge dz)
&=
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,\\
d(y\,dz\wedge dx)
&=
dy\wedge dz\wedge dx,\\
d(z\,dx\wedge dy)
&=
dz\wedge dx\wedge dy.
\end{aligned}
$$
Each cyclic permutation has positive sign, so their sum is
$$
3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
$$
:::

<1>2. One has
$$
d(r^{2\alpha})
=
2\alpha r^{2\alpha-2}
(x\,dx+y\,dy+z\,dz).
$$

::: {.proof}
Differentiate
$$
r^{2\alpha}
=(x^2+y^2+z^2)^\alpha
$$
by the chain rule.
:::

<1>3. The wedge product satisfies
$$
\boxed{
(x\,dx+y\,dy+z\,dz)\wedge\beta
=
r^2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Expand the product.
All terms containing a repeated coordinate differential vanish.
The surviving terms are
$$
x^2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
$$
$$
y^2\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx,
$$
and
$$
z^2\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy.
$$
Again the cyclic permutations have positive sign, so the sum is
$$
(x^2+y^2+z^2)
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
$$
:::

<1>4. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
d\omega
=
(2\alpha+3)r^{2\alpha}
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Using the product rule and steps <1>1--<1>3,
$$
\begin{aligned}
d\omega
&=
d(r^{2\alpha})\wedge\beta
+r^{2\alpha}d\beta\\
&=
2\alpha r^{2\alpha-2}
r^2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
+3r^{2\alpha}
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\\
&=
(2\alpha+3)r^{2\alpha}
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>5. Hence
$$
\boxed{
d\omega=0
\iff
\alpha=-\frac32}.
$$

::: {.proof}
On
$$
\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\},
$$
the factor
$$
r^{2\alpha}
$$
never vanishes.
Thus step <1>4 vanishes identically exactly when
$$
2\alpha+3=0.
$$
:::

<1>6. Assume
$$
\alpha=-\frac32.
$$
Let
$$
i:S^2\hookrightarrow
\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}
$$
be the inclusion of the unit sphere.
Then
$$
i^*\omega
$$
is the standard oriented area form on
$$
S^2.
$$

::: {.proof}
On
$$
S^2
$$
one has
$$
r=1,
$$
so
$$
i^*\omega=i^*\beta.
$$
For tangent vectors
$$
u,v\in T_pS^2,
$$
the form
$$
\beta_p(u,v)
$$
equals
$$
p\cdot(u\times v),
$$
which is precisely the standard area form induced by the outward orientation.
:::

<1>7. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
\int_{S^2}i^*\omega
=
4\pi
\ne0}.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>6, the integral is the surface area of the unit sphere.
:::

<1>8. The closed form
$$
\omega
$$
is not exact.

::: {.proof}
Suppose
$$
\omega=d\eta
$$
for some smooth
$$
1
$$
-form
$$
\eta
$$
on
$$
\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}.
$$
Pullback commutes with exterior differentiation, so
$$
i^*\omega
=
d(i^*\eta).
$$
Since
$$
S^2
$$
has empty boundary, Stokes' theorem gives
$$
\int_{S^2}i^*\omega
=
\int_{S^2}d(i^*\eta)
=
\int_{\partial S^2}i^*\eta
=
0,
$$
contradicting step <1>7.
:::

<1>9. Therefore
$$
\boxed{
\alpha=-\frac32
}
$$
is the unique value for which $\omega$ is closed, and for this value $\omega$ is not exact.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 gives the unique closed case, and step <1>8 proves nonexactness.
:::

<1>10. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>9 is the required conclusion.
:::
