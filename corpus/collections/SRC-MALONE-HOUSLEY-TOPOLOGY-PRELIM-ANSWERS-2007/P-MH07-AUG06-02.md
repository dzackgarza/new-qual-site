::: {.problem}
For which values of $a>0$ does the hyperboloid
\[
x^2+y^2-z^2=1
\]
intersect the sphere
\[
x^2+y^2+z^2=a
\]
transversally in $\mathbb R^3$?
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
H=\{x^2+y^2-z^2=1\},
\qquad
S_a=\{x^2+y^2+z^2=a\}.
$$

<1>1. A point lies in $H\cap S_a$ if and only if
$$
z^2=\frac{a-1}{2},
\qquad
x^2+y^2=\frac{a+1}{2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Subtracting the hyperboloid equation from the sphere equation gives
$$
2z^2=a-1.
$$
Substituting this into
$$
x^2+y^2-z^2=1
$$
gives the second equation.
Conversely, the two displayed equations imply both defining equations.
:::

<1>2. The intersection is empty for
$$
0<a<1,
$$
and nonempty for
$$
a\ge1.
$$

::: {.proof}
If
$$
0<a<1,
$$
step <1>1 would require
$$
z^2<0,
$$
which is impossible over $\mathbb R$.
If
$$
a\ge1,
$$
choose
$$
z=\sqrt{\frac{a-1}{2}},
\qquad
x=\sqrt{\frac{a+1}{2}},
\qquad
y=0.
$$
Then step <1>1 holds.
:::

<1>3. At a point
$$
p=(x,y,z)\in H\cap S_a,
$$
normal vectors to the two surfaces are
$$
n_H=(x,y,-z),
\qquad
n_S=(x,y,z).
$$

::: {.proof}
The surfaces are the regular level sets of
$$
F=x^2+y^2-z^2
$$
and
$$
G=x^2+y^2+z^2.
$$
Their gradients are
$$
2(x,y,-z)
$$
and
$$
2(x,y,z),
$$
so the displayed vectors span the same normal lines.
By step <1>1,
$$
x^2+y^2=\frac{a+1}{2}>0,
$$
so at least one of $x,y$ is nonzero.
:::

<1>4. The surfaces are transverse at $p$ if and only if $n_H$ and $n_S$ are linearly independent.

::: {.proof}
For two hypersurfaces in $\mathbb R^3$,
$$
T_pH+T_pS_a=\mathbb R^3
$$
exactly when their normal lines are distinct.
:::

<1>5. The normals are linearly dependent if and only if
$$
z=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
Suppose
$$
(x,y,-z)=\lambda(x,y,z).
$$
Since at least one of $x,y$ is nonzero, one gets
$$
\lambda=1.
$$
Then
$$
-z=z,
$$
so
$$
z=0.
$$
Conversely, if $z=0$, the two normals are equal.
:::

<1>6. A nonempty intersection fails to be transverse exactly when
$$
a=1.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1,
$$
z^2=\frac{a-1}{2}.
$$
Thus
$$
z=0
\iff
a=1.
$$
Apply steps <1>4--<1>5.
:::

<1>7. Therefore the hyperboloid and sphere have a nonempty transverse intersection exactly for
$$
\boxed{a>1}.
$$

::: {.proof}
By step <1>2, nonempty intersection requires
$$
a\ge1.
$$
Step <1>6 excludes exactly
$$
a=1.
$$
:::

<1>8. Under the standard definition of transversality, the empty intersections for
$$
0<a<1
$$
are transverse vacuously.

::: {.proof}
The transversality condition is checked only at points of
$$
H\cap S_a.
$$
If the intersection is empty, there are no points at which it can fail.
:::

<1>9. Thus, as a statement about transverse submanifolds,
$$
\boxed{
H\pitchfork S_a
\iff
a\ne1.
}
$$
If the wording is taken to require an actual intersection, the answer is
$$
\boxed{a>1}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Combine steps <1>2, <1>6, <1>7, and <1>8.
:::

<1>10. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>9 gives the complete classification.
:::
