::: {.problem}
State the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem and the needed fact about the ring structure of cohomology. Use them to show that every map
\[
f:\mathbb{CP}^n\to\mathbb{CP}^n
\]
has a fixed point when $n$ is even. Construct a fixed-point-free self-map when $n$ is odd.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
u
\in
H^2(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Z)
$$
be the standard generator.

<1>1. Lefschetz fixed-point theorem.

::: {.proof}
Let
$$
X
$$
be a compact triangulable space and
$$
f:X\to X
$$
continuous.
Its Lefschetz number is
$$
L(f)
\coloneqq
\sum_{i\ge0}
(-1)^i
\operatorname{tr}
\left(
f_*:
H_i(X;\mathbb Q)
\to
H_i(X;\mathbb Q)
\right).
$$
If
$$
L(f)\ne0,
$$
then $f$ has a fixed point.
:::

<1>2. The integral cohomology ring of $\mathbb{CP}^n$ is
$$
\boxed{
H^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Z)
\cong
\mathbb Z[u]/(u^{n+1}),
\qquad
\deg u=2.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Complex projective space has one cell in each even dimension
$$
0,2,\ldots,2n,
$$
and no odd-dimensional cells.
The standard degree-$2$ class $u$ generates the cohomology ring, with
$$
u^j
$$
generating
$$
H^{2j}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Z)
\cong
\mathbb Z
$$
for
$$
0\le j\le n,
$$
and
$$
u^{n+1}=0.
$$
:::

<1>3. For every continuous self-map
$$
f:\mathbb{CP}^n\to\mathbb{CP}^n,
$$
there is an integer
$$
k
$$
such that
$$
f^*(u)=ku.
$$

::: {.proof}
The group
$$
H^2(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Z)
\cong
\mathbb Z
$$
is generated by $u$.
Every endomorphism of this infinite cyclic group is multiplication by an integer.
:::

<1>4. For every
$$
0\le j\le n,
$$
one has
$$
f^*(u^j)
=
k^ju^j.
$$

::: {.proof}
Pullback preserves cup products:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f^*(u^j)
&=
f^*(u)^j\\
&=
(ku)^j\\
&=
k^ju^j.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>5. On rational cohomology,
$$
f^*:
H^{2j}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Q)
\to
H^{2j}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Q)
$$
has trace
$$
k^j,
$$
and all odd-degree cohomology groups vanish.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>2,
$$
H^{2j}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Q)
$$
is one-dimensional with basis
$$
u^j.
$$
Step <1>4 shows that $f^*$ acts by multiplication by $k^j$.
There are no odd-dimensional cohomology groups.
:::

<1>6. The Lefschetz number of $f$ is
$$
\boxed{
L(f)
=
1+k+k^2+\cdots+k^n.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
Over a field, the trace of the induced map on homology equals the trace of the dual map on cohomology in the same degree.
By step <1>5, only even degrees contribute, so every sign in the Lefschetz sum is positive:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L(f)
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{n}
\operatorname{tr}
\left(
f^*:
H^{2j}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Q)
\to
H^{2j}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb Q)
\right)\\
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{n}k^j.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>7. Assume
$$
n
$$
is even.
If
$$
k=1,
$$
then
$$
L(f)=n+1\ne0.
$$

::: {.proof}
Substitute
$$
k=1
$$
into the formula from step <1>6.
:::

<1>8. Assume
$$
n
$$
is even and
$$
k\ne1.
$$
Then
$$
L(f)
=
\frac{k^{n+1}-1}{k-1}.
$$

::: {.proof}
This is the finite geometric-series identity.
:::

<1>9. If
$$
n
$$
is even, then
$$
L(f)\ne0
$$
for every integer
$$
k.
$$

::: {.proof}
The case
$$
k=1
$$
is step <1>7.

Now let
$$
k\ne1.
$$
If
$$
L(f)=0,
$$
step <1>8 would give
$$
k^{n+1}=1.
$$
Since
$$
n
$$
is even,
$$
n+1
$$
is odd.
The only integer satisfying
$$
k^{n+1}=1
$$
is
$$
k=1,
$$
contrary to the present assumption.
Therefore
$$
L(f)\ne0.
$$
:::

<1>10. Therefore every self-map
$$
f:\mathbb{CP}^n\to\mathbb{CP}^n
$$
has a fixed point when
$$
n
$$
is even.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>9,
$$
L(f)\ne0.
$$
Apply the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem from step <1>1.
Thus
$$
\boxed{
n\text{ even}
\implies
\text{every self-map of }\mathbb{CP}^n
\text{ has a fixed point}.
}
$$
:::

<1>11. Now assume
$$
n
$$
is odd.
Then
$$
n+1
=
2m
$$
for some positive integer
$$
m.
$$

::: {.proof}
An odd integer plus $1$ is even.
:::

<1>12. Define an anti-linear map
$$
J:\mathbb C^{2m}\to\mathbb C^{2m}
$$
by
$$
\boxed{
J(z_0,z_1,\ldots,z_{2m-2},z_{2m-1})
=
(-\overline{z_1},\overline{z_0},
\ldots,
-\overline{z_{2m-1}},\overline{z_{2m-2}}).
}
$$

::: {.proof}
The coordinates are grouped into
$$
m
$$
pairs.
On each pair
$$
(z_{2r},z_{2r+1}),
$$
the map is
$$
(z_{2r},z_{2r+1})
\longmapsto
(-\overline{z_{2r+1}},\overline{z_{2r}}).
$$
This is real-linear and anti-complex-linear.
:::

<1>13. The map $J$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{
J^2=-I.
}
$$

::: {.proof}
On one coordinate pair,
$$
\begin{aligned}
J^2(z_{2r},z_{2r+1})
&=
J(-\overline{z_{2r+1}},\overline{z_{2r}})\\
&=
(-z_{2r},-z_{2r+1}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $J^2=-I$ on every pair and hence on all of
$$
\mathbb C^{2m}.
$$
:::

<1>14. The map $J$ induces a well-defined smooth self-map
$$
\overline J:
\mathbb{CP}^{2m-1}
\to
\mathbb{CP}^{2m-1}
$$
by
$$
\overline J([z])
\coloneqq
[Jz].
$$

::: {.proof}
If
$$
z'
=
\lambda z
$$
with
$$
\lambda\in\mathbb C^\times,
$$
then anti-linearity gives
$$
Jz'
=
J(\lambda z)
=
\overline\lambda\,Jz.
$$
Thus
$$
[Jz']
=
[Jz].
$$
Hence the formula is independent of the chosen representative.
Smoothness follows because complex conjugation and the coordinate operations defining $J$ are smooth as real maps.
:::

<1>15. The projective map
$$
\overline J
$$
has no fixed point.

::: {.proof}
Suppose
$$
[z]
$$
were fixed.
Then
$$
[Jz]
=
[z],
$$
so there would exist
$$
\lambda\in\mathbb C^\times
$$
with
$$
Jz
=
\lambda z.
$$

Apply $J$ again.
Because $J$ is anti-linear,
$$
\begin{aligned}
J^2z
&=
J(\lambda z)\\
&=
\overline\lambda\,Jz\\
&=
\overline\lambda\lambda z\\
&=
|\lambda|^2z.
\end{aligned}
$$
But step <1>13 gives
$$
J^2z
=
-z.
$$
Therefore
$$
|\lambda|^2z
=
-z.
$$
Since
$$
z\ne0,
$$
this implies
$$
|\lambda|^2=-1,
$$
which is impossible.
Hence there is no fixed point.
:::

<1>16. Thus when
$$
n
$$
is odd, there exists a fixed-point-free self-map of
$$
\mathbb{CP}^n.
$$

::: {.proof}
Write
$$
n=2m-1
$$
as in step <1>11.
Then the map
$$
\overline J
$$
constructed in step <1>14 is a self-map of
$$
\mathbb{CP}^n
$$
and is fixed-point-free by step <1>15.
Thus
$$
\boxed{
n\text{ odd}
\implies
\mathbb{CP}^n
\text{ admits a fixed-point-free self-map}.
}
$$
:::

<1>17. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>10 proves the even-dimensional fixed-point statement, and step <1>16 gives the required fixed-point-free map for odd $n$.
:::
