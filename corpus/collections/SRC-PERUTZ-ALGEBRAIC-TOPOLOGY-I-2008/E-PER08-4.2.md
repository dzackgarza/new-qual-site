::: {.problem}
Let $K$ be the trefoil knot.
We have
\[
\pi_1(S^3\setminus K)=\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3\rangle.
\]

Explain how to find a word representing a given loop in $S^3\setminus K$.
Find words representing a meridian of $K$ and a longitude of $K$.

Let $Z$ be the kernel of the homomorphism
\[
\pi_1(S^3\setminus K)\longrightarrow \langle a,b\mid a^2,b^3\rangle
\]
that sends $a$ to $a$ and $b$ to $b$.
Show that $Z\cong\mathbb Z$, generated by a longitude, and that $Z$ is contained in the center of $\pi_1(S^3\setminus K)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
\[
G=\pi_1(S^3\setminus K)=\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3\rangle.
\]
Recall the decomposition used in Proposition 4.7: $S^3\setminus K$ is covered by two pieces which deformation retract to core circles, represented by $a$ and $b$, while their intersection deformation retracts to a circle $K'$ parallel to $K$. The circle $K'$ winds twice around the $a$-core and three times around the $b$-core.

<1>1. A loop can be converted to a word in $a$ and $b$ by the van Kampen decomposition.
::: {.proof}
Take a loop $\gamma$ in $S^3\setminus K$ and subdivide its parameter interval so that each subpath lies entirely in one of the two pieces of the cover. Choose paths from the subdivision points to the basepoint through the relevant piece or through the intersection, exactly as in the proof of van Kampen's theorem.

A subloop lying in the first piece is homotopic to an integral power of its core circle, hence contributes a power $a^m$. A subloop lying in the second piece contributes a power $b^n$. Reading the subloops in order gives a word
\[
a^{m_1}b^{n_1}a^{m_2}b^{n_2}\cdots
\]
representing $[\gamma]$.

Changing the auxiliary paths or subdivision only changes the word using the relation contributed by the overlap circle $K'$. Since $K'$ winds twice around the first core and three times around the second, that relation is
\[
a^2=b^3.
\]
Thus this procedure gives precisely a word in the presentation $\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3\rangle$ representing the given loop.
:::

<1>2. The element
\[
h:=a^2=b^3
\]
is represented by a longitude of the trefoil.
::: {.proof}
The circle $K'$ in the overlap torus is chosen parallel to the knot $K$. Hence $K'$ is a longitude in the sense used in the exercise.

In the first piece, $K'$ winds twice around the core circle, so it represents $a^2$. In the second piece it winds three times around the other core, so it represents $b^3$. Thus
\[
[K']=a^2=b^3=h.
\]
Therefore $h$ is represented by a longitude.
:::

<1>3. A meridian is represented by
\[
\mu=a^{-1}b.
\]
::: {.proof}
On the boundary torus of a tubular neighborhood of a $(2,3)$-torus knot, the two core-circle classes $a$ and $b$ have algebraic linking numbers $3$ and $2$, respectively, with the knot. Equivalently, in the abelianization of the knot group the relation
\[
2[a]=3[b]
\]
holds, and choosing the meridian class as the positive generator gives
\[
[a]=3[\mu],
\qquad
[b]=2[\mu].
\]
This is the standard Bezout computation for a $(p,q)$-torus knot: a meridian is $a^r b^s$ when
\[
pr+qs=1.
\]
For $(p,q)=(2,3)$ we may take
\[
r=-1,
\qquad
s=1,
\qquad
2(-1)+3(1)=1.
\]
Hence one meridian word is
\[
\boxed{\mu=a^{-1}b}.
\]
Indeed its abelianization is
\[
-[a]+[b]=-3[\mu]+2[\mu]=-[\mu],
\]
so depending on the chosen orientation it represents the meridian or its inverse; reversing the meridian orientation replaces the word by $b^{-1}a$. Either is a meridian.
:::

<1>4. The longitude element $h=a^2=b^3$ is central in $G$.
::: {.proof}
Since $h=a^2$, it commutes with $a$. Since $h=b^3$, it commutes with $b$. The group $G$ is generated by $a$ and $b$, so an element commuting with both generators commutes with every element of $G$. Therefore
\[
h\in Z(G).
\]
Consequently the cyclic subgroup $\langle h\rangle$ is central and hence normal.
:::

<1>5. The kernel $Z$ is exactly $\langle h\rangle$.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
q:G\longrightarrow\langle a,b\mid a^2,b^3\rangle
\]
be the homomorphism in the problem. Its target is obtained from the presentation of $G$ by imposing the additional relation
\[
h=a^2=b^3=1.
\]
Therefore
\[
G/\!\langle\!\langle h\rangle\!\rangle
\cong
\langle a,b\mid a^2,b^3\rangle.
\]
Hence
\[
\ker q=\langle\!\langle h\rangle\!\rangle,
\]
the normal closure of $h$.

By <1>4, $h$ is central, so all its conjugates are equal to $h$. Its normal closure is therefore just its ordinary cyclic subgroup:
\[
\langle\!\langle h\rangle\!\rangle=\langle h\rangle.
\]
Thus
\[
Z=\ker q=\langle h\rangle.
\]
:::

<1>6. The longitude $h$ has infinite order.
::: {.proof}
Abelianizing
\[
G=\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3\rangle
\]
gives
\[
G_{\mathrm{ab}}
\cong
\mathbb Z^2/\langle(2,-3)\rangle
\cong\mathbb Z,
\]
since $\gcd(2,3)=1$. An explicit homomorphism to $\mathbb Z$ is
\[
[a]\longmapsto3,
\qquad
[b]\longmapsto2,
\]
which respects the relation because
\[
2\cdot3=3\cdot2.
\]
Under this map,
\[
h=a^2=b^3
\]
maps to
\[
6\neq0.
\]
Thus $h$ has infinite order in $G$.
:::

By <1>5 and <1>6,
\[
Z=\langle h\rangle\cong\mathbb Z.
\]
By <1>2, $h$ is represented by a longitude, and by <1>4,
\[
Z\subseteq Z(G).
\]
This proves all the claims.
:::
