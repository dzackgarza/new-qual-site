::: {.problem}
Show that a group of order $30$ must have a subgroup of order $15$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $G$ be a group of order $30$.

<1>1. At least one of the Sylow $3$- and Sylow $5$-subgroups
is normal in $G$.

::: {.proof}
Sylow's theorems give
$$
n_3\mid10,\quad n_3\equiv1\pmod3,
\qquad n_5\mid6,\quad n_5\equiv1\pmod5
$$
[@DF04]. Among the divisors $1,2,5,10$ of $10$, only
$1,10$ satisfy the first congruence. Among $1,2,3,6$,
only $1,6$ satisfy the second. Thus
$n_3\in\{1,10\}$ and $n_5\in\{1,6\}$.

If both counts exceeded $1$, the ten subgroups of order
$3$ would supply $10(3-1)=20$ distinct elements of order
$3$, and the six subgroups of order $5$ would supply
$6(5-1)=24$ distinct elements of order $5$.
Distinct subgroups of the same prime order intersect only
in the identity, since any common nonidentity element
generates both. An element cannot have both orders $3$
and $5$. Hence these are $44$ distinct nonidentity
elements, impossible in a group of order $30$.
At least one count is $1$; its unique Sylow subgroup is
normal because conjugation preserves subgroup orders.
:::

<1>2. The product of a Sylow $3$-subgroup and a Sylow
$5$-subgroup is a subgroup of order $15$.

::: {.proof}
Choose Sylow subgroups of orders $3$ and $5$. By step
<1>1, at least one is normal; call a normal one $N$ and
the other $H$. Their intersection is trivial, since its
order divides both $3$ and $5$.

The set $NH$ is a subgroup. It contains the identity, and
normality of $N$ gives
$$
(n_1h_1)(n_2h_2)=n_1(h_1n_2h_1^{-1})h_1h_2\in NH,
\qquad
(nh)^{-1}=(h^{-1}n^{-1}h)h^{-1}\in NH.
$$
Multiplication $N\times H\to NH$ is surjective by
definition and injective: $n_1h_1=n_2h_2$ implies
$n_2^{-1}n_1=h_2h_1^{-1}\in N\cap H=\{1\}$.
Therefore $|NH|=|N||H|=3\cdot5=15$.
:::
:::
