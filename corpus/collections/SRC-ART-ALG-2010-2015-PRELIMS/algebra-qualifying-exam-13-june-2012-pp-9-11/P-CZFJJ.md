::: {.problem}
Consider the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
Let $I = (2, x^2 + 3x - 1)$ be the ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ generated by 2 and $x^2 + 3x - 1$.
Describe the structure of the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]/I$ in as much detail as you can.
Do the same for the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]/J$ where $J = (2, x^3 + 3x^2 - x + 4)$.
:::

::: solution
The two rings are respectively $\mathbb F_4$ and
$\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_4$.

<1>1. Reduction modulo $2$ gives
$$
\mathbb Z[x]/I\cong\mathbb F_2[x]/(q),\qquad
\mathbb Z[x]/J\cong\mathbb F_2[x]/(xq),
\qquad q=x^2+x+1.
$$

::: proof
Coefficient reduction $\mathbb Z[x]\to\mathbb F_2[x]$ is
surjective and has kernel $(2)$. For any $f\in\mathbb Z[x]$,
the inverse image of $(\overline f)$ is $(2,f)$: if
$\overline h=\overline f\,\overline a$, lift $\overline a$
to an integer polynomial $a$; then $h-fa\in(2)$.
The reverse inclusion follows immediately by reduction.
Thus the induced quotient maps are isomorphisms.
The polynomials in the problem reduce to $q$ and
$x^3+x^2+x=xq$, respectively.
:::

<1>2. The first ring is the field
$$
E=\mathbb F_2[\alpha],\qquad
\alpha^2=\alpha+1,
\qquad E=\{0,1,\alpha,1+\alpha\}.
$$

::: proof
The polynomial $q$ has neither $0$ nor $1$ as a root. A
reducible quadratic over a field has a linear factor and hence
a root, so $q$ is irreducible. Polynomial division gives
unique representatives $a+bx$, with $a,b\in\mathbb F_2$,
in $\mathbb F_2[x]/(q)$. Let $\alpha$ be the class of $x$.
Its defining relation is $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, and
$\alpha^3=\alpha(\alpha+1)=1$.
The three nonzero elements are $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$ and
are all invertible, proving directly that $E$ is a field
of four elements.

It has characteristic $2$, additive group $C_2\times C_2$,
and multiplicative group $E^\times=\langle\alpha\rangle\cong C_3$.
Its only ideals are $(0)$ and $E$, since a nonzero ideal
contains a unit. In particular $I$ is maximal and prime.
The only idempotents of $E$ are $0,1$, as $e^2=e$ implies
$e(e-1)=0$ in a field; it has no nonzero zero divisors or nilpotents.
:::

<1>3. The second ring $B=\mathbb F_2[x]/(xq)$ is explicitly
isomorphic to $\mathbb F_2\times E$.

::: proof
Define
$$
\Theta:B\longrightarrow\mathbb F_2\times E,
\qquad [h(x)]\longmapsto(h(0),h(\alpha)).
$$
Both evaluations are ring homomorphisms, and $xq$ vanishes
at $0$ and $\alpha$, so the map is well defined.
If $\Theta([h])=0$, then $x\mid h$ and $q\mid h$.
The identity $q-x(x+1)=1$ shows that $x$ and $q$ are coprime.
More explicitly, write $h=xk$. Modulo $q$, the class of $x$
is the unit $\alpha$, so $q\mid k$. Thus $xq\mid h$,
proving injectivity.

Let
$$
e_0=[q],\qquad e_1=[x^2+x].
$$
Then $\Theta(e_0)=(1,0)$ and $\Theta(e_1)=(0,1)$.
For $a,b,c\in\mathbb F_2$, the element
$$
a e_0+(b+c[x])e_1
$$
maps to $(a,b+c\alpha)$. This proves surjectivity and
gives the inverse on every element of the product.
Consequently $B\cong\mathbb F_2\times E$ as rings.
:::

<1>4. The product description determines the remaining ring structure.

::: proof
The ring $B$ has eight elements, characteristic $2$, and
additive group $C_2^3$. Equivalently, polynomial division
gives the basis $1,[x],[x^2]$ over $\mathbb F_2$.
A pair $(a,b)\in\mathbb F_2\times E$ is a unit exactly
when $a=1$ and $b\ne0$, so $B^\times\cong E^\times\cong C_3$.
A nonzero pair is a zero divisor exactly when at least one
component is zero: multiply by a nonzero pair supported
in that component to obtain zero; when both components
are nonzero the pair is a unit. Thus there are four nonzero
zero divisors, namely $(1,0)$ and the three $(0,b)$ with $b\ne0$.

Every ideal $L$ in the product decomposes into its two
coordinate ideals: for $(a,b)\in L$, multiplication by
$(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ puts $(a,0)$ and $(0,b)$ in $L$.
Each coordinate ideal in a field is zero or the whole field.
Therefore the complete ideal list in $B$ is
$$
(0),\quad (e_0),\quad(e_1),\quad B.
$$
The middle two are maximal, with quotients $E$ and
$\mathbb F_2$, respectively, and are the only prime ideals:
the zero ideal is not prime because $e_0e_1=0$ with both
factors nonzero. Hence $B$ is neither a field nor a domain
nor a local ring, and $J$ is neither prime nor maximal.

The only idempotents are $0,e_0,e_1,1$, because each coordinate
of an idempotent in a field is $0$ or $1$. Finally, a nilpotent
pair has nilpotent coordinates, which must both be zero.
Thus $B$ is reduced despite having nonzero zero divisors.
:::
