::: {.problem}
For each of the following, either give an example or explain why no such example exists.
One or two sentence answers will suffice; you don't need to give formal proofs.

a. A commutative ring $R$ and an ideal $I$ of $R$ that is prime but not maximal.

b. A commutative ring $R$ and a finitely generated, torsion-free $R$-module $M$ that is not free.

c. A PID $R$ and an $R$-module that is torsion-free but not free.

d. A Euclidean domain which is not a UFD.

e. A UFD which is not a PID.

f. An integral domain which is not Noetherian.

(Conventions: All rings are rings with 1, and an integral domain is commutative.)
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Two nonzero elements $a,b$ of an ideal of an integral domain $R$ satisfy the nontrivial relation $b\cdot a-a\cdot b=0$; hence an ideal of $R$ is a free $R$-module only if it is $0$ or principal.

::: {.proof}
The coefficients $b$ and $-a$ are nonzero, so a basis of the ideal has at most one element; an ideal with basis $\{f\}$ equals $(f)$.
:::

<1>2. In part (a), take $R=\ZZ[x]$ and $I=(x)$.

::: {.proof}
$R/I\cong\ZZ$ is an integral domain but not a field, so $I$ is prime but not maximal; indeed $(x)\subsetneq(2,x)\subsetneq R$.
:::

<1>3. In part (b), take $R=k[x,y]$ for a field $k$ and $M=(x,y)$.

::: {.proof}
$M$ is generated by $x,y$, and it is torsion-free as a nonzero ideal of an integral domain.
If $(x,y)=(f)$, then $f$ divides $x$ and $y$, so $f$ is a nonzero constant and $(x,y)=R$, contradicting that every element of $(x,y)$ vanishes at $(0,0)$.
By step <1>1, $M$ is not free.
:::

<1>4. In part (c), take $R=\ZZ$ and $M=\QQ$.

::: {.proof}
For $n\ne0$ and $q\ne0$, $nq\ne0$, so $\QQ$ is torsion-free.
Any two nonzero rationals $p/q$ and $r/s$ satisfy $(qr)\cdot\frac pq-(ps)\cdot\frac rs=0$ with nonzero integer coefficients, so a basis has at most one element; but $\QQ$ is not cyclic, since $\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac pq\notin\ZZ\frac pq$ for $p/q\ne0$. Hence $\QQ$ is not free.
:::

<1>5. In part (d), no example exists.

::: {.proof}
A Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain, since a nonzero element of minimal Euclidean norm in a nonzero ideal generates it, and a principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain [@DF04].
:::

<1>6. In part (e), take $R=\ZZ[x]$.

::: {.proof}
By Gauss's lemma, $\ZZ[x]$ is a UFD because $\ZZ$ is [@DF04].
If $(2,x)=(f)$, then $f\mid2$ forces $f\in\{\pm1,\pm2\}$; $f=\pm1$ is impossible because every element of $(2,x)$ has even constant term, and $f=\pm2$ is impossible because $x\notin(2)$. So $\ZZ[x]$ is not a PID.
:::

<1>7. In part (f), take $R=k[x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]$ for a field $k$.

::: {.proof}
$R$ is an integral domain, and the chain $(x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2,x_3)\subsetneq\cdots$ does not stabilize: $x_{n+1}\notin(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, since setting $x_1=\cdots=x_n=0$ sends that ideal to $0$ and $x_{n+1}$ to $x_{n+1}\ne0$.
:::

<1>8. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Steps <1>2 through <1>7 answer parts (a) through (f).
:::
