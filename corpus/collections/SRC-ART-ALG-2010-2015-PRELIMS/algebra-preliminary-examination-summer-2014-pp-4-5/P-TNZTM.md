::: {.problem}
Prove or disprove each of the following:

a. If $R[x]$ is a PID, then $R$ is a field.

b. All cyclic subgroups of order 15 in $S_8$ are conjugate.

c. All torsion-free $\mathbb{Z}$-modules are free.

d. All degree two field extensions are Galois.
:::

::: solution
The assertions are respectively true, true, false, and false.

<1>1. If $R[x]$ is a principal ideal domain, then $R$ is a field.

::: proof
The constant polynomials identify $R$ with a subring of the
commutative integral domain $R[x]$, so $R$ is a commutative
integral domain with $1\ne0$. Fix $0\ne a\in R$. The ideal
$(a,x)$ is generated by some $f\in R[x]$. As $a=fg$ for a
nonzero $g\in R[x]$, additivity of degrees over a domain gives
$\deg f=\deg g=0$. Thus $f$ is a nonzero constant.
The identity $x=fh$ for some $h\in R[x]$ shows, by comparing
coefficients of $x$, that $1=fh_1$ for an element $h_1\in R$.
Hence $f$ is a unit, and $(a,x)=R[x]$.

Write $1=au(x)+xv(x)$ with $u,v\in R[x]$. Evaluation at $x=0$
gives $1=au(0)$. Every nonzero $a\in R$ is therefore invertible.
:::

<1>2. All cyclic subgroups of order $15$ in $S_8$ are conjugate.

::: proof
The order of a permutation is the least common multiple of its
disjoint-cycle lengths [@DF04]. A permutation of order $15$ in
$S_8$ must have a cycle with length divisible by $5$. Every cycle
length divides $15$ and is at most $8$, so this cycle has length
$5$. Only three letters remain. The order must also be divisible
by $3$, so those letters form a $3$-cycle. Thus every generator
of a cyclic subgroup of order $15$ has cycle type $(5)(3)$.

For two such generators, match the five letters in their cyclic
orders and then match the three remaining letters in their cyclic
orders. The resulting permutation conjugates one generator to the
other. It consequently conjugates their generated subgroups.
The subgroup generated by $(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)(6\,7\,8)$ shows
that such subgroups exist.
:::

<1>3. The $\mathbb Z$-module $\mathbb Q$ is torsion-free but not free.

::: proof
For a nonzero integer $n$ and $q\in\mathbb Q$, the equation
$nq=0$ implies $q=0$, so the module is torsion-free.
Moreover, $2\mathbb Q=\mathbb Q$, since $q=2(q/2)$.

If $\mathbb Q$ were free over $\mathbb Z$, its nonzero underlying
module would have a nonempty basis. A basis vector $e$ could not
belong to twice the module: in the unique finite basis expansion
of $2m$, every coefficient is even, whereas the coefficient of
$e$ in itself is $1$. This contradicts $2\mathbb Q=\mathbb Q$.
:::

<1>4. In characteristic $2$ a quadratic extension need not be Galois.

::: proof
Let $F=\mathbb F_2(t)$ with $t$ an indeterminate. The polynomial
$X^2-t$ has no root in $F$. Indeed, a root $A(t)/B(t)$ with
nonzero polynomials $A,B$ would imply $A(t)^2=tB(t)^2$.
The degrees of the two sides would be respectively even and odd,
an impossibility. A quadratic over a field is reducible exactly
when it has a root, so $X^2-t$ is irreducible.

Set $K=F(u)$ where $u^2=t$. The minimal polynomial of $u$ is
$X^2-t$, giving $[K:F]=2$. In characteristic $2$,
$$
X^2-t=(X-u)^2.
$$
Thus the minimal polynomial has a repeated root, and $K/F$ is
not separable. In particular it is not Galois [@DF04].
:::
