::: {.problem}
Let $K$ be the splitting field of $x^4 - 6$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

a. What is the dimension of $K$ over $\mathbb{Q}$?

b. What is the Galois group of $K$ over $\mathbb{Q}$?
You must not only identify the group, but describe how it acts on the roots of $x^4 - 6$.

c. How many subfields does $K$ have of each possible dimension over $\mathbb{Q}$?

d. How many of those subfields in each dimension are Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$?
:::

::: {.solution}
Set $a=\sqrt[4]{6}>0$, and label the roots by
$a_j=i^ja$ for $j\in\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$.

<1>1. The field is $K=\mathbb Q(a,i)$ and
$[K:\mathbb Q]=8$.

::: {.proof}
All four roots lie in $\mathbb Q(a,i)$. Conversely, the
field generated by the roots contains $a$ and $i=(ia)/a$.
The polynomial $x^4-6$ is Eisenstein at $2$, so
$[\mathbb Q(a):\mathbb Q]=4$ [@DF04]. This field lies
in $\mathbb R$, whereas $i$ does not. The minimal polynomial
of $i$ over it is therefore $x^2+1$, and the tower law
gives $[K:\mathbb Q]=2\cdot4=8$.
As a splitting field in characteristic zero, $K/\mathbb Q$
is Galois [@DF04].
:::

<1>2. The Galois group $G$ is the dihedral group of order $8$,
with generators
$$
r(a)=ia,\quad r(i)=i,\qquad
s(a)=a,\quad s(i)=-i.
$$
On the root indices, these act as
$r:j\mapsto j+1$ and $s:j\mapsto-j$.

::: {.proof}
Since $[\mathbb Q(i):\mathbb Q]=2$, step <1>1 gives
$[K:\mathbb Q(i)]=4$. Thus $x^4-6$ is still the minimal
polynomial of $a$ over $\mathbb Q(i)$. Substitution of its
root $ia$ for $a$ therefore defines a $\mathbb Q(i)$-embedding
of $K$ into itself. Its image contains $i$ and
$a=-i(ia)$, so it is the automorphism $r$.
Complex conjugation gives the automorphism $s$.

Their actions on $a_j$ give the stated permutations. In
particular, $r=(0\,1\,2\,3)$ and $s=(1\,3)$.
The eight products $r^us^v$, with $0\leq u<4$ and
$v\in\{0,1\}$, act by
$$
j\longmapsto u+(-1)^vj\pmod4.
$$
They are distinct: evaluating at $0$ determines $u$,
and evaluating at $1$ distinguishes the two values of $v$.
The Galois group has order $8$, so these are all its elements.
They satisfy $r^4=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$ and give precisely
the four rotations and four reflections of the square on
the root indices. This identifies $G$ with the dihedral
group of order $8$.
:::

<1>3. The subgroups of $G$, listed without repetitions, are
$$
\{1\},\quad
\langle r^2\rangle,\quad
\langle r^j s\rangle\ (j=0,1,2,3),\quad
\langle r\rangle,\quad
\langle r^2,s\rangle,\quad
\langle r^2,rs\rangle,\quad G.
$$

::: {.proof}
Write $R=\langle r\rangle$, the cyclic rotation subgroup
of order $4$. Its only subgroups are $\{1\}$,
$\langle r^2\rangle$, and $R$: a subgroup containing
$r$ or $r^3$ is $R$, and any other subgroup is contained
in $\{1,r^2\}$.

Let $H\leq G$, and put $T=H\cap R$. If $H\subseteq R$,
it is one of those three subgroups. Otherwise choose
$r^j s\in H$. Every element of $H$ outside $R$ lies in
the coset $(r^j s)T$, since the quotient of two reflections
is a rotation in $H$. Hence
$$
H=T\mathbin{\sqcup}(r^j s)T.
$$
If $T=\{1\}$, this gives the four order-$2$ subgroups
$\langle r^j s\rangle$. Each is a subgroup because
$(r^j s)^2=1$, and the four are distinct because their
nonidentity elements are distinct.

If $T=\langle r^2\rangle$, this gives
$\{1,r^2,r^j s,r^{j+2}s\}$, which depends exactly on
the parity of $j$. These are the two subgroups
$\langle r^2,s\rangle$ and $\langle r^2,rs\rangle$;
the element $r^2$ commutes with each reflection, so both
displayed sets are indeed subgroups of order $4$.
Finally, $T=R$ gives all of $G$.
These cases exhaust the possible intersections $T$,
proving completeness of the list.
:::

<1>4. The subfield counts, including $\mathbb Q$ and $K$,
are as follows.

| $[L:\mathbb Q]$ | Number of subfields $L$ | Number Galois over $\mathbb Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $2$ | $3$ | $3$ |
| $4$ | $5$ | $1$ |
| $8$ | $1$ | $1$ |

::: {.proof}
The Galois correspondence sends a subgroup $H$ to its fixed
field $K^H$, with $[K^H:\mathbb Q]=8/|H|$. Moreover,
$K^H/\mathbb Q$ is Galois exactly when $H$ is normal
in $G$ [@DF04]. Step <1>3 lists one subgroup of order $1$,
five of order $2$, three of order $4$, and one of order $8$.
This gives the middle column; no other degree is possible
by the tower law.

The subgroups $\{1\}$ and $G$ are normal. All three
order-$4$ subgroups have index $2$ and are normal: both
the left and right coset of an element outside such a
subgroup are its complement. The subgroup
$\langle r^2\rangle$ is central, since $r^2$ commutes
with $r$ and $sr^2s=r^{-2}=r^2$.

None of the four reflection subgroups is normal, since
$$
r(r^j s)r^{-1}=r^{j+2}s\ne r^j s.
$$
Conjugation therefore takes its unique nonidentity element
outside that subgroup. These are all the order-$2$
subgroups apart from $\langle r^2\rangle$, so exactly
one quartic subfield is Galois. This proves the last column.
:::
