::: {.problem}
Prove or give a counterexample to the following assertion. Let $f,g$ be real-valued $C^1$ functions on $(0,1)$ such that
\[
\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=\lim_{x\to0}g(x)=0,
\]
and suppose $g$ and $g'$ never vanish. If
\[
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=c,
\]
must one have
\[
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}=c?
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
The assertion is false.

<1>1. Define on $(0,1)$
$$
g(x)\coloneqq x,
\qquad
f(x)\coloneqq x^2\sin\!\left(\frac1{x^2}\right).
$$
Then $f$ and $g$ satisfy all hypotheses with
$$
c=0.
$$

::: {.proof}
Both functions are $C^1$ on $(0,1)$. Moreover,
$$
g(x)=x\ne0,
\qquad
g'(x)=1\ne0
$$
throughout that interval. As $x\to0^+$,
$$
\abs{f(x)}
\le
x^2
\longrightarrow0,
$$
and $g(x)\to0$. Finally,
$$
\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}
=
x\sin\!\left(\frac1{x^2}\right),
$$
whose absolute value is at most $x$. Hence
$$
\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0.
$$
:::

<1>2. For these functions,
$$
\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}
=
2x\sin\!\left(\frac1{x^2}\right)
-
\frac2x\cos\!\left(\frac1{x^2}\right),
$$
and this expression has no finite limit as $x\to0^+$.

::: {.proof}
Differentiating gives the displayed formula because $g'(x)=1$.

For
$$
x_n\coloneqq\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi n}},
$$
one has
$$
\sin(1/x_n^2)=0,
\qquad
\cos(1/x_n^2)=1,
$$
so
$$
\frac{f'(x_n)}{g'(x_n)}
=
-\frac2{x_n}
\longrightarrow-\infty.
$$
For
$$
y_n\coloneqq\frac1{\sqrt{(2n+1)\pi}},
$$
one has
$$
\sin(1/y_n^2)=0,
\qquad
\cos(1/y_n^2)=-1,
$$
so
$$
\frac{f'(y_n)}{g'(y_n)}
=
\frac2{y_n}
\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$
Thus the derivative quotient cannot converge.
:::

<1>3. Therefore the proposed converse to l'Hôpital's rule fails.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 gives functions satisfying the assumptions with $c=0$, while
step <1>2 shows that
$$
\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}
$$
does not tend to $0$ or to any finite value.
:::

<1>4. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>3 supplies the required counterexample.
:::
:::
