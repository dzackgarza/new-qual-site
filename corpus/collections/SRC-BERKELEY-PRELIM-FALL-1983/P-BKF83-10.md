::: {.problem}
Let $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be uniformly continuous. Suppose
\[
\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_0^b f(x)\,dx
\]
exists and is finite. Prove that
\[
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0.
\]
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Convergence of the improper integral implies the following tail
Cauchy property: for every $\eta>0$ there is $A>0$ such that
$$
\left|
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx
\right|<\eta
$$
whenever $b\ge a\ge A$.

::: {.proof}
Set
$$
F(t)\coloneqq\int_0^t f(x)\,dx.
$$
By hypothesis, $F(t)$ has a finite limit as $t\to\infty$. Hence for
sufficiently large $a,b$,
$$
\abs{F(b)-F(a)}<\eta.
$$
Since
$$
F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx,
$$
the stated property follows.
:::

<1>2. If $f(x)$ does not tend to $0$, then there are
$$
\varepsilon>0
$$
and a sequence $x_n\to\infty$ such that
$$
\abs{f(x_n)}\ge\varepsilon
$$
for every $n$.

::: {.proof}
This is exactly the negation of
$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0.
$$
:::

<1>3. Uniform continuity turns every point $x_n$ from step <1>2 into an
interval of fixed width on which $f$ has the same sign as $f(x_n)$ and
absolute value at least $\varepsilon/2$.

::: {.proof}
Uniform continuity gives $\delta>0$ such that
$$
\abs{x-y}<\delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\abs{f(x)-f(y)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
For every sufficiently large $n$, one has $x_n>\delta/2$. If
$$
x\in
\left[
x_n-\frac{\delta}{2},
x_n+\frac{\delta}{2}
\right],
$$
then
$$
\abs{f(x)-f(x_n)}
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
Since $\abs{f(x_n)}\ge\varepsilon$, this implies that $f(x)$ has the same
sign as $f(x_n)$ and
$$
\abs{f(x)}
\ge
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
:::

<1>4. Steps <1>1 and <1>3 are incompatible.

::: {.proof}
Apply step <1>1 with
$$
\eta\coloneqq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}.
$$
Choose $n$ so large that
$$
x_n-\frac{\delta}{2}\ge A.
$$
Set
$$
a_n=x_n-\frac{\delta}{2},
\qquad
b_n=x_n+\frac{\delta}{2}.
$$
By step <1>3, $f$ has one sign throughout $[a_n,b_n]$ and
$\abs{f(x)}\ge\varepsilon/2$ there. Therefore
$$
\left|
\int_{a_n}^{b_n}f(x)\,dx
\right|
=
\int_{a_n}^{b_n}\abs{f(x)}\,dx
\ge
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\delta
=
\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{2}.
$$
But step <1>1 gives
$$
\left|
\int_{a_n}^{b_n}f(x)\,dx
\right|
<
\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4},
$$
a contradiction.
:::

<1>5. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0}.
$$

::: {.proof}
Step <1>4 contradicts the negation in step <1>2, so the desired limit
must hold.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>5 is the claimed conclusion.
:::
:::
