::: {.problem}
Let $p:X\to Y$ be a quotient map.
Show that if $Y$ is connected and moreover each set $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ is connected, then $X$ is connected.
:::

::: {.solution}
Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $X$ is disconnected.
Choose a separation
\[
X=U\sqcup V,
\]
where $U$ and $V$ are nonempty disjoint open subsets of $X$.

<1>1. Every fiber $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ lies entirely in either $U$ or $V$.
::: {.proof}
Fix $y\in Y$.
Because $p$ is a quotient map, it is surjective, so the fiber $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ is nonempty.
Inside this fiber we have the disjoint union
\[
p^{-1}(\{y\})
=
\bigl(p^{-1}(\{y\})\cap U\bigr)
\sqcup
\bigl(p^{-1}(\{y\})\cap V\bigr).
\]
Both pieces are open in the subspace topology on the fiber.
Since the fiber is connected, they cannot both be nonempty.
Hence the whole fiber is contained in one of $U$ or $V$.
:::

<1>2. The sets $U$ and $V$ are saturated for $p$, and
\[
p^{-1}(p(U))=U,
\qquad
p^{-1}(p(V))=V.
\]
::: {.proof}
Certainly
\[
U\subseteq p^{-1}(p(U)).
\]
Conversely, if $x\in p^{-1}(p(U))$, then there is $u\in U$ with $p(x)=p(u)$.
Thus $x$ and $u$ lie in the same fiber.
By <1>1 that fiber, since it meets $U$, lies entirely in $U$.
Hence $x\in U$.
The proof for $V$ is identical.
:::

<1>3. The sets $p(U)$ and $p(V)$ form a separation of $Y$.
::: {.proof}
By <1>2 and the defining property of a quotient map,
\[
p^{-1}(p(U))=U\text{ open}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p(U)\text{ open in }Y,
\]
and similarly $p(V)$ is open.
Both images are nonempty because $U$ and $V$ are nonempty.
Surjectivity gives
\[
Y=p(U)\cup p(V).
\]
They are disjoint: if $y\in p(U)\cap p(V)$, then its fiber meets both $U$ and $V$, contradicting <1>1. Thus $Y$ is disconnected.
:::

<1>4. Therefore $X$ is connected.
::: {.proof}
The separation of $Y$ in <1>3 contradicts the hypothesis that $Y$ is connected.
Hence the assumed separation of $X$ cannot exist.
:::
:::
