::: exercise
3. In this problem, consider the group $G$ of matrices $\left( \begin{array} { l l } { a } & { b } \\ { c } & { d } \end{array} \right)$ with integer entries, determinant 1, and such that a and d have the same parity, b and c have the same parity, and c and d have opposite parity.
   This group also acts on the upper half-plane by fractional linear transformations.
   To the group $G$ corresponds the fundamental domain $\mathcal { F }$ defined by $| \tau | \geq 1 , | \mathrm { R e } ( \tau ) | \leq 1$ , and Im $. ( \tau ) \geq 0$ (see Figure 1). Also, let

$$
S (\tau) = - 1 / \tau \leftrightarrow \left( \begin{array}{c c} 0 & - 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array} \right) \quad \text { and } \quad T _ {2} (\tau) = \tau + 2 \leftrightarrow \left( \begin{array}{c c} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array} \right).
$$

Prove that every fractional linear transformation corresponding to $g \in G$ is a composition of finitely many S, $T _ { 2 }$ and their inverses, in analogy with the previous problem.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Define the subgroup $G_0 = \langle S, T_2 \rangle \le \operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ generated by $S$ and $T_2$: <2>1. $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in G$ because $a=0, d=0$ have the same parity, $b=-1, c=1$ have the same parity, and $c=1, d=0$ have opposite parity.
Proof: parity check of matrix entries.
<2>2. $T_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in G$ because $a=1, d=1$ are odd, $b=2, c=0$ are even, and $c=0, d=1$ have opposite parity.
Proof: parity check of matrix entries.
<2>3. Since $G$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, the subgroup $G_0 = \langle S, T_2 \rangle \subseteq G$ (modulo $\pm I$). Proof: $S, T_2 \in G$ and $G$ is closed under multiplication and inverses.

<1>2. Show that every orbit of $G_0$ on the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}$ intersects the fundamental domain $\mathcal{F} = \{\tau \in \mathbb{H} : |\tau| \ge 1, \; |\operatorname{Re}(\tau)| \le 1\}$: <2>1. For any $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in G_0$ and $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$, the imaginary part transforms as:
\[
\operatorname{Im}(M(\tau)) = \frac{\operatorname{Im}(\tau)}{|c\tau + d|^2}.
\]
Proof: standard formula for fractional linear transformations $\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right) = \frac{(ad-bc)\operatorname{Im}(\tau)}{|c\tau+d|^2}$ with $\det M = 1$.
<2>2. For fixed $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$, the set of points $c\tau + d$ as $(c, d)$ ranges over bottom rows of matrices in $G_0 \subseteq \operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is a subset of the discrete lattice $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z}\tau + \mathbb{Z}$.
Proof: $c, d \in \mathbb{Z}$.
<2>3. Since $\Lambda$ is discrete, $|c\tau + d|$ achieves a positive minimum on this set, so there exists $M_0 \in G_0$ such that $\tau' = M_0(\tau)$ maximizes $\operatorname{Im}(\gamma(\tau))$ over all $\gamma \in G_0$.
Proof: discreteness of the lattice in $\mathbb{C}$.
<2>4. Since the length of the interval $[-1, 1]$ is 2, there exists an integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $\tau'' = T_2^k(\tau') = \tau' + 2k$ satisfies $|\operatorname{Re}(\tau'')| \le 1$.
Proof: rounding to the nearest odd/even integer: for any $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $\exists k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $x + 2k \in [-1, 1]$.
<2>5. Since $T_2^k$ is a horizontal translation, $\operatorname{Im}(\tau'') = \operatorname{Im}(\tau')$, so $\tau''$ also maximizes the imaginary part in the $G_0$-orbit.
Proof: $\operatorname{Im}(\tau + 2k) = \operatorname{Im}(\tau)$.
<2>6. $|\tau''| \ge 1$: if $|\tau''| < 1$, then $S(\tau'') = -1/\tau'' \in G_0(\tau)$ would have:
\[
\operatorname{Im}(S(\tau'')) = \frac{\operatorname{Im}(\tau'')}{|\tau''|^2} > \operatorname{Im}(\tau''),
\]
contradicting the maximality of $\operatorname{Im}(\tau'')$ in the orbit $G_0(\tau)$.
Proof: $|\tau''| < 1 \implies 1/|\tau''|^2 > 1$.
<2>7. Thus $|\tau''| \ge 1$ and $|\operatorname{Re}(\tau'')| \le 1$, so $\tau'' \in \mathcal{F}$.
Proof: <2>4 and <2>6.

<1>3. Prove that $G = G_0$ (modulo $\pm I$): <2>1. Fix an interior point $\tau_0 \in \operatorname{int}(\mathcal{F})$ that is not fixed by any non-trivial element of $\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ (for example $\tau_0 = 2i$). Proof: $\tau_0 = 2i$ satisfies $|\tau_0| = 2 > 1$ and $\operatorname{Re}(\tau_0) = 0 \in (-1, 1)$.
<2>2. Let $g \in G$ be arbitrary.
Consider the point $w = g(\tau_0) \in \mathbb{H}$.
Proof: $g \in G$ acts on $\mathbb{H}$.
<2>3. By <1>2, there exists $\gamma \in G_0$ such that $\gamma(w) = (\gamma g)(\tau_0) \in \mathcal{F}$.
Proof: <1>2 applied to $w$.
<2>4. The transformation $h = \gamma g \in G$.
Since $\tau_0 \in \operatorname{int}(\mathcal{F})$ and $h(\tau_0) \in \mathcal{F}$, geometry of the fundamental domain $\mathcal{F}$ for $G$ implies $h(\tau_0) = \tau_0$.
Proof: the translates of the interior of $\mathcal{F}$ under $G$ are pairwise disjoint.
<2>5. The only element of $\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ fixing $\tau_0 = 2i$ in $G$ is the identity transformation.
Proof: $c(2i)^2 + (d-a)(2i) - b = 0 \implies -4c - b + 2(d-a)i = 0 \implies d = a, b = -4c$.
With $ad - bc = a^2 + 4c^2 = 1$, the only integer solution is $a = \pm 1, c = 0, b = 0, d = \pm 1$.
<2>6. Thus $\gamma g = \pm I$, which means $g = \pm \gamma^{-1} \in G_0$.
Proof: <2>4 and <2>5.

<1>4. Conclusion: Every transformation in $G$ is a finite composition of $S, T_2$, and their inverses.
Q.E.D. Proof: <1>3.
:::
