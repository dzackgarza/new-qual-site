::: {.exercise}
Give a reasonable definition of a non-negative $n \times n$ matrix, and then prove that such a matrix has a unique non-negative square root.
:::

::: solution
Call a complex matrix $A$ **non-negative** if
$$
A=A^*
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
X^*AX\ge0
$$
for every column vector $X$. Equivalently, $A$ is self-adjoint and all of its
characteristic values are non-negative.

<1>1. Every non-negative matrix has a non-negative square root.
::: proof
By the spectral theorem there is a unitary matrix $U$ and non-negative real
numbers $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ such that
$$
A=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)U^*.
$$
Define
$$
B
=U\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_n})U^*.
$$
Then $B=B^*$ and its characteristic values are the non-negative numbers
$\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, so $B$ is non-negative. Moreover
$$
B^2
=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)U^*
=A.
$$
Thus a non-negative square root exists.
:::

<1>2. Any non-negative square root of $A$ commutes with $A$ and preserves every eigenspace of $A$.
::: proof
Suppose $C$ is non-negative and $C^2=A$. Then
$$
CA=C^3=AC.
$$
If $Av=\lambda v$, then
$$
A(Cv)=C(Av)=\lambda Cv,
$$
so each eigenspace
$$
E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)
$$
is invariant under $C$.
:::

<1>3. The restriction of a non-negative square root to $E_\lambda$ is $\sqrt\lambda I$.
::: proof
On $E_\lambda$ one has
$$
C^2=\lambda I.
$$
The restriction $C|_{E_\lambda}$ is self-adjoint and non-negative, hence is
unitarily diagonalizable with non-negative characteristic values $\mu$.
Every such $\mu$ satisfies
$$
\mu^2=\lambda.
$$
Because $\mu\ge0$, necessarily
$$
\mu=\sqrt\lambda.
$$
Therefore all characteristic values of $C|_{E_\lambda}$ are equal to
$\sqrt\lambda$, and since the restriction is diagonalizable,
$$
C|_{E_\lambda}=\sqrt\lambda I.
$$
:::

<1>4. The non-negative square root is unique.
::: proof
The eigenspaces of the self-adjoint matrix $A$ form an orthogonal direct sum
of the whole space. By step <1>3, every non-negative square root acts on each
$E_\lambda$ as multiplication by the same scalar $\sqrt\lambda$. Hence every
non-negative square root equals the matrix $B$ constructed in step <1>1.
Therefore
$$
\boxed{A\text{ has a unique non-negative square root}.}
$$
:::
:::
