::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be the linear operator on $\RR^3$ which is represented in the standard ordered basis by the matrix
$$
\begin{bmatrix} -9 & 4 & 4 \\ -8 & 3 & 4 \\ -16 & 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
Prove that $T$ is diagonalizable by exhibiting a basis for $\RR^3$, each vector of which is a characteristic vector of $T$.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise: the collection's provenance is an archive.org record, so the argument is the card's own. Let $A$ be the matrix of $T$ in the standard basis of $\RR^3$,
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}-9&4&4\\ -8&3&4\\ -16&8&7\end{bmatrix}.
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #charpoly}
$\chi_A(x)=\det(xI-A)=x^3-x^2-5x-3=(x-3)(x+1)^2$.

::: pf-proof
Expanding the determinant of
$$
xI-A=\begin{bmatrix}x+9&-4&-4\\ 8&x-3&-4\\ 16&-8&x-7\end{bmatrix}
$$
along the first row gives $(x+9)\bigl((x-3)(x-7)-32\bigr)+4\bigl(8(x-7)+64\bigr)-4\bigl(-64-16(x-3)\bigr)$, that is $(x+9)(x^2-10x-11)+4(8x+8)-4(-16x-16)=x^3-x^2-5x-3$ after collecting. Factoring, $(x-3)(x+1)^2=x^3+2x^2+x-3x^2-6x-3=x^3-x^2-5x-3$, which agrees.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #eigenvectors}
$A(1,2,0)^{t}=-(1,2,0)^{t}$, $A(1,0,2)^{t}=-(1,0,2)^{t}$ and $A(1,1,2)^{t}=3(1,1,2)^{t}$.

::: pf-proof
Multiply out. $A(1,2,0)^{t}=(-9+8,\,-8+6,\,-16+16)^{t}=(-1,-2,0)^{t}=-(1,2,0)^{t}$. $A(1,0,2)^{t}=(-9+8,\,-8+8,\,-16+14)^{t}=(-1,0,-2)^{t}=-(1,0,2)^{t}$. $A(1,1,2)^{t}=(-9+4+8,\,-8+3+8,\,-16+8+14)^{t}=(3,3,6)^{t}=3(1,1,2)^{t}$. So the three vectors are characteristic vectors, for the eigenvalues $-1,-1,3$, which are the roots of $\chi_A$ found in step [](#charpoly){.pf-ref}.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #independent}
The vectors $v_1=(1,2,0)$, $v_2=(1,0,2)$, $v_3=(1,1,2)$ are linearly independent.

::: pf-proof
The matrix with these as columns is $\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\ 2&0&1\\ 0&2&2\end{bmatrix}$, whose determinant is $1\cdot(0\cdot2-1\cdot2)-1\cdot(2\cdot2-1\cdot0)+1\cdot(2\cdot2-0\cdot0)=-2-4+4=-2\ne0$. A square matrix with nonzero determinant has independent columns, so the three vectors are independent.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$T$ is diagonalizable: the three $v_j$ form a basis of $\RR^3$ consisting of characteristic vectors of $T$.

::: pf-proof
Three independent vectors in the three-dimensional space $\RR^3$ form a basis. Each is a characteristic vector of $T$, since a vector that is a characteristic vector of the matrix $A$ is one of the operator $T$ it represents in the standard basis: $Av=\lambda v$ means $T(v)=\lambda v$ with coordinates taken in that basis. A basis of characteristic vectors diagonalizes $T$: the matrix of $T$ in the ordered basis $(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ is $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,3)$, and a change of basis carries the matrix of $T$ in the standard basis to it. So $T$ is diagonalizable, which is what the exercise asks to be shown by exhibiting such a basis.
:::
