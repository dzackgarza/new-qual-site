::: {.exercise}
Use the result of Exercise 11 to prove the following: If $A$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix with complex entries, then $A$ is similar over $\CC$ to a matrix of one of the two types
$$
\begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{bmatrix} \qquad \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 1 & a \end{bmatrix}.
$$
:::

::: {.solution}

The argument is the card's own, with Exercise 11 of the same section supplying the normal form of a nilpotent $2\times2$ matrix in the non-diagonalisable case.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #split}

The characteristic polynomial of $A$ has degree $2$ and splits over $\CC$.

::: pf-proof

$\det(xI-A)$ is a monic polynomial of degree $2$ with coefficients in $\CC$, and $\CC$ is algebraically closed, so it factors as $(x-a)(x-b)$ with $a,b\in\CC$; the roots $a,b$ are the eigenvalues of $A$, and they are the diagonal entries in the two target types.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #distinct}

If $a\ne b$ then $A$ is similar over $\CC$ to $\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\0&b\end{bmatrix}$.

::: pf-proof

Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent, so choosing $v$ with $Av=av$ and $w$ with $Aw=bw$ gives a basis $(v,w)$ of the two-dimensional space $\CC^{2}$. In that ordered basis the matrix of the operator $x\mapsto Ax$ has columns the coordinate tuples of $Av=av$ and $Aw=bw$, namely $(a,0)^{t}$ and $(0,b)^{t}$. So $A$ is similar to $\operatorname{diag}(a,b)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #nilpotent}

If the characteristic polynomial is $(x-a)^{2}$ and $N=A-aI$, then $N^{2}=0$.

::: pf-proof

The characteristic polynomial of $N=A-aI$ is $\det\big(xI-(A-aI)\big)=\det\big((x+a)I-A\big)$, which is the characteristic polynomial of $A$ evaluated at $x+a$, namely $x^{2}$. By the Cayley--Hamilton theorem $N$ satisfies its own characteristic polynomial, so $N^{2}=0$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #cases}

If $N=0$ then $A=aI$; if $N\ne0$ then $A$ is similar to $\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\1&a\end{bmatrix}$.

::: pf-proof

If $N=0$, then $A=aI=\operatorname{diag}(a,a)$, of the first type. Suppose $N\ne0$. By step [](#nilpotent){.pf-ref}, $N^{2}=0\ne N$, so $N$ is a nilpotent matrix whose square is zero and which is not zero, and Exercise 11 of this section gives an ordered basis in which the matrix of $N$ is $\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$. In that basis the matrix of the operator $aI$ is $aI$ in every basis, so
$$
A=aI+N\ \text{has matrix}\ aI+\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\1&a\end{bmatrix}.
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #conclusion}

$A$ is similar over $\CC$ to a matrix of one of the two stated types.

::: pf-proof

Steps [](#distinct){.pf-ref} and [](#cases){.pf-ref} cover the two possibilities for the split characteristic polynomial of step [](#split){.pf-ref}: either it has distinct roots and $A$ is similar to $\operatorname{diag}(a,b)$, or it is $(x-a)^{2}$ and $A$ is similar to $\operatorname{diag}(a,a)$ or to $\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\1&a\end{bmatrix}$:
$$
\boxed{A\sim_{\CC}\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\0&b\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad A\sim_{\CC}\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\1&a\end{bmatrix}.}
$$

:::
