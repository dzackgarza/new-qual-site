::: {.exercise}
Let $\sigma$ and $\tau$ be the permutations of degree 4 defined by $\sigma 1 = 2$, $\sigma 2 = 3$, $\sigma 3 = 4$, $\sigma 4 = 1$, $\tau 1 = 3$, $\tau 2 = 1$, $\tau 3 = 2$, $\tau 4 = 4$.

(a) Is $\sigma$ odd or even?
Is $\tau$ odd or even?

(b) Find $\sigma\tau$ and $\tau\sigma$.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise: the collection's provenance is an archive.org record, so the argument is the card's own. Let $\sigma,\tau\in S_4$ be as in the statement, and use the convention $(\sigma\tau)(i)=\sigma(\tau(i))$, so that the permutation acts on the left of the point and products are read right to left.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #cycles}
$\sigma=(1\,2\,3\,4)$ and $\tau=(1\,3\,2)$, with $\tau(4)=4$.

::: pf-proof
A cycle $(a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_r)$ is the permutation sending $a_j\mapsto a_{j+1}$ for $j<r$, $a_r\mapsto a_1$, and fixing every other point. For $\sigma$: $\sigma1=2$, $\sigma2=3$, $\sigma3=4$, $\sigma4=1$, exactly the cycle $(1\,2\,3\,4)$. For $\tau$: $\tau1=3$, $\tau3=2$, $\tau2=1$, and $\tau4=4$, exactly the cycle $(1\,3\,2)$ with $4$ fixed.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #sign}
The sign $\operatorname{sgn}:S_n\to\{\pm1\}$ is a homomorphism and $\operatorname{sgn}(\text{a transposition})=-1$; hence a product of $m$ transpositions has sign $(-1)^m$ and the parity of $m$ depends only on the permutation.

::: pf-proof
Take $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=\det(P_\pi)$, where $P_\pi$ is the permutation matrix of $\pi$, that is $P_\pi e_j=e_{\pi(j)}$. The determinant is multiplicative and $P_{\pi\rho}=P_\pi P_\rho$, so $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi\rho)=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)$; and the permutation matrix of the transposition $(a\,b)$ differs from the identity by exchanging two rows, so its determinant is $-1$. Hence a product of $m$ transpositions has sign $(-1)^m$, and since $(-1)^m$ is determined by $\operatorname{sgn}$ alone, the parity of any expression of $\pi$ as a product of transpositions is determined by $\pi$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #parity}
$\sigma$ is odd and $\tau$ is even.

::: pf-proof
The cycle identity $(a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_r)=(a_1\,a_r)(a_1\,a_{r-1})\cdots(a_1\,a_2)$ writes an $r$-cycle as $r-1$ transpositions: apply the right side to $a_1$, which is moved successively to $a_2,\dots,a_r$ and finally to $a_1$ by the leftmost factor, and check the other points. So by step [](#cycles){.pf-ref} $\sigma=(1\,2\,3\,4)$ is a product of $3$ transpositions and $\tau=(1\,3\,2)$ of $2$; by step [](#sign){.pf-ref} $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=(-1)^3=-1$ and $\operatorname{sgn}\tau=(-1)^2=1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #sigma_tau}
$\sigma\tau=(1\,4)$.

::: pf-proof
Evaluate at each point, using $(\sigma\tau)(i)=\sigma(\tau(i))$ and step [](#cycles){.pf-ref}: $(\sigma\tau)(1)=\sigma(\tau(1))=\sigma(3)=4$; $(\sigma\tau)(2)=\sigma(\tau(2))=\sigma(1)=2$; $(\sigma\tau)(3)=\sigma(\tau(3))=\sigma(2)=3$; $(\sigma\tau)(4)=\sigma(\tau(4))=\sigma(4)=1$. So the product moves $1\mapsto4\mapsto1$ and fixes $2,3$, which is $(1\,4)$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #tau_sigma}
$\tau\sigma=(3\,4)$.

::: pf-proof
Again pointwise: $(\tau\sigma)(1)=\tau(\sigma(1))=\tau(2)=1$; $(\tau\sigma)(2)=\tau(3)=2$; $(\tau\sigma)(3)=\tau(4)=4$; $(\tau\sigma)(4)=\tau(1)=3$. So the product fixes $1,2$ and exchanges $3,4$, which is $(3\,4)$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$\sigma$ is odd, $\tau$ is even, $\sigma\tau=(1\,4)$ and $\tau\sigma=(3\,4)$; in particular $\sigma\tau\ne\tau\sigma$.

::: pf-proof
The parities are step [](#parity){.pf-ref} and the two products are steps [](#sigma_tau){.pf-ref} and [](#tau_sigma){.pf-ref}. The two results differ as permutations --- $(1\,4)$ moves $1$ and $(3\,4)$ fixes it --- so $\sigma\tau\ne\tau\sigma$.
:::
