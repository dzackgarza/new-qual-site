::: {.exercise}
If $\sigma$ is a permutation of degree $n$ and $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix over the field $F$ with row vectors $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$, let $\sigma(A)$ denote the $n \times n$ matrix with row vectors $\alpha_{\sigma 1}, \ldots, \alpha_{\sigma n}$.

(a) Prove that $\sigma(AB) = \sigma(A)B$, and in particular that $\sigma(A) = \sigma(I)A$.

(b) If $T$ is the linear operator of Exercise 9, prove that the matrix of $T$ in the standard ordered basis is $\sigma(I)$.

(c) Is $\sigma^{-1}(I)$ the inverse matrix of $\sigma(I)$?

(d) Is it true that $\sigma(A)$ is similar to $A$?
:::

::: {.solution}

The argument is the card's own. Let $A$ have rows $\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}$, so that $\sigma(A)$ has rows $\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(n)}$; the operation is a permutation of the row list.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #rows}

(a) The $i$-th row of $\sigma(AB)$ equals the $i$-th row of $\sigma(A)B$.

::: pf-proof

The $j$-th row of $AB$ is $\alpha_{j}B$. By the definition of $\sigma(\cdot)$, the $i$-th row of $\sigma(AB)$ is the $\sigma(i)$-th row of $AB$, that is $\alpha_{\sigma(i)}B$. On the other hand the $i$-th row of $\sigma(A)$ is $\alpha_{\sigma(i)}$, so the $i$-th row of $\sigma(A)B$ is $\alpha_{\sigma(i)}B$ as well. Two matrices with equal rows in every position are equal.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #parta}

(a) $\sigma(AB)=\sigma(A)B$, and in particular $\sigma(A)=\sigma(I)A$.

::: pf-proof

The first claim is step [](#rows){.pf-ref} applied in every row index. For the second, apply the first with $A:=I$: $\sigma(IB)=\sigma(I)B$, and $IB=B$, so $\sigma(B)=\sigma(I)B$ for every $B$; renaming $B$ as $A$ gives $\sigma(A)=\sigma(I)A$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #partb}

(b) The matrix of the operator $T(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})$ in the standard ordered basis is $\sigma(I)$.

::: pf-proof

Write $x$ for the column vector with entries $x_{1},\dots,x_{n}$. The $i$-th coordinate of $\sigma(I)x$ is the product of the $i$-th row of $\sigma(I)$ with $x$; by step [](#parta){.pf-ref} the $i$-th row of $\sigma(I)$ is the $\sigma(i)$-th row of $I$, namely the standard vector $e_{\sigma(i)}$, so that product is $x_{\sigma(i)}$. Hence $\sigma(I)x=(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})^{t}$, which is exactly $Tx$ in coordinates; the matrix of $T$ in the standard basis is therefore $\sigma(I)$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #partc}

(c) Yes: $\sigma^{-1}(I)$ is the inverse of $\sigma(I)$.

::: pf-proof

Apply step [](#parta){.pf-ref} with the permutation $\sigma^{-1}$ and with $A:=\sigma(I)$:
$$
\sigma^{-1}\big(\sigma(I)\big)=\sigma^{-1}(I)\,\sigma(I).
$$
Permuting the rows of $\sigma(I)$ by $\sigma^{-1}$ undoes the permutation $\sigma$, returning the rows $\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}$ of $I$; so the left side is $I$, and $\sigma^{-1}(I)\sigma(I)=I$. The same argument with $\sigma$ and $\sigma^{-1}$ interchanged gives $\sigma(I)\sigma^{-1}(I)=I$. A square matrix with a two-sided inverse is invertible and the inverse is that matrix, so $\sigma^{-1}(I)=\sigma(I)^{-1}$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #partd}

(d) No: $\sigma(A)$ need not be similar to $A$.

::: pf-proof

Take $A=I$ and $\sigma$ any permutation with $\sigma(1)\ne1$. Then $\sigma(I)$ has $i$-th row $e_{\sigma(i)}$, so its first row is $e_{\sigma(1)}\ne e_{1}$ and $\sigma(I)\ne I$. A matrix similar to $I$ is $P^{-1}IP=I$ for every invertible $P$, so $\sigma(I)$ is not similar to $I$:
$$
\boxed{\sigma(A)\ne\text{similar to }A\ \text{in general: }A=I,\ \sigma\ne\mathrm{id}\ \text{gives}\ \sigma(I)\ne I\ \text{while every conjugate of } I \text{ is } I.}
$$

:::
