::: {.exercise}
An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is called triangular if $A_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i > j$ or if $A_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i < j$.
Prove that the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product $A_{11}A_{22} \cdots A_{nn}$ of its diagonal entries.
:::

::: {.solution}
**Goal.** Show $\det A = A_{11} A_{22} \cdots A_{nn}$ for a triangular matrix.

<1>1. It suffices to consider the upper-triangular case (the lower-triangular case is identical by transposition, since $\det A = \det A^T$).
Proof: $\det A = \det A^T$, and the transpose of a lower-triangular matrix is upper-triangular.

<1>2. Expand the determinant along the first column.
<2>1. In an upper-triangular matrix, the first column has only one nonzero entry, $A_{11}$.
Proof: $A_{i1} = 0$ for $i > 1$.
<2>2. Hence $\det A = A_{11} \cdot \det A_{11}$, where $A_{11}$ is the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ minor obtained by deleting the first row and column.
Proof: cofactor expansion along the first column.
<2>3. $A_{11}$ is again upper-triangular with diagonal $A_{22}, \dots, A_{nn}$.
Proof: deleting the first row and column of an upper-triangular matrix gives an upper-triangular matrix.

<1>3. Induct on $n$.
<2>1. Base case $n = 1$: $\det A = A_{11}$.
Proof: trivial.
<2>2. Inductive step: $\det A = A_{11} \det A_{11} = A_{11} (A_{22} \cdots A_{nn})$.
Proof: by <1>2.2 and the induction hypothesis applied to $A_{11}$.

<1>4. Q.E.D.
Proof: <1>3.2 gives $\det A = A_{11} A_{22} \cdots A_{nn}$.
:::
