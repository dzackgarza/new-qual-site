::: {.exercise}
Let $F$ be a field, $n$ a positive integer, and $S$ the set of $n \times n$ matrices over $F$.
Let $V$ be the vector space of all functions from $S$ into $F$.
Let $W$ be the set of alternating $n$-linear functions on $S$.
Prove that $W$ is a subspace of $V$.
What is the dimension of $W$?
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise: the collection's provenance is an archive.org record, so the argument is the card's own. Let $S=M_n(F)$, let $V$ be the space of all functions $S\to F$, and let $W\subset V$ be the set of alternating $n$-linear functions, meaning functions $D$ of the $n$ row arguments which are linear in each argument separately and which vanish whenever two arguments are equal. Write $e_1,\dots,e_n$ for the standard basis of $F^n$ and $I$ for the identity matrix.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #subspace}
$W$ is a subspace of $V$.

::: pf-proof
Let $D,E\in W$ and $a,b\in F$. For each $i$, the map $A\mapsto aD(A)+bE(A)$ is linear in the $i$-th row because $D$ and $E$ are, and a linear combination of linear maps is linear; so $aD+bE$ is $n$-linear. If two rows of $A$ are equal then $D(A)=E(A)=0$, so $(aD+bE)(A)=0$, so it is alternating. The zero function is $n$-linear and alternating. Hence $W$ is nonempty and closed under linear combinations.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #sign_lemma}
If $D\in W$ then $D(\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(n)})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)D(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ for every permutation $\sigma$ and all row vectors $\alpha_i$.

::: pf-proof
First, swapping two arguments changes the sign: for arguments $u,v$ in positions $i<j$ and all others fixed, multilinearity gives
$$
0=D(\dots,u+v,\dots,u+v,\dots)=D(\dots,u,\dots,u,\dots)+D(\dots,u,\dots,v,\dots)+D(\dots,v,\dots,u,\dots)+D(\dots,v,\dots,v,\dots),
$$
where the two terms with equal arguments vanish by alternation, so $D(\dots,v,\dots,u,\dots)=-D(\dots,u,\dots,v,\dots)$. Now write $\sigma=\tau_1\cdots\tau_m$ as a product of $m$ transpositions, so $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=(-1)^m$; applying the swap rule once per transposition multiplies $D$ by $(-1)^m$, giving the claim. (That $\operatorname{sgn}$ is well defined for this purpose is the same fact used in the definition of the determinant.)
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #expand}
For $D\in W$ and $A$ with rows $\alpha_i=\sum_jA_{ij}e_j$,
$$
D(A)=\sum_{(j_1,\dots,j_n)}\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,j_i}\Bigr)D(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_n}).
$$

::: pf-proof
Apply $n$-linearity once for each row: replacing $\alpha_i$ by $\sum_jA_{ij}e_j$ in the $i$-th slot and expanding gives the displayed sum over all $n$-tuples $(j_1,\dots,j_n)$ with $1\le j_i\le n$, the coefficients being the products of the $A_{i,j_i}$; this is the iterated multilinear expansion, and every scalar $A_{ij}$ may be pulled out of all $n$ slots because the function is linear in each.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #vanish}
In the sum of step [](#expand){.pf-ref}, every term whose indices are not pairwise distinct vanishes, so
$$
D(A)=D(I)\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,\sigma(i)} .
$$

::: pf-proof
If $j_p=j_q$ for some $p<q$, the term contains $D(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_n})$ with two equal arguments, which is $0$ by alternation. The remaining tuples are exactly the permutations $j_i=\sigma(i)$, and for those step [](#sign_lemma){.pf-ref} gives $D(e_{\sigma(1)},\dots,e_{\sigma(n)})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)D(e_1,\dots,e_n)=D(I)\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$. Collecting the coefficient of $D(I)$ gives the displayed sum, which is $D(I)\det A$ by the Leibniz formula.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #spanning}
Every $D\in W$ satisfies $D=D(I)\det$, so $W\subseteq F\det$.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#vanish){.pf-ref} gives $D(A)=D(I)\det A$ for every $A\in S$. So $D$ is the scalar $D(I)$ times the determinant function.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #converse}
$\det\in W$ and $\det\ne0$, so $F\det\subseteq W$ and $F\det$ is one-dimensional.

::: pf-proof
The determinant is $n$-linear and alternating in the rows, which is the standard property of $\det$ following from its Leibniz formula and from the vanishing of a determinant with two equal rows; and $\det I=1\ne0$, so it is not the zero function. Hence $\det\in W$, and its scalar multiples are in $W$ because $W$ is a subspace by step [](#subspace){.pf-ref}. Since $\det\ne0$, the multiples $c\det$ are distinct for distinct $c$, so $F\det$ is one-dimensional.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$W=F\det$ and $\dim W=1$.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#spanning){.pf-ref} and [](#converse){.pf-ref} give the two inclusions $W\subseteq F\det$ and $F\det\subseteq W$; the last step also shows $F\det$ is one-dimensional, so $W$ is a subspace of $V$ of dimension $1$. That answers both questions of the exercise.
:::
