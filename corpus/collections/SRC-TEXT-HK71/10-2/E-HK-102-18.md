::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex vector space and let $f$ be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on $V$.
Show that there is a basis
\[
\mathfrak B=\{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\}
\]
whose $f$-dual basis is $\mathfrak B$ itself.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. Choose a basis in which the symmetric form is diagonal.
::: proof
By diagonalization of symmetric bilinear forms, there is a basis
$$
e_1,\ldots,e_n
$$
such that
$$
f(e_i,e_j)=0
\qquad(i\ne j).
$$
Write
$$
\lambda_i=f(e_i,e_i).
$$

Every $\lambda_i$ is nonzero. Indeed, if $\lambda_i=0$, then $e_i$ is
orthogonal to every basis vector $e_j$, hence to all of $V$. This would put
$e_i$ in the radical of $f$, contradicting non-degeneracy.
:::

<1>2. Rescale the diagonal basis to make every diagonal value equal to one.
::: proof
Because the scalar field is $\mathbb C$ and $\lambda_i\ne0$, choose
$\mu_i\in\mathbb C^\times$ such that
$$
\mu_i^2=\lambda_i.
$$
Set
$$
\alpha_i=\mu_i^{-1}e_i.
$$
Then for $i\ne j$,
$$
f(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)=0,
$$
while
$$
f(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)
=\mu_i^{-2}f(e_i,e_i)
=\mu_i^{-2}\lambda_i
=1.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{f(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)=\delta_{ij}.}
$$
:::

<1>3. Identify the $f$-dual basis.
::: proof
By definition, the $f$-dual basis to
$$
\mathfrak B=\{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\}
$$
is the unique basis
$\{\alpha'_1,\ldots,\alpha'_n\}$ satisfying
$$
f(\alpha_i,\alpha'_j)=\delta_{ij}.
$$
Step <1>2 shows that the vectors $\alpha'_j=\alpha_j$ already satisfy these
relations. Uniqueness of the $f$-dual basis from Exercise 13 therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak B'=\mathfrak B.}
$$
:::
:::
