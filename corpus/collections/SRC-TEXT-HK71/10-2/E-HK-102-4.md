::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a subfield $F$ of the complex numbers, and let $S$ be the set of all symmetric bilinear forms on $V$.

(a) Show that $S$ is a subspace of $L(V, V, F)$.

(b) Find $\dim S$.

Let $Q$ be the set of all quadratic forms on $V$.

(c) Show that $Q$ is a subspace of the space of all functions from $V$ into $F$.

(d) Describe explicitly an isomorphism $T$ of $Q$ onto $S$, without reference to a basis.

(e) Let $U$ be a linear operator on $V$ and $q$ an element of $Q$.
Show that the equation $(U^{\dagger}q)(\alpha) = q(U\alpha)$ defines a quadratic form $U^{\dagger}q$ on $V$.

(f) If $U$ is a linear operator on $V$, show that the function $U^{\dagger}$ defined in part (e) is a linear operator on $Q$.
Show that $U^{\dagger}$ is invertible if and only if $U$ is invertible.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
n=\dim V.
$$
Since $F$ is a subfield of $\mathbb C$, its characteristic is zero and in
particular $2$ is invertible in $F$.

<1>1. The symmetric bilinear forms form a vector subspace.
::: {.proof}
If $f,g\in S$ and $a,b\in F$, then $af+bg$ is bilinear. Moreover
$$
\begin{aligned}
(af+bg)(\alpha,\beta)
&=a f(\alpha,\beta)+b g(\alpha,\beta)\\
&=a f(\beta,\alpha)+b g(\beta,\alpha)\\
&=(af+bg)(\beta,\alpha).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $af+bg\in S$. The zero bilinear form is symmetric, so $S$ is a subspace
of $L(V,V,F)$.
:::

<1>2. Compute $\dim S$.
::: {.proof}
Choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V$. A symmetric bilinear form is
represented in this basis by a symmetric $n\times n$ matrix. Such a matrix is
determined freely by the $n$ diagonal entries and the
$$
\binom n2
$$
entries above the diagonal. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dim S=n+\binom n2=\frac{n(n+1)}2.}
$$
:::

<1>3. The quadratic forms form a vector subspace of all functions $V\to F$.
::: {.proof}
Every quadratic form is of the form
$$
q_f(\alpha)=f(\alpha,\alpha)
$$
for a symmetric bilinear form $f$. If $q_f,q_g\in Q$ and $a,b\in F$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
(a q_f+b q_g)(\alpha)
&=a f(\alpha,\alpha)+b g(\alpha,\alpha)\\
&=(af+bg)(\alpha,\alpha).
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $af+bg$ is symmetric by step <1>1, $a q_f+b q_g\in Q$. The zero
function is the quadratic form associated with the zero bilinear form. Thus
$Q$ is a vector subspace of the function space $F^V$.
:::

<1>4. Construct the basis-free isomorphism $T:Q\to S$.
::: {.proof}
For $q\in Q$, define
$$
(Tq)(\alpha,\beta)
=\frac12\bigl(q(\alpha+\beta)-q(\alpha)-q(\beta)\bigr).
$$
This formula makes no reference to a basis.

If $q=q_f$ for a symmetric bilinear form $f$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
q(\alpha+\beta)-q(\alpha)-q(\beta)
&=f(\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\beta)
-f(\alpha,\alpha)-f(\beta,\beta)\\
&=f(\alpha,\beta)+f(\beta,\alpha)\\
&=2f(\alpha,\beta).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
Tq_f=f.
$$
In particular $Tq$ is always a symmetric bilinear form.

The map $T$ is linear because the displayed polarization formula is linear in
$q$. Its inverse is
$$
S\longrightarrow Q,
\qquad
f\longmapsto q_f,
\qquad
q_f(\alpha)=f(\alpha,\alpha),
$$
because $Tq_f=f$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T:Q\overset\sim\longrightarrow S}
$$
is the required isomorphism.
:::

<1>5. Pullback by $U$ preserves quadratic forms.
::: {.proof}
Let $q=q_f$ with $f\in S$. Define
$$
h(\alpha,\beta)=f(U\alpha,U\beta).
$$
Because $U$ is linear and $f$ is symmetric bilinear, $h$ is symmetric
bilinear. Its associated quadratic form is
$$
q_h(\alpha)
=h(\alpha,\alpha)
=f(U\alpha,U\alpha)
=q(U\alpha)
=(U^\dagger q)(\alpha).
$$
Thus $U^\dagger q\in Q$.
:::

<1>6. The map $U^\dagger:Q\to Q$ is linear.
::: {.proof}
For $q_1,q_2\in Q$, scalars $a,b$, and $\alpha\in V$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
U^\dagger(aq_1+bq_2)(\alpha)
&=(aq_1+bq_2)(U\alpha)\\
&=a q_1(U\alpha)+b q_2(U\alpha)\\
&=(aU^\dagger q_1+bU^\dagger q_2)(\alpha).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence $U^\dagger$ is linear.
:::

<1>7. If $U$ is invertible, then $U^\dagger$ is invertible.
::: {.proof}
If $U$ is invertible, then for every $q\in Q$ and $\alpha\in V$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
((U^{-1})^\dagger U^\dagger q)(\alpha)
&=(U^\dagger q)(U^{-1}\alpha)\\
&=q(UU^{-1}\alpha)\\
&=q(\alpha).
\end{aligned}
$$
Similarly
$$
U^\dagger (U^{-1})^\dagger q=q.
$$
Thus
$$
(U^\dagger)^{-1}=(U^{-1})^\dagger.
$$
:::

<1>8. If $U$ is singular, then $U^\dagger$ is singular.
::: {.proof}
Suppose $U$ is not invertible. Since $V$ is finite-dimensional,
$\operatorname{im}U$ is a proper subspace. Choose a nonzero functional
$L\in V^*$ that vanishes on $\operatorname{im}U$. Then
$$
q(\alpha)=L(\alpha)^2
$$
is a nonzero quadratic form: it is associated with the symmetric bilinear
form
$$
f(\alpha,\beta)=L(\alpha)L(\beta).
$$
But for every $\alpha$,
$$
(U^\dagger q)(\alpha)
=q(U\alpha)
=L(U\alpha)^2
=0.
$$
Thus $U^\dagger q=0$ with $q\ne0$, so $U^\dagger$ is not injective and hence
not invertible.

Combining steps <1>7 and <1>8 gives
$$
\boxed{U^\dagger\text{ is invertible}\iff U\text{ is invertible}.}
$$
:::
