::: {.exercise}
Describe the range and the null space for the differentiation transformation of Example 2. Do the same for the integration transformation of Example 5.
:::

::: solution
For the differentiation map $D$ on the space $V$ of polynomial functions
$F\to F$,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{range}D=V,
\qquad
\ker D=\{\text{constant polynomial functions}\}.}
$$
For the integration map $T$ on $C(\mathbb R,\mathbb R)$,
$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{range}T
=\{g\in C^1(\mathbb R):g(0)=0\},
\qquad
\ker T=\{0\}.}
$$

<1>1. Determine the range and null space of differentiation.
::: proof
In Example 2, a polynomial function
$$
p(x)=c_0+c_1x+\cdots+c_nx^n
$$
is sent to its derivative. Given any polynomial function $p$ as above, define
$$
q(x)=c_0x+\frac{c_1}{2}x^2+\cdots+\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}.
$$
The setting in Hoffman--Kunze uses a field of characteristic zero here, so the
displayed denominators are nonzero scalars. Then $Dq=p$, proving
$\operatorname{range}D=V$ [@HK71].

Also $Dp=0$ if and only if every coefficient of positive degree is zero, so
$p$ is constant. Thus the null space is exactly the subspace of constant
polynomial functions.
:::

<1>2. Determine the range of the integration transformation.
::: proof
Example 5 takes
$$
V=C(\mathbb R,\mathbb R),
\qquad
(Tf)(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt
$$
[@HK71]. If $f\in V$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
$$
(Tf)'=f,
$$
so $Tf$ is continuously differentiable, and clearly $(Tf)(0)=0$. Hence
$$
\operatorname{range}T
\subseteq\{g\in C^1(\mathbb R):g(0)=0\}.
$$

Conversely, if $g\in C^1(\mathbb R)$ and $g(0)=0$, then $f=g'$ is continuous,
so $f\in V$, and the fundamental theorem gives
$$
(Tf)(x)=\int_0^x g'(t)\,dt=g(x)-g(0)=g(x).
$$
Thus every such $g$ lies in the range, proving equality.
:::

<1>3. Determine the null space of the integration transformation.
::: proof
If $Tf=0$, then the identically zero function $Tf$ has derivative zero.
But $(Tf)'=f$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus, so $f=0$ identically.
Conversely $T0=0$. Therefore $\ker T=\{0\}$.
:::
:::
