::: {.exercise}
Is there a linear transformation $T$ from $\RR^3$ into $\RR^2$ such that $T(1, -1, 1) = (1, 0)$ and $T(1, 1, 1) = (0, 1)$?
:::


::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise, and the section's textbook is not in the checkout (`SRC-TEXT-HK71`'s provenance is the archive.org record and the publisher's table of contents; `tmp/sources` holds no Hoffman--Kunze text). The argument is the card's own, and it rests on the criterion for prescribing a linear map on a set of vectors rather than only on the explicit construction.

Let $v_1=(1,-1,1)$ and $v_2=(1,1,1)$ in $\RR^3$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #criterion}
A linear $T:\RR^3\to\RR^2$ with prescribed values on $v_1,\dots,v_k$ exists if and only if every linear relation satisfied by the $v_i$ is also satisfied by the prescribed values.

::: pf-proof
If $T$ exists and $\sum c_iv_i=0$ then $0=T\bigl(\sum c_iv_i\bigr)=\sum c_iT(v_i)$ by linearity, so the relation carries over. Conversely, extend $v_1,\dots,v_k$ to a basis of $\RR^3$ — possible because $\RR^3$ is finite dimensional — prescribe arbitrary values on the added basis vectors, and extend by linearity; the resulting map is well defined precisely because every relation among the $v_i$ holds among their prescribed values, and any two such extensions differ only on the added vectors, which is why existence, not uniqueness, is the question asked.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #independence}
$v_1$ and $v_2$ are linearly independent, so they satisfy no nontrivial relation.

::: pf-proof
$v_2$ is not a scalar multiple of $v_1$, since in the first coordinate it would need scalar $1$ and in the second scalar $-1$. So the pair is independent, and the only relation $\sum c_iv_i=0$ has $c_1=c_2=0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #construction}
There is a linear $T:\RR^3\to\RR^2$ with $T(v_1)=(1,0)$ and $T(v_2)=(0,1)$; hence the answer is yes.

::: pf-proof
By step [](#independence){.pf-ref} the prescribed values $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ must satisfy no nontrivial relation, and they do not: they are independent in $\RR^2$. So the criterion of step [](#criterion){.pf-ref} is met. Explicitly, $v_1,v_2$ extend to a basis of $\RR^3$ by adding $v_3=(1,0,0)$, which is not in their span; the map
$$
T(x_1,x_2,x_3)=\Bigl(\tfrac12(x_1-x_2),\ \tfrac12(x_1+x_2)\Bigr)
$$
is linear and is the extension determined by $T(v_1)=(1,0)$, $T(v_2)=(0,1)$ and $T(v_3)=\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\bigr)$, as substitution gives $T(1,-1,1)=(1,0)$ and $T(1,1,1)=(0,1)$. So such a $T$ exists, and it does not depend on $x_3$.
:::
