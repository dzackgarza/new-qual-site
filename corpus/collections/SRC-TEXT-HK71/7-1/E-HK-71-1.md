::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on $F^2$.
Prove that any non-zero vector which is not a characteristic vector for $T$ is a cyclic vector for $T$.
Hence, prove that either $T$ has a cyclic vector or $T$ is a scalar multiple of the identity operator.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. Every nonzero non-characteristic vector is cyclic.
::: proof
Let $0\ne\alpha\in F^2$ and suppose $\alpha$ is not a characteristic vector
for $T$. Then $T\alpha$ is not a scalar multiple of $\alpha$, so
$$
\alpha,T\alpha
$$
are linearly independent. Since $F^2$ has dimension two, they form a basis.
Therefore the $T$-cyclic subspace generated by $\alpha$, which contains
$\alpha$ and $T\alpha$, is all of $F^2$. Hence $\alpha$ is cyclic [@HK71].
:::

<1>2. If there is no cyclic vector, then $T$ is scalar.
::: proof
Assume $T$ has no cyclic vector. By step <1>1, every nonzero vector is then a
characteristic vector. Choose a basis $u,v$. There are scalars $a,b,c$ with
$$
Tu=au,
\qquad
Tv=bv,
\qquad
T(u+v)=c(u+v).
$$
By linearity,
$$
au+bv=cu+cv.
$$
Independence of $u,v$ gives $a=b=c$. Thus $T$ has the same scalar action on a
basis and hence
$$
T=aI.
$$
Consequently either $T$ has a cyclic vector or it is a scalar multiple of the
identity.
:::
:::
