::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space over the field $F$, and let $N$ be a nilpotent linear operator on $V$.
Suppose $N^{n-1} \neq 0$, and let $\alpha$ be any vector in $V$ such that $N^{n-1}\alpha \neq 0$.
Prove that $\alpha$ is a cyclic vector for $N$.
What exactly is the matrix of $N$ in the ordered basis $\{\alpha, N\alpha, \ldots, N^{n-1}\alpha\}$?
:::

::: solution
Since $N$ is nilpotent on an $n$-dimensional space, its characteristic
polynomial is $x^n$, and Cayley--Hamilton gives
$$
N^n=0
$$
[@HK71].

<1>1. The vectors $\alpha,N\alpha,\ldots,N^{n-1}\alpha$ are linearly independent.
::: proof
Suppose
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_jN^j\alpha=0.
$$
If some coefficient is nonzero, let $r$ be the least index with $c_r\ne0$.
Apply $N^{n-1-r}$ to the relation. The $r$th term becomes
$$
c_rN^{n-1}\alpha,
$$
while every term with $j>r$ contains the power
$$
N^{n-1-r+j},
$$
whose exponent is at least $n$, and hence vanishes. Thus
$$
c_rN^{n-1}\alpha=0.
$$
The hypothesis $N^{n-1}\alpha\ne0$ forces $c_r=0$, a contradiction.
Therefore all coefficients vanish, so the $n$ displayed vectors are linearly
independent. Since $\dim V=n$, they form a basis. Hence $\alpha$ is cyclic.
:::

<1>2. In the stated ordered basis, $N$ is the nilpotent shift matrix.
::: proof
Let
$$
\mathfrak B=(\alpha,N\alpha,\ldots,N^{n-1}\alpha).
$$
For $0\le j<n-1$,
$$
N(N^j\alpha)=N^{j+1}\alpha,
$$
the next basis vector, while
$$
N(N^{n-1}\alpha)=N^n\alpha=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{
[N]_{\mathfrak B}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The ones lie on the subdiagonal because matrix columns are coordinate vectors
of the images of the ordered basis vectors.
:::
:::
