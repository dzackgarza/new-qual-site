::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be the linear operator on $\CC^3$ which is represented in the standard ordered basis by the matrix
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & i & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -i \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
Find the $T$-annihilator of the vector $(1, 0, 0)$.
Find the $T$-annihilator of $(1, 0, i)$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step
Definition of the $T$-annihilator:

::: pf-proof
The two subclaims record the definition and the degree formula that the computations below use.

::: pf-step
For a linear operator $T$ on a vector space $V$ and a vector $v \in V$, the $T$-annihilator of $v$ (denoted $p_v(x)$) is the unique monic polynomial of minimal degree in $F[x]$ such that $p_v(T)v = 0$.

::: pf-proof
The polynomials annihilating $v$ form an ideal of $F[x]$: it is closed under sums and under multiplication by arbitrary polynomials, and it is non-zero because the characteristic polynomial of $T$ lies in it by Cayley–Hamilton. The monic generator of that ideal is unique, and it is the polynomial of least degree in the ideal.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The degree of $p_v(x)$ equals the dimension of the $T$-cyclic subspace $Z(v; T) = \operatorname{span}\{v, Tv, T^2v, \dots\}$.

::: pf-proof
The map $F[x]\to Z(v;T)$ sending $f$ to $f(T)v$ is surjective by the definition of the cyclic subspace, and its kernel is exactly the ideal generated by $p_v$; hence $\dim Z(v;T)=\deg p_v$ by the first isomorphism theorem for vector spaces.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
$T$-annihilator of $v_1 = (1, 0, 0)^T$:

::: pf-proof
The subclaims show that $v_1, Tv_1, T^2v_1$ are independent, so the annihilator has degree $3$ and must be the characteristic polynomial.

::: pf-step
Compute the powers of $T$ acting on $v_1$:
$$v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Tv_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -i \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},$$
$$T^2 v_1 = T(Tv_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -i \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - i \\ -3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

::: pf-proof
Matrix multiplication: $Tv_1$ is the first column of $T$, and applying $T$ to the column $(1,-1,0)$ gives $1+i(-1)=1-i$, $-1+2(-1)=-3$ and $0+1(-1)=-1$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Place the vectors $v_1, Tv_1, T^2v_1$ as columns of a matrix and compute the determinant:
$$\det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1-i \\ 0 & -1 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot (-1) = 1 \neq 0.$$

::: pf-proof
The matrix is upper triangular, so its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Because the determinant is non-zero, the set $\{v_1, Tv_1, T^2v_1\}$ is linearly independent in $\CC^3$.

::: pf-proof
A square matrix over a field has linearly independent columns exactly when its determinant is non-zero.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Thus $\dim Z(v_1; T) = 3 = \dim \CC^3$, which implies $\deg p_{v_1}(x) = 3$.

::: pf-proof
The cyclic subspace is spanned by the three vectors, which are independent, so it has dimension $3$; the degree formula of the first container then gives $\deg p_{v_1}=3$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Consequently, $p_{v_1}(x)$ must equal the characteristic polynomial $\det(xI - T)$ of $T$:
$$\det(xI - T) = \det \begin{bmatrix} x - 1 & -i & 0 \\ 1 & x - 2 & i \\ 0 & -1 & x - 1 \end{bmatrix} = (x - 1)[(x - 2)(x - 1) + i] + i(x - 1) = (x - 1)(x^2 - 3x + 2 + 2i).$$

::: pf-proof
Cayley–Hamilton gives $\chi_T(T)=0$, so $\chi_T(T)v_1=0$ and $p_{v_1}$ divides $\chi_T$; both are monic of degree $3$, so they are equal. The determinant is expanded along the first row: the two surviving minors are $(x-2)(x-1)-i(-1)$ and $(x-1)$, the second entering with the sign $+i$ from the entry $-i$ in position $(1,2)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Expanding gives
$$p_{v_1}(x) = \boxed{x^3 - 4x^2 + (5 + 2i)x - (2 + 2i)}.$$

::: pf-proof
Multiply out $(x-1)(x^2-3x+2+2i)=x^3-3x^2+(2+2i)x-x^2+3x-(2+2i)$ and collect terms.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
$T$-annihilator of $v_2 = (1, 0, i)^T$:

::: pf-proof
The subclaims compute $Tv_2$ and read off the monic annihilator of degree one.

::: pf-step
Compute $Tv_2$:
$$Tv_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -i \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 0 + 0 \\ -1 + 0 - i^2 \\ 0 + 0 + i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ i \end{bmatrix} = v_2.$$

::: pf-proof
Matrix multiplication gives $1+i\cdot0+0\cdot i=1$, $-1+2\cdot0+(-i)\cdot i=-1-i^2=0$ and $0+1\cdot0+1\cdot i=i$, using $i^2=-1$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Thus $(T - I)v_2 = 0$.

::: pf-proof
The computation shows $Tv_2=v_2$, which is the same statement.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since $v_2 \neq 0$, the degree of the $T$-annihilator is at least 1, so the monic polynomial of degree 1 annihilating $v_2$ is
$$p_{v_2}(x) = \boxed{x - 1}.$$

::: pf-proof
The only monic polynomial of degree $0$ is $1$, and $1\cdot v_2=v_2\neq0$, so the annihilator has degree at least $1$; the monic polynomial $x-1$ satisfies $(T-I)v_2=0$ and has degree $1$, hence is the annihilator.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
Conclusion:

::: pf-proof
The $T$-annihilator of $(1, 0, 0)$ is $x^3 - 4x^2 + (5 + 2i)x - (2 + 2i)$, and the $T$-annihilator of $(1, 0, i)$ is $x - 1$.
:::
