::: {.exercise}
Consider $\RR^4$ with the standard inner product.
Let $W$ be the subspace of $\RR^4$ consisting of all vectors which are orthogonal to both $\alpha = (1, 0, -1, 1)$ and $\beta = (2, 3, -1, 2)$.
Find a basis for $W$.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise; the argument is the card's own, checked against Hoffman--Kunze, *Linear Algebra* (1971), §8.2 Exercise 1. The two basis vectors were recomputed as the null space of the $2\times4$ matrix with rows $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and checked against both orthogonality conditions.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #conditions}
$x=(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)\in W$ if and only if $x_1-x_3+x_4=0$ and $2x_1+3x_2-x_3+2x_4=0$.

::: pf-proof
$W$ is the set of vectors orthogonal to $\alpha$ and to $\beta$, and orthogonality to $\alpha=(1,0,-1,1)$ is $x_1-x_3+x_4=0$, to $\beta=(2,3,-1,2)$ is $2x_1+3x_2-x_3+2x_4=0$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #solve}
The solutions are $x=s(-3,1,-3,0)+t(-1,0,0,1)$ for $s,t\in\RR$.

::: pf-proof
Subtracting twice the first equation from the second gives $3x_2+x_3=0$, so $x_3=-3x_2$; substituting in the first gives $x_1=-3x_2-x_4$. Writing $s=x_2$ and $t=x_4$ gives the displayed form, and conversely any such $x$ satisfies both equations.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #orthogonal}
The vectors $u=(-3,1,-3,0)$ and $v=(-1,0,0,1)$ are orthogonal to $\alpha$ and to $\beta$.

::: pf-proof
$\alpha\cdot u=-3+0+3+0=0$ and $\beta\cdot u=-6+3+3+0=0$; $\alpha\cdot v=-1+0+0+1=0$ and $\beta\cdot v=-2+0+0+2=0$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #independent}
$u$ and $v$ are linearly independent.

::: pf-proof
If $au+bv=0$ then the second coordinate gives $a=0$ and the fourth gives $b=0$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #basis}
$(u,v)$ is a basis of $W$.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#solve){.pf-ref} shows every element of $W$ is a combination of $u$ and $v$, so they span $W$, and step [](#independent){.pf-ref} shows they are independent; step [](#orthogonal){.pf-ref} confirms they lie in $W$. Hence they are a basis of $W$, whose dimension is $2$, the rank of the pair $(\alpha,\beta)$ being $2$.
:::
:::
