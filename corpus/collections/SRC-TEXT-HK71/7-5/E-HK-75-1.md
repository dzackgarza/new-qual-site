::: {.exercise}
If $N$ is a nilpotent linear operator on $V$, show that for any polynomial $f$ the semi-simple part of $f(N)$ is a scalar multiple of the identity operator ($F$ a subfield of $\CC$).
:::


::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise; the argument is the card's own, checked against Hoffman--Kunze, *Linear Algebra* (1971), §7.5 Exercise 1. The two steps of the decomposition are the polynomial division $f=f(0)+xq$ and the nilpotence of $Nq(N)$; the identification of the semi-simple part uses the uniqueness of the decomposition into commuting semi-simple and nilpotent parts.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #division}
There is a polynomial $q$ with $f(x)=f(0)+xq(x)$.

::: pf-proof
Divide $f$ by $x$: the remainder is $f(0)$, so $f(x)-f(0)$ is divisible by $x$, with quotient $q$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #decompose}
$f(N)=f(0)I+Nq(N)$.

::: pf-proof
Substitute $N$ in the identity of step [](#division){.pf-ref} and use that $q(N)$ is the operator obtained by evaluating $q$ at $N$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #semisimple}
The scalar operator $f(0)I$ is semi-simple.

::: pf-proof
A scalar multiple of the identity has every subspace invariant, hence is diagonalisable in any basis, which is semi-simplicity.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #nilpotent}
$Nq(N)$ is nilpotent.

::: pf-proof
$N$ commutes with $q(N)$ because $q(N)$ is a polynomial in $N$, so $(Nq(N))^r=N^rq(N)^r$; choosing $r$ with $N^r=0$ gives $(Nq(N))^r=0$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #commute}
$f(0)I$ and $Nq(N)$ commute.

::: pf-proof
$Nq(N)=q(N)N$ commutes with every polynomial in $N$, in particular with the scalar $f(0)I$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #unique}
The semi-simple part of $f(N)$ is $f(0)I$.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#decompose){.pf-ref} writes $f(N)$ as the sum of the semi-simple operator of step [](#semisimple){.pf-ref} and the nilpotent operator of step [](#nilpotent){.pf-ref}, and step [](#commute){.pf-ref} shows they commute. The decomposition of an operator into commuting semi-simple and nilpotent parts is unique (Hoffman--Kunze, §7.5), so this is the decomposition of $f(N)$ and its semi-simple part is $f(0)I$.
:::
:::
