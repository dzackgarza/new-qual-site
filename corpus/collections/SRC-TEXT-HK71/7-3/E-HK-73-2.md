::: {.exercise}
Use the result of Exercise 1 and the Jordan form to prove the following.
Let $A$ and $B$ be $n \times n$ matrices over the field $F$ which have the same characteristic polynomial
$$
f = (x - c_1)^{d_1} \cdots (x - c_k)^{d_k}
$$
and the same minimal polynomial.
If no $d_i$ is greater than 3, then $A$ and $B$ are similar.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise; the argument is the card's own. It is carried out in Jordan form, so the eigenvalues $c_{1},\dots,c_{k}$ are taken in the field $F$, as the displayed factorization of $f$ asserts. For an eigenvalue $c$ let $d$ be the exponent of $(x-c)$ in the common characteristic polynomial, so that $d$ is the total dimension of the $c$-primary part, and let $e$ be the exponent of $(x-c)$ in the common minimal polynomial, so that $e$ is the size of the largest Jordan block for $c$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #d-e}
The Jordan form of $A$, and that of $B$, has for each $c_{i}$ a partition of $d_{i}$ into block sizes whose largest part is $e_{i}$.

::: pf-proof
In Jordan form the $c_{i}$-primary part is a direct sum of blocks $J_{m}(c_{i})$ with $m$ the block sizes, so the sizes form a partition of the dimension $d_{i}$ of that part. The minimal polynomial of a single block $J_{m}(c)$ is $(x-c)^{m}$, and the minimal polynomial of a direct sum is the least common multiple of those of its summands; for the $c_{i}$-primary part this is $(x-c_{i})^{e_{i}}$, where $e_{i}$ is the largest block size. The same two computations apply to $B$, whose characteristic and minimal polynomials are the same by hypothesis.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #table}
For $d\le3$ the pair $(d,e)$ determines the partition of $d$: the possibilities are
$$
(d,e)=(1,1)\mapsto1,\quad (2,1)\mapsto1+1,\quad (2,2)\mapsto2,\quad (3,1)\mapsto1+1+1,\quad (3,2)\mapsto2+1,\quad (3,3)\mapsto3 .
$$

::: pf-proof
List the partitions of each $d\le3$ and record the largest part. For $d=1$ the only partition is $1$, with largest part $1$. For $d=2$ they are $1+1$ and $2$, with largest parts $1$ and $2$. For $d=3$ they are $1+1+1$, $2+1$ and $3$, with largest parts $1,2,3$. In each case a different largest part belongs to a different partition, so the six pairs listed are distinct and exhaustive; in particular no two partitions of the same $d\le3$ share a largest part.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
If no $d_{i}$ exceeds $3$, then $A$ and $B$ are similar.

::: pf-proof
By hypothesis $A$ and $B$ have the same characteristic polynomial, so they share each $d_{i}$, and the same minimal polynomial, so they share each $e_{i}$. Fix $i$. If $d_{i}\le3$ then by step [](#table){.pf-ref} the pair $(d_{i},e_{i})$ determines the partition of $d_{i}$, hence determines the multiset of Jordan block sizes for the eigenvalue $c_{i}$; and by step [](#d-e){.pf-ref} those block sizes are the Jordan blocks of both $A$ and $B$ at $c_{i}$. Doing this for every eigenvalue gives the same multiset of Jordan blocks for $A$ and for $B$, so their Jordan forms differ at most by the order of the blocks; two matrices with the same Jordan form up to block order are similar, since both are similar to that form. Hence $A\sim B$, as claimed. The argument is the classification of nilpotent operators of dimension at most three, applied to $A-c_{i}I$ and $B-c_{i}I$ on their primary parts.
:::
