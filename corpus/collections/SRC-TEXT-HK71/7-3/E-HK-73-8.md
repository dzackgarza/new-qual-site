::: {.exercise}
Classify up to similarity all $3 \times 3$ complex matrices $A$ such that $A^3 = I$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The argument is the card's own; no retained solution set is cited on the card, and no printed copy of Hoffman and Kunze is retained in `tmp/sources`, so the exercise is cited by section and number rather than by page. It is checked against Hoffman and Kunze, Linear Algebra (1971), §7.3 Exercise 8 [@HK71].

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #roots}
$x^{3}-1=(x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^{2})$ has three distinct roots, where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$.

::: pf-proof
$\omega^{3}=1$ and $\omega\ne1$, and the three displayed numbers are distinct roots of $x^{3}-1$; a cubic has at most three roots, so these are all of them and each is simple.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #divisor}
The minimal polynomial of $A$ divides $x^{3}-1$, hence has distinct linear factors.

::: pf-proof
$A^{3}=I$ means that $x^{3}-1$ annihilates $A$, and the minimal polynomial divides every annihilating polynomial. Its roots are among the roots of $x^{3}-1$, which are simple, so it is a product of distinct linear factors.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #diagonalisable}
$A$ is diagonalisable, with eigenvalues among $1,\omega,\omega^{2}$.

::: pf-proof
A matrix whose minimal polynomial has distinct linear factors is diagonalisable (equivalently, it has no repeated root in its minimal polynomial, so every Jordan block is $1\times1$). The eigenvalues are roots of the minimal polynomial, hence among the three roots of $x^{3}-1$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #canonical}
$A$ is similar to $\operatorname{diag}(I_{a},\omega I_{b},\omega^{2}I_{c})$ for unique $a,b,c\ge0$ with $a+b+c=3$.

::: pf-proof
Similarity preserves the multiset of eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, so a diagonalisable matrix is determined up to similarity by the multiplicities $a,b,c$ of its eigenvalues $1,\omega,\omega^{2}$; these are nonnegative integers summing to the size $3$. The diagonal matrix with those multiplicities is similar to $A$ by step [](#diagonalisable){.pf-ref}, and two such diagonal matrices are similar exactly when the triples agree, since similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial $(x-1)^{a}(x-\omega)^{b}(x-\omega^{2})^{c}$ and distinct roots make the exponents readable from it.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #count}
There are $10$ similarity classes.

::: pf-proof
The classes correspond to the triples $(a,b,c)$ of nonnegative integers with $a+b+c=3$, and by stars and bars there are $\binom{3+3-1}{3-1}=\binom{5}{2}=10$ of them.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
The similarity classes of $3\times3$ complex matrices with $A^{3}=I$ are exactly the ten $\operatorname{diag}(I_{a},\omega I_{b},\omega^{2}I_{c})$.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#canonical){.pf-ref} and [](#count){.pf-ref} give completeness, irredundancy and the count:
$$
\boxed{A^{3}=I\iff A\sim\operatorname{diag}(I_{a},\omega I_{b},\omega^{2}I_{c}),\quad a+b+c=3;\quad\text{ten classes}.}
$$
The converse is immediate: any such diagonal matrix satisfies $D^{3}=I$, and so does anything similar to it.
:::
