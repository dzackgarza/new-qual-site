::: {.exercise}
If $N$ is a nilpotent $3 \times 3$ matrix over $C$, prove that $A = I + \frac{1}{2}N - \frac{1}{8}N^2$ satisfies $A^2 = I + N$, i.e., $A$ is a square root of $I + N$.
Use the binomial series for $(1 + t)^{1/2}$ to obtain a similar formula for a square root of $I + N$, where $N$ is any nilpotent $n \times n$ matrix over $C$.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. Verify the displayed formula in size three.
::: proof
For a nilpotent $3\times3$ matrix, $N^3=0$ [@HK71]. Put
$$
A=I+\frac12N-\frac18N^2.
$$
Because all powers of $N$ commute,
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^2
&=I+N+\left(\frac14-\frac14\right)N^2
-\frac18N^3+\frac1{64}N^4\\
&=I+N.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the stated $A$ is a square root of $I+N$.
:::

<1>2. Truncate the binomial series at the nilpotency index.
::: proof
Suppose generally that $N^r=0$. The formal binomial series satisfies
$$
(1+t)^{1/2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{1/2}{j}t^j
$$
and its square is $1+t$. Define the finite polynomial
$$
q(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{1/2}{j}t^j.
$$
The power-series identity implies
$$
q(t)^2-(1+t)
$$
has no terms of degree below $r$, so it is divisible by $t^r$ in
$\mathbb C[t]$ modulo terms irrelevant after substitution. Equivalently,
$$
q(t)^2\equiv1+t\pmod{t^r}.
$$
Substituting $t=N$ and using $N^r=0$ gives
$$
q(N)^2=I+N.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{
(I+N)^{1/2}
=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{1/2}{j}N^j}
$$
is a polynomial square root of $I+N$.
:::
:::
