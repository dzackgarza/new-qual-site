::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space.
For each $\alpha, \beta$ in $V$, let $T_{\alpha, \beta}$ be the linear operator on $V$ defined by $T_{\alpha, \beta}(\gamma) = (\gamma \mid \beta)\alpha$.
Show that

(a) $T_{\alpha, \beta}^* = T_{\beta, \alpha}$.

(b) $\operatorname{trace}(T_{\alpha, \beta}) = (\alpha \mid \beta)$.

(c) $T_{\alpha,\beta}T_{\gamma,\delta} = T_{\alpha,(\beta \mid \gamma)\delta}$.

(d) Under what conditions is $T_{\alpha, \beta}$ self-adjoint?
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The adjoint is $T_{\beta,\alpha}$.
::: {.proof}
For arbitrary $\gamma,\eta\in V$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
(T_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma\mid\eta)
&=((\gamma\mid\beta)\alpha\mid\eta)\\
&=(\gamma\mid\beta)(\alpha\mid\eta),
\end{aligned}
$$
while
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\gamma\mid T_{\beta,\alpha}\eta)
&=(\gamma\mid(\eta\mid\alpha)\beta)\\
&=\overline{(\eta\mid\alpha)}(\gamma\mid\beta)\\
&=(\alpha\mid\eta)(\gamma\mid\beta).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the two inner products agree for every $\gamma,\eta$, so
$$
\boxed{T_{\alpha,\beta}^*=T_{\beta,\alpha}}.
$$
:::

<1>2. The trace is $(\alpha\mid\beta)$.
::: {.proof}
Choose any orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V$. The diagonal entry of
$T_{\alpha,\beta}$ in position $i$ is
$$
(T_{\alpha,\beta}e_i\mid e_i)
=(e_i\mid\beta)(\alpha\mid e_i).
$$
Hence
$$
\operatorname{tr}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
=\sum_{i=1}^n(e_i\mid\beta)(\alpha\mid e_i).
$$
Since
$$
\beta=\sum_i(\beta\mid e_i)e_i,
$$
conjugate symmetry gives $(e_i\mid\beta)=\overline{(\beta\mid e_i)}$, and
therefore
$$
(\alpha\mid\beta)
=\sum_i(\alpha\mid e_i)(e_i\mid\beta).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(T_{\alpha,\beta})=(\alpha\mid\beta)}.
$$
:::

<1>3. The product formula is
$$
T_{\alpha,\beta}T_{\gamma,\delta}
=T_{\alpha,(\beta\mid\gamma)\delta}.
$$
::: {.proof}
For every $\eta\in V$,
$$
T_{\alpha,\beta}T_{\gamma,\delta}(\eta)
=(\eta\mid\delta)(\gamma\mid\beta)\alpha.
$$
On the other hand,
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{\alpha,(\beta\mid\gamma)\delta}(\eta)
&=(\eta\mid(\beta\mid\gamma)\delta)\alpha\\
&=\overline{(\beta\mid\gamma)}(\eta\mid\delta)\alpha\\
&=(\gamma\mid\beta)(\eta\mid\delta)\alpha.
\end{aligned}
$$
The two operators therefore agree.
:::

<1>4. Characterize when $T_{\alpha,\beta}$ is self-adjoint.
::: {.proof}
By step <1>1, self-adjointness is equivalent to
$$
T_{\alpha,\beta}=T_{\beta,\alpha}.
$$
If either $\alpha=0$ or $\beta=0$, then $T_{\alpha,\beta}=0$, so the operator
is self-adjoint.

Assume now that $\alpha,\beta\ne0$ and that the two rank-one operators are
equal. Their ranges are respectively $\operatorname{span}\{\alpha\}$ and
$\operatorname{span}\{\beta\}$, hence these lines coincide. Write
$$
\alpha=c\beta
$$
with $c\ne0$. Then
$$
T_{\alpha,\beta}=cT_{\beta,\beta},
$$
whereas, because the second slot is conjugate-linear,
$$
T_{\beta,\alpha}
=T_{\beta,c\beta}
=\overline c\,T_{\beta,\beta}.
$$
Since $T_{\beta,\beta}\ne0$, equality holds if and only if
$c=\overline c$, i.e. $c\in\mathbb R$.

Conversely, if $\alpha=c\beta$ with $c\in\mathbb R$, the two operators are
equal. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{$T_{\alpha,\beta}$ is self-adjoint iff one vector is zero, or
$\alpha=c\beta$ for some $c\in\mathbb R$.}}
$$
:::
