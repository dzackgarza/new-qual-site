::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be the set of complex numbers, regarded as a real vector space.

(a) Show that $(\alpha \mid \beta) = \operatorname{Re}(\alpha \bar{\beta})$ defines an inner product on $V$.

(b) Exhibit an (inner product space) isomorphism of $V$ onto $\RR^2$ with the standard inner product.

(c) For each $\gamma$ in $V$, let $M_\gamma$ be the linear operator on $V$ defined by $M_\gamma(\alpha) = \gamma\alpha$.
Show that $(M_\gamma)^* = M_{\overline{\gamma}}$.

(d) For which complex numbers $\gamma$ is $M_\gamma$ self-adjoint?

(e) For which $\gamma$ is $M_\gamma$ unitary?

(f) For which $\gamma$ is $M_\gamma$ positive?

(g) What is $\det(M_\gamma)$?

(h) Find the matrix of $M_\gamma$ in the basis $\{1, i\}$.

(i) If $T$ is a linear operator on $V$, find necessary and sufficient conditions for $T$ to be an $M_\gamma$.

(j) Find a unitary operator on $V$ which is not an $M_\gamma$.
:::

::: solution
Write $\gamma=a+ib$ with $a,b\in\mathbb R$.

<1>1. The formula $(\alpha\mid\beta)=\operatorname{Re}(\alpha\overline\beta)$ is an inner product on the real vector space $V$.
::: proof
Real bilinearity follows from linearity of the real part in real scalars, and
symmetry follows from
$$
\operatorname{Re}(\alpha\overline\beta)
=\operatorname{Re}(\overline{\alpha\overline\beta})
=\operatorname{Re}(\beta\overline\alpha).
$$
Moreover
$$
(\alpha\mid\alpha)=\operatorname{Re}(|\alpha|^2)=|\alpha|^2\ge0,
$$
with equality exactly when $\alpha=0$. Thus all inner-product axioms hold.
:::

<1>2. The map $\Phi:V\to\mathbb R^2$ given by $\Phi(x+iy)=(x,y)$ is an inner-product-space isomorphism.
::: proof
It is clearly a real-linear bijection. If
$\alpha=x+iy$ and $\beta=u+iv$, then
$$
(\alpha\mid\beta)
=\operatorname{Re}((x+iy)(u-iv))
=xu+yv,
$$
which is exactly the standard dot product of $(x,y)$ and $(u,v)$.
:::

<1>3. The adjoint of $M_\gamma$ is $M_{\overline\gamma}$.
::: proof
For $\alpha,\beta\in V$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
(M_\gamma\alpha\mid\beta)
&=\operatorname{Re}(\gamma\alpha\overline\beta)\\
&=\operatorname{Re}(\alpha\overline{\overline\gamma\beta})\\
&=(\alpha\mid M_{\overline\gamma}\beta).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{M_\gamma^*=M_{\overline\gamma}}.
$$
:::

<1>4. $M_\gamma$ is self-adjoint exactly when $\gamma$ is real.
::: proof
By step <1>3,
$$
M_\gamma=M_\gamma^*
\iff M_\gamma=M_{\overline\gamma}.
$$
Evaluating both operators at $1$ gives
$\gamma=\overline\gamma$, which is equivalent to $\gamma\in\mathbb R$.
The converse is immediate.
:::

<1>5. $M_\gamma$ is unitary exactly when $|\gamma|=1$.
::: proof
Using step <1>3,
$$
M_\gamma^*M_\gamma
=M_{\overline\gamma}M_\gamma
=M_{|\gamma|^2}.
$$
This is the identity operator exactly when $|\gamma|^2=1$.
Therefore
$$
\boxed{M_\gamma\text{ unitary}\iff |\gamma|=1}.
$$
:::

<1>6. $M_\gamma$ is positive exactly when $\gamma$ is a positive real number.
::: proof
A positive operator is self-adjoint, so step <1>4 first forces
$\gamma\in\mathbb R$. For nonzero $\alpha$,
$$
(M_\gamma\alpha\mid\alpha)
=\operatorname{Re}(\gamma|\alpha|^2)
=\gamma|\alpha|^2.
$$
This is strictly positive for every $\alpha\ne0$ exactly when $\gamma>0$.
Thus
$$
\boxed{M_\gamma\text{ positive}\iff \gamma\in\mathbb R_{>0}}.
$$
:::

<1>7. The determinant of $M_\gamma$ is $|\gamma|^2$.
::: proof
In the real basis $(1,i)$,
$$
M_\gamma(1)=a+bi,
\qquad
M_\gamma(i)=-b+ai,
$$
so
$$
[M_\gamma]_{(1,i)}
=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\det(M_\gamma)=a^2+b^2=|\gamma|^2}.
$$
This also answers part (h): the displayed matrix is the requested matrix.
:::

<1>8. Characterize which real-linear operators are multiplication operators.
::: proof
Let
$$
J=M_i,
$$
so $J(\alpha)=i\alpha$. Every multiplication operator commutes with $J$:
$$
M_\gamma J=JM_\gamma.
$$
Conversely, suppose a real-linear operator $T$ commutes with $J$. Put
$$
\gamma=T(1).
$$
Every $\alpha=x+iy$ satisfies
$$
\alpha=x\cdot1+y\cdot i,
$$
and hence, by real linearity and commutation with $J$,
$$
T(\alpha)
=xT(1)+yT(i)
=x\gamma+yJ\gamma
=x\gamma+yi\gamma
=\gamma\alpha.
$$
Thus $T=M_\gamma$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T=M_\gamma\text{ for some }\gamma\iff TJ=JT.}
$$
Equivalently, in the basis $(1,i)$ the matrix of $T$ has the form
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}.
$$
:::

<1>9. Complex conjugation is a unitary real-linear operator that is not any $M_\gamma$.
::: proof
Define
$$
C(z)=\overline z.
$$
It is real-linear, and
$$
(C\alpha\mid C\beta)
=\operatorname{Re}(\overline\alpha\,\beta)
=\operatorname{Re}(\alpha\overline\beta)
=(\alpha\mid\beta),
$$
so $C$ is unitary. But
$$
CJ(1)=C(i)=-i,
\qquad
JC(1)=i,
$$
so $CJ\ne JC$. By step <1>8, $C$ cannot equal $M_\gamma$ for any
$\gamma\in\mathbb C$.
:::
:::
