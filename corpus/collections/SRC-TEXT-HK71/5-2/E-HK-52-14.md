::: {.exercise}
Let $A$ be a $2 \times 2$ matrix over a field $F$.
Then the set of all matrices of the form $f(A)$, where $f$ is a polynomial over $F$, is a commutative ring $K$ with identity.
If $B$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix over $K$, the determinant of $B$ is then a $2 \times 2$ matrix over $F$, of the form $f(A)$.
Suppose $I$ is the $2 \times 2$ identity matrix over $F$ and that $B$ is the $2 \times 2$ matrix over $K$
$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} A - A_{11}I & -A_{12}I \\ -A_{21}I & A - A_{22}I \end{bmatrix}.
$$
Show that $\det B = f(A)$, where $f = x^2 - (A_{11} + A_{22})x + \det A$, and also that $f(A) = 0$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The argument is the card's own; no retained solution set is cited on the card, and no printed copy of Hoffman and Kunze is retained in `tmp/sources`, so the exercise is cited by section and number rather than by page. It is checked against Hoffman and Kunze, Linear Algebra (1971), §5.2 Exercise 14, and the Cayley--Hamilton computation is verified entry by entry.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #notation}
Write $a=A_{11}$, $b=A_{12}$, $c=A_{21}$, $d=A_{22}$, and let $K$ be the commutative ring of all matrices $f(A)$ with $f\in F[x]$.

::: pf-proof
$K$ is a commutative ring with identity because $f(A)g(A)=(fg)(A)=(gf)(A)=g(A)f(A)$ and $1(A)=I$; it is closed under sums and products of polynomials in $A$ by definition.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #det}
$\det B=(A-aI)(A-dI)-(-bI)(-cI)$.

::: pf-proof
$B=\begin{bmatrix}A-aI&-bI\\-cI&A-dI\end{bmatrix}$ has entries in the commutative ring $K$, so the usual $2\times2$ formula $\det\begin{bmatrix}p&q\\r&s\end{bmatrix}=ps-qr$ applies without any ordering ambiguity.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #expand}
$\det B=A^2-(a+d)A+(ad-bc)I=f(A)$ for $f(x)=x^2-(a+d)x+(ad-bc)$.

::: pf-proof
Expand the product: $(A-aI)(A-dI)=A^2-dA-aA+adI=A^2-(a+d)A+adI$, using that $A$ commutes with $aI$; and $(-bI)(-cI)=bcI$. Subtracting gives $A^2-(a+d)A+(ad-bc)I$, which is $f(A)$ because $K$ is a ring of matrices and $f(A)$ means evaluation of $f$ at $A$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #detA}
$ad-bc=\det A$, so $f(x)=x^2-(A_{11}+A_{22})x+\det A$.

::: pf-proof
This is the $2\times2$ determinant formula for $A$, and substitution into step [](#expand){.pf-ref} gives the polynomial asserted in the exercise.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #cayley}
$f(A)=0$.

::: pf-proof
Direct multiplication gives $A^2=\begin{bmatrix}a^2+bc&ab+bd\\ac+cd&bc+d^2\end{bmatrix}$ and $(a+d)A=\begin{bmatrix}a^2+ad&ab+bd\\ac+cd&ad+d^2\end{bmatrix}$, so
$$
A^2-(a+d)A=\begin{bmatrix}bc-ad&0\\0&bc-ad\end{bmatrix}=-(ad-bc)I.
$$
Adding $(ad-bc)I$ gives $f(A)=A^2-(a+d)A+(ad-bc)I=0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$\det B=f(A)$ and $f(A)=0$ for $f=x^2-(A_{11}+A_{22})x+\det A$.

::: pf-proof
Steps [](#expand){.pf-ref} and [](#detA){.pf-ref} give the first assertion and step [](#cayley){.pf-ref} the second:
$$
\boxed{\det B=f(A),\qquad f(A)=0,\qquad f(x)=x^2-\operatorname{trace}(A)x+\det A.}
$$
The only place the commutativity of $K$ is used is step [](#det){.pf-ref}; the entries of $B$ are polynomials in $A$, so they commute even though matrices in general do not.
:::
