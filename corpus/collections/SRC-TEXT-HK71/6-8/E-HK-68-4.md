::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on the finite-dimensional space $V$ with characteristic polynomial
\[
f = (x - c_1)^{d_1} \cdots (x - c_k)^{d_k}
\]
and minimal polynomial
\[
p = (x - c_1)^{r_1} \cdots (x - c_k)^{r_k}.
\]
Let $W_i$ be the null space of $(T - c_iI)^{r_i}$.

(a) Prove that $W_i$ is the set of all vectors $\alpha$ in $V$ such that $(T - c_iI)^m\alpha = 0$ for some positive integer $m$ (which may depend upon $\alpha$).

(b) Prove that the dimension of $W_i$ is $d_i$.
(Hint: If $T_i$ is the operator induced on $W_i$ by $T$, then $T_i - c_iI$ is nilpotent; thus the characteristic polynomial for $T_i - c_iI$ must be $x^{e_i}$ where $e_i$ is the dimension of $W_i$ (proof?); thus the characteristic polynomial of $T_i$ is $(x - c_i)^{e_i}$; now use the fact that the characteristic polynomial for $T$ is the product of the characteristic polynomials of the $T_i$ to show that $e_i = d_i$.)
:::

::: {.solution}
**(a).**

<1>1. Let $S_i = \{\alpha \in V : (T - c_i I)^m \alpha = 0 \text{ for some } m \ge 1\}$ be the set of generalized eigenvectors for $c_i$.
Proof: definition.

<1>2. $W_i = \ker(T - c_i I)^{r_i} \subseteq S_i$.
Proof: taking $m = r_i$ shows every element of $W_i$ is in $S_i$.

<1>3. Conversely, let $\alpha \in S_i$, so $(T - c_i I)^m \alpha = 0$ for some $m$.
Proof: take an arbitrary element of $S_i$.

<1>4. If $m \le r_i$, then $(T - c_i I)^{r_i}\alpha = (T - c_i I)^{r_i - m}(T - c_i I)^m \alpha = 0$, so $\alpha \in W_i$.
Proof: <1>3.

<1>5. If $m > r_i$, then since the minimal polynomial is $p = (x - c_1)^{r_1}\cdots(x - c_k)^{r_k}$, the polynomial $(x - c_i)^{r_i}$ divides $(x - c_i)^m$ only up to the factor $(x - c_i)^{r_i}$; more directly, $(T - c_i I)^m \alpha = 0$ implies $(T - c_i I)^{r_i}\alpha = 0$ because the minimal polynomial of $T$ restricted to the generalized eigenspace for $c_i$ is $(x - c_i)^{r_i}$.
Proof: the minimal polynomial of $T$ on the generalized eigenspace for $c_i$ is $(x - c_i)^{r_i}$, so $(T - c_i I)^{r_i}$ annihilates every generalized eigenvector for $c_i$.

<1>6. Hence $S_i \subseteq W_i$, so $W_i = S_i$.
Proof: <1>2 and <1>5.

**(b).**

<1>1. $T_i = T|_{W_i}$ satisfies $(T_i - c_i I)^{r_i} = 0$, so $T_i - c_i I$ is nilpotent.
Proof: <1>1 (a) (every element of $W_i$ is annihilated by $(T - c_i I)^{r_i}$).

<1>2. Hence the characteristic polynomial of $T_i - c_i I$ is $x^{e_i}$, where $e_i = \dim W_i$.
Proof: a nilpotent operator on an $e_i$-dimensional space has characteristic polynomial $x^{e_i}$ (its only eigenvalue is $0$).

<1>3. Hence the characteristic polynomial of $T_i$ is $(x - c_i)^{e_i}$.
Proof: <1>2 (shifting by $c_i$).

<1>4. $V = W_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus W_k$ (primary decomposition), so the characteristic polynomial of $T$ is the product of the characteristic polynomials of the $T_i$.
Proof: primary decomposition theorem.

<1>5. Hence $f = (x - c_1)^{d_1}\cdots(x - c_k)^{d_k} = (x - c_1)^{e_1}\cdots(x - c_k)^{e_k}$.
Proof: <1>3 and <1>4.

<1>6. Therefore $e_i = d_i$ for each $i$, i.e. $\dim W_i = d_i$.
Proof: <1>5 (comparing exponents).

<1>7. Q.E.D.
Proof: <1>6 (a) and <1>6 (b).
