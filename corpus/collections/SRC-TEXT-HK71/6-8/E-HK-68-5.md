::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over the field of complex numbers.
Let $T$ be a linear operator on $V$ and let $D$ be the diagonalizable part of $T$.
Prove that if $g$ is any polynomial with complex coefficients, then the diagonalizable part of $g(T)$ is $g(D)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Write the Jordan decomposition of $T$ as
$$
T=D+N,
$$
where $D$ is diagonalizable, $N$ is nilpotent, and $DN=ND$ [@HK71].

<1>1. The operator $g(D)$ is diagonalizable.
::: {.proof}
Choose a basis in which
$$
D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).
$$
Then in the same basis
$$
g(D)=\operatorname{diag}(g(\lambda_1),\ldots,g(\lambda_n)),
$$
so $g(D)$ is diagonalizable.
:::

<1>2. The difference $g(T)-g(D)$ is nilpotent and commutes with $g(D)$.
::: {.proof}
Let
$$
g(x)=\sum_{r=0}^m a_rx^r.
$$
Since $D$ and $N$ commute, the binomial theorem gives
$$
(D+N)^r-D^r
=\sum_{j=1}^r\binom rjD^{r-j}N^j
=N\sum_{j=1}^r\binom rjD^{r-j}N^{j-1}.
$$
Hence
$$
M:=g(T)-g(D)=NQ(D,N)
$$
for a polynomial $Q$ in the commuting operators $D,N$. Thus $M$ commutes with
$D$, $N$, and in particular with $g(D)$.

If $N^s=0$, then
$$
M^s=N^sQ(D,N)^s=0,
$$
because all factors commute. Therefore $M$ is nilpotent.
:::

<1>3. Uniqueness identifies the diagonalizable part.
::: {.proof}
We have written
$$
g(T)=g(D)+M
$$
as a sum of a diagonalizable operator and a commuting nilpotent operator.
The uniqueness part of the diagonalizable--nilpotent decomposition theorem
therefore implies that the diagonalizable part of $g(T)$ is exactly
$$
\boxed{g(D)}.
$$
:::
:::
