::: {.exercise}
If you thought about Exercise 11, think about it again, after you observe what Theorem 7 tells you about the diagonalizable and nilpotent parts of $T$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Exercise 11 decomposes an arbitrary upper-triangular matrix $A$ as its literal
diagonal part plus its strictly upper-triangular part. The problem is that these
two matrices need not commute. The actual diagonalizable and nilpotent parts
of $T$ do commute, and that extra condition is what makes the decomposition
canonical.

Let
$$
T=D+N,
\qquad DN=ND,
$$
be the diagonalizable--nilpotent decomposition, with $D$ diagonalizable and
$N$ nilpotent [@HK71].

<1>1. The commuting operators $D$ and $N$ can be triangularized simultaneously.
::: {.proof}
The operator $D$ is diagonalizable, hence triangulable. Since $N$ is
nilpotent, its minimal polynomial divides a power of $x$, so its characteristic
polynomial splits and $N$ is triangulable. Chapter 6, Theorem 7 states that a
commuting family of triangulable operators on a finite-dimensional space is
simultaneously triangulable [@HK71]. Thus there is a basis in which both $D$
and $N$, and consequently $T=D+N$, are upper triangular.

In such a basis the diagonal of $N$ is zero, because every diagonal entry of an
upper-triangular representation is an eigenvalue and the only eigenvalue of a
nilpotent operator is zero. Hence $T$ and $D$ have the same diagonal entries.
This observation alone does not say that the matrix of $D$ is literally
diagonal: a diagonalizable operator can have a non-diagonal triangular matrix
in a poorly chosen basis. That is exactly why the argument in Exercise 11
cannot simply take the diagonal entries of an arbitrary triangular matrix for
$T$.
:::

<1>2. One can choose a better common triangular basis in which $D$ is actually diagonal and $N$ is strictly upper triangular.
::: {.proof}
Write the eigenspace decomposition of the diagonalizable operator $D$ as
$$
V=V_{\lambda_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\lambda_r}.
$$
Because $DN=ND$, every eigenspace is $N$-invariant: if
$Dv=\lambda_i v$, then
$$
D(Nv)=N(Dv)=\lambda_iNv.
$$
Thus $N_i=N|_{V_{\lambda_i}}$ is a nilpotent operator on
$V_{\lambda_i}$. Its minimal polynomial is a power of $x$, so it is
triangulable. Choose a basis of each $V_{\lambda_i}$ in which $N_i$ is upper
triangular, and concatenate these bases.

In this concatenated basis, $D$ acts as the scalar $\lambda_i$ on every vector
of the $i$th block, so its matrix is diagonal. Each block of $N$ is upper
triangular and nilpotent, hence has zero diagonal; therefore the whole matrix
of $N$ is strictly upper triangular. Consequently
$$
[T]=[D]+[N]
$$
really is a diagonal matrix plus a commuting strictly upper-triangular
nilpotent matrix in this specially adapted basis.
:::

<1>3. This identifies the precise correction to the proof in Exercise 11.
::: {.proof}
The failed proof begins with an arbitrary triangularizing basis for $T$ and
declares the diagonal and strictly upper-triangular pieces to be the desired
$D$ and $N$. Those pieces need not commute. The valid argument runs in the
opposite direction: first obtain the intrinsic commuting parts $D,N$, then
choose a basis adapted simultaneously to them as in step <1>2. In that basis
the matrix split has the desired shape and represents the actual
diagonalizable--nilpotent decomposition.
:::
:::
