::: {.exercise}
What's wrong with the following proof of Theorem 13? Suppose that the minimal polynomial for $T$ is a product of linear factors.
Then, by Theorem 5, $T$ is triangulable.
Let $\mathfrak{B}$ be an ordered basis such that $A = [T]_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is upper-triangular.
Let $D$ be the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $a_{11}, \ldots, a_{nn}$.
Then $A = D + N$, where $N$ is strictly upper-triangular.
Evidently $N$ is nilpotent.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The proof claims $A = D + N$ with $D$ diagonal and $N$ nilpotent, and implicitly that $D$ and $N$ commute (so that this is the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition).
::: {.proof}
restate what the proof asserts.
:::

<1>2. The error is that $D$ and $N$ need not commute.
::: {.proof}
for a general upper-triangular matrix $A$, the diagonal part $D$ and the strictly-upper-triangular part $N$ do not commute in general.
:::

<1>3. Example: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, so $D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; then $DN = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ but $ND = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $DN \neq ND$.
::: {.proof}
explicit counterexample.
:::

<1>4. The Jordan decomposition requires $D$ and $N$ to commute (and $D$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent); without commutativity, $A = D + N$ is not the Jordan decomposition.
::: {.proof}
the definition of the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition.
:::

<1>5. Hence the proof is wrong because it does not establish (and in general cannot establish) that $D$ and $N$ commute.
::: {.proof}
<1>2–<1>4.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
<1>5.
:::
:::
