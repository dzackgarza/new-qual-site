::: {.exercise}
What's wrong with the following proof of Theorem 13? Suppose that the minimal polynomial for $T$ is a product of linear factors.
Then, by Theorem 5, $T$ is triangulable.
Let $\mathfrak{B}$ be an ordered basis such that $A = [T]_{\mathfrak{B}}$ is upper-triangular.
Let $D$ be the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $a_{11}, \ldots, a_{nn}$.
Then $A = D + N$, where $N$ is strictly upper-triangular.
Evidently $N$ is nilpotent.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step

The proof claims $A = D + N$ with $D$ diagonal and $N$ nilpotent, and implicitly that $D$ and $N$ commute (so that this is the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition).

::: pf-proof

restate what the proof asserts.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

The error is that $D$ and $N$ need not commute.

::: pf-proof

for a general upper-triangular matrix $A$, the diagonal part $D$ and the strictly-upper-triangular part $N$ do not commute in general.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

Example: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, so $D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; then $DN = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ but $ND = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $DN \neq ND$.

::: pf-proof

explicit counterexample.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

The Jordan decomposition requires $D$ and $N$ to commute (and $D$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent); without commutativity, $A = D + N$ is not the Jordan decomposition.

::: pf-proof

the definition of the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

Hence the proof is wrong because it does not establish (and in general cannot establish) that $D$ and $N$ commute.

::: pf-proof

Steps [](#s2){.pf-ref}, [](#s3){.pf-ref} and [](#s4){.pf-ref}.

:::
