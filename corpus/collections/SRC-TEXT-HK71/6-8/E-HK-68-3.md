::: {.exercise}
If $V$ is the space of all polynomials of degree less than or equal to $n$ over a field $F$, prove that the differentiation operator on $V$ is nilpotent.
:::

::: {.solution}

The argument is the card's own: no copy of Hoffman and Kunze and no solution set for it is retained in `tmp/sources`, so nothing here is transcribed. The statement is the exercise the collection index records as Hoffman and Kunze, *Linear Algebra* §6.8 Exercise 3.

Let $V=\{f\in F[x]\mid \deg f\leq n\}$ with the usual basis $1,x,\dots,x^{n}$, and let $D\colon V\to V$ be formal differentiation, $D(x^{k})=kx^{k-1}$ for $k\geq1$ and $D(1)=0$. An operator is nilpotent when some power of it is the zero operator.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #basis}
$1,x,\dots,x^{n}$ is a basis of $V$, and $D$ is a linear operator on $V$.

::: pf-proof
Every element of $V$ is a unique combination $\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}$ by the definition of a polynomial of degree at most $n$, so the listed monomials span $V$ and are independent. Differentiation of $x^{k}$ returns a multiple of $x^{k-1}$, hence an element of $V$, and the usual rules $D(f+g)=Df+Dg$ and $D(af)=aDf$ hold, so $D$ is a linear operator.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #power}
For $0\leq k\leq n$ and $j\geq0$,
$$
D^{j}(x^{k})=\begin{cases}k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)\,x^{k-j},&j\leq k,\\ 0,&j>k.\end{cases}
$$

::: pf-proof
Induction on $j$. For $j=0$ the formula reads $x^{k}=x^{k}$. If $j\leq k$ and the formula holds for $j$, then $D^{j+1}(x^{k})=D\bigl(k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)x^{k-j}\bigr)=k(k-1)\cdots(k-j)x^{k-j-1}$ when $j<k$, and $D(x^{0})=0$ when $j=k$; this is the formula for $j+1$ in both cases. For $j>k$ the value is already $0$ and stays $0$ since $D(0)=0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #vanishes}
$D^{n+1}(x^{k})=0$ for every $k$ with $0\leq k\leq n$.

::: pf-proof
Here $j=n+1>k$, so step [](#power){.pf-ref} gives $0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #zero-operator}
$D^{n+1}=0$ as an operator on $V$.

::: pf-proof
$D^{n+1}$ is linear, being an iterate of the linear operator $D$. A linear map that vanishes on a basis vanishes everywhere: if $f=\sum_{k}a_{k}x^{k}$ then $D^{n+1}f=\sum_{k}a_{k}D^{n+1}(x^{k})=0$ by step [](#vanishes){.pf-ref}.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #nilpotent}
$D$ is nilpotent.

::: pf-proof
Step [](#zero-operator){.pf-ref} exhibits the positive power $n+1$ at which $D$ becomes zero.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #index}
If $F$ has characteristic $0$ then $D^{n}(x^{n})=n!\,x^{0}\neq0$, so the nilpotence index of $D$ is exactly $n+1$.

::: pf-proof
$D^{n}(x^{n})=n(n-1)\cdots1\cdot x^{0}=n!$ by step [](#power){.pf-ref}. In characteristic $0$ no positive integer is zero, so $n!\neq0$ and $D^{n}\neq0$; with step [](#zero-operator){.pf-ref} this makes $n+1$ the least exponent annihilating $D$. Over a field of characteristic $p$ dividing some factor of $n!$ the index can be smaller, which is why the exercise asks only for nilpotence.
:::
