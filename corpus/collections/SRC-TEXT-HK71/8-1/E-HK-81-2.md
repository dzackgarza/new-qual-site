::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$.
Show that the sum of two inner products on $V$ is an inner product on $V$.
Is the difference of two inner products an inner product?
Show that a positive multiple of an inner product is an inner product.
:::


::: {.solution}
Let $(\cdot\mid\cdot)_1$ and $(\cdot\mid\cdot)_2$ be inner products on $V$.

<1>1. Their sum is an inner product.
::: {.proof}
Define
$$
(\alpha\mid\beta)=(\alpha\mid\beta)_1+(\alpha\mid\beta)_2.
$$
Linearity in the first variable and conjugate symmetry follow term by term from
the corresponding axioms for the two summands. Moreover
$$
(\alpha\mid\alpha)
=(\alpha\mid\alpha)_1+(\alpha\mid\alpha)_2\ge0,
$$
and if this sum is zero, both nonnegative summands are zero, so $\alpha=0$.
Thus the sum is positive definite and hence an inner product [@HK71].
:::

<1>2. The difference of two inner products need not be an inner product.
::: {.proof}
Take the same inner product twice. Their difference is the zero form, and for
any nonzero $\alpha$ it satisfies
$$
(\alpha\mid\alpha)_1-(\alpha\mid\alpha)_1=0.
$$
Hence positive definiteness fails. So the answer is $\boxed{\text{no}}$.
:::

<1>3. Every positive scalar multiple of an inner product is an inner product.
::: {.proof}
If $c>0$, define
$$
[\alpha,\beta]=c(\alpha\mid\beta).
$$
Linearity and conjugate symmetry are immediate, while
$$
[\alpha,\alpha]=c(\alpha\mid\alpha)>0
$$
for every nonzero $\alpha$. Thus $[\cdot,\cdot]$ is an inner product.
:::
:::
