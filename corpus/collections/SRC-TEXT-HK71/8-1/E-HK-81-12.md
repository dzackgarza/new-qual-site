::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space and let $\mathfrak{B} = \{\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n\}$ be a basis for $V$.
Let $(\quad | \quad)$ be an inner product on $V$.
If $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ are any $n$ scalars, show that there is exactly one vector $\alpha$ in $V$ such that $(\alpha \mid \alpha_j) = c_j$, $j = 1, \ldots, n$.
:::

::: solution
Define
$$
\Phi:V\longrightarrow F^n,
\qquad
\Phi(\alpha)
=\bigl((\alpha\mid\alpha_1),\ldots,(\alpha\mid\alpha_n)\bigr).
$$
With the convention used in Hoffman--Kunze, the inner product is linear in
its first argument, so $\Phi$ is linear [@HK71].

We claim that $\Phi$ is injective. If $\Phi(\alpha)=0$, then
$$
(\alpha\mid\alpha_j)=0
$$
for every basis vector $\alpha_j$. By linearity in the second slot up to the
usual conjugation convention, this implies
$$
(\alpha\mid\beta)=0
$$
for every $\beta\in V$, because the $\alpha_j$ span $V$. Taking
$\beta=\alpha$ gives
$$
(\alpha\mid\alpha)=0,
$$
and positive definiteness forces $\alpha=0$. Thus $\ker\Phi=0$.

Now
$$
\dim_FV=n=\dim_FF^n.
$$
An injective linear map between finite-dimensional spaces of the same
dimension is bijective. Therefore for every
$$
(c_1,\ldots,c_n)\in F^n
$$
there exists a unique $\alpha\in V$ such that
$$
\Phi(\alpha)=(c_1,\ldots,c_n),
$$
that is,
$$
\boxed{(\alpha\mid\alpha_j)=c_j\quad(j=1,\ldots,n).}
$$
:::
