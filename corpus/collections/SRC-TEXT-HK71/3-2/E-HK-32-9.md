::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on the finite-dimensional space $V$.
Suppose there is a linear operator $U$ on $V$ such that $TU = I$.
Prove that $T$ is invertible and $U = T^{-1}$.
Give an example which shows that this is false when $V$ is not finite-dimensional.
(Hint: Let $T = D$, the differentiation operator on the space of polynomial functions.)
:::

::: solution
Assume first that $V$ is finite-dimensional and $TU=I$. Then $T$ is onto,
because every $v\in V$ equals $T(Uv)$. For an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional
space, surjectivity implies injectivity, hence $T$ is invertible. Multiplying
$TU=I$ on the left by $T^{-1}$ gives
\[
U=T^{-1}.
\]

The finite-dimensional hypothesis is essential. Let $V=\mathbb R[x]$, let
$T=D$ be differentiation, and define
\[
(Uf)(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt.
\]
Then $U$ maps polynomials to polynomials and
\[
TU=D\circ U=I.
\]
But
\[
UT(f)=\int_0^x f'(t)\,dt=f(x)-f(0),
\]
so $UT\ne I$; for instance $UT(1)=0$. Thus a right inverse need not be a
left inverse in infinite dimension.
:::
