::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on the finite-dimensional space $V$.
Prove that there exists a vector $\alpha$ in $V$ with this property.
If $f$ is a polynomial and $f(T)\alpha = 0$, then $f(T) = 0$.
(Such a vector $\alpha$ is called a separating vector for the algebra of polynomials in $T$.)
When $T$ has a cyclic vector, give a direct proof that any cyclic vector is a separating vector for the algebra of polynomials in $T$.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. A separating vector always exists.
::: proof
Let $m_T$ be the minimal polynomial of $T$. The preceding consequence of the
cyclic decomposition theorem gives a vector $\alpha\in V$ whose
$T$-annihilator is exactly $m_T$ [@HK71]. Thus, if
$$
f(T)\alpha=0,
$$
then $f$ belongs to the annihilator ideal generated by $m_T$, so
$$
m_T\mid f.
$$
Write $f=m_Tq$. Since $m_T(T)=0$ on all of $V$,
$$
f(T)=m_T(T)q(T)=0.
$$
Hence this $\alpha$ is a separating vector for the algebra of polynomials in
$T$.
:::

<1>2. Every cyclic vector is separating, directly.
::: proof
Now suppose $\alpha$ is cyclic and $f(T)\alpha=0$. Every vector $v\in V$ has
the form
$$
v=g(T)\alpha
$$
for some polynomial $g$, because $Z(\alpha;T)=V$. Polynomial expressions in
$T$ commute, so
$$
f(T)v=f(T)g(T)\alpha=g(T)f(T)\alpha=0.
$$
Thus $f(T)$ annihilates every vector in $V$, hence
$$
f(T)=0.
$$
Therefore every cyclic vector is separating.
:::
:::
