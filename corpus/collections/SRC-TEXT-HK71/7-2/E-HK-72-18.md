::: {.exercise}
If $T$ is a diagonalizable linear operator, then every $T$-invariant subspace has a complementary $T$-invariant subspace.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let the distinct eigenvalues of $T$ be $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$, with
eigenspaces
$$
E_i=\ker(T-\lambda_iI).
$$
Diagonalizability gives
$$
V=E_1\oplus\cdots\oplus E_r
$$
[@HK71]. Let $W\subseteq V$ be $T$-invariant.

<1>1. The subspace $W$ splits across the eigenspaces.
::: {.proof}
For each $i$, the Lagrange polynomial
$$
p_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}
$$
defines the spectral projection $p_i(T)$ onto $E_i$. Since $W$ is invariant
under $T$, it is invariant under every polynomial in $T$. Therefore
$$
p_i(T)W\subseteq W.
$$
If $w=\sum_iw_i$ is the eigenspace decomposition of $w\in W$, then
$w_i=p_i(T)w\in W$. Hence
$$
W=(W\cap E_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus(W\cap E_r).
$$
:::

<1>2. Choose complements inside the eigenspaces.
::: {.proof}
For each $i$, choose an ordinary vector-space complement $F_i$ such that
$$
E_i=(W\cap E_i)\oplus F_i.
$$
This is possible by extending a basis of $W\cap E_i$ to a basis of $E_i$.
Every subspace of $E_i$ is $T$-invariant, because $T$ acts on $E_i$ as the
scalar $\lambda_i$. Thus each $F_i$ is $T$-invariant.
:::

<1>3. Their direct sum is the required invariant complement.
::: {.proof}
Put
$$
F=F_1\oplus\cdots\oplus F_r.
$$
It is $T$-invariant by step <1>2. Combining the decompositions in steps
<1>1--<1>2 with $V=\bigoplus_iE_i$ gives
$$
V=W\oplus F.
$$
Hence every invariant subspace of a diagonalizable operator has a
complementary invariant subspace.
:::
:::
