::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on the finite-dimensional space $V$.
Prove that $T$ has a cyclic vector if and only if the following is true: Every linear operator $U$ which commutes with $T$ is a polynomial in $T$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. A cyclic vector forces every commuting operator to be a polynomial in $T$.
::: {.proof}
If $T$ has a cyclic vector, Exercise 7.1.8 proves directly that every linear
operator commuting with $T$ is a polynomial in $T$ [@HK71].
:::

<1>2. If $T$ has no cyclic vector, construct a commuting operator that is not a polynomial in $T$.
::: {.proof}
Apply the cyclic decomposition theorem with $W_0=0$. Then
$$
V=Z(\alpha_1;T)\oplus\cdots\oplus Z(\alpha_r;T),
$$
where the nonzero vectors $\alpha_i$ have $T$-annihilators
$$
p_r\mid\cdots\mid p_2\mid p_1.
$$
If $r=1$, then $\alpha_1$ is cyclic for $T$. Thus under the present assumption
$r\ge2$.

Define $U$ to be zero on $Z(\alpha_1;T)$ and the identity on
$$
Z(\alpha_2;T)\oplus\cdots\oplus Z(\alpha_r;T).
$$
Each summand is $T$-invariant, so $UT=TU$.

Suppose $U=q(T)$ for some polynomial $q$. Since
$$
q(T)\alpha_1=U\alpha_1=0,
$$
the annihilator property gives $p_1\mid q$. Hence every $p_i$ divides $q$.
Therefore $q(T)$ annihilates every cyclic summand, so $U=q(T)=0$ on all of
$V$. But $U$ is the identity on the nonzero space $Z(\alpha_2;T)$, a
contradiction. Thus this commuting $U$ is not a polynomial in $T$.
:::

<1>3. Conclude the equivalence.
::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 proves the forward implication, while step <1>2 proves the
contrapositive of the reverse implication. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T\text{ has a cyclic vector}\iff
\operatorname{Comm}(T)=F[T].}
$$
:::
:::
