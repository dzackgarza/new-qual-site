::: {.exercise}
An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $F$ is called orthogonal if $AA^t = I$.
If $A$ is orthogonal, show that $\det A = \pm 1$.
Give an example of an orthogonal matrix for which $\det A = -1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise: the collection's provenance is an archive.org record, so the argument is the card's own. Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix over a field $F$ with $AA^{t}=I$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #transpose}
$\det(A^{t})=\det A$ for every square matrix over any field.

::: pf-proof
The Leibniz formula writes $\det A=\sum_{\pi\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\prod_iA_{i,\pi(i)}$ with $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$ defined by the sign of the permutation. Transposing relabels $(i,j)$ by $(j,i)$, so $\det(A^{t})=\sum_\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\prod_iA_{\pi(i),i}$; reindexing each summand by $\pi^{-1}$, whose sign equals $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$, turns it into $\sum_\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\prod_jA_{j,\pi(j)}=\det A$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #product}
$\det(AA^{t})=(\det A)^2$.

::: pf-proof
By multiplicativity of the determinant and step [](#transpose){.pf-ref}, $\det(AA^{t})=\det A\det A^{t}=(\det A)^2$. No division is used, so this is valid over any field.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #unit}
$\det A=1$ or $\det A=-1$.

::: pf-proof
From $AA^{t}=I$ and step [](#product){.pf-ref}, $(\det A)^2=\det I=1$, that is $\det A$ is a root of $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ in the field $F$. A field has no zerodivisors, so a product of two elements being zero forces one factor to be zero; hence $\det A-1=0$ or $\det A+1=0$, which is the claim. When $\operatorname{char}F=2$ one has $1=-1$, so the two values coincide and the conclusion is the single statement $\det A=1$; the phrase "$\pm1$" is then not a pair of alternatives.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #example}
$A_0=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)$ is orthogonal and $\det A_0=-1$ when $\operatorname{char}F\ne2$.

::: pf-proof
A diagonal matrix is its own transpose, and $A_0A_0^{t}=A_0^2=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\dots,1)=I$, so $A_0$ is orthogonal. Its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries, $(-1)\cdot1\cdots1=-1$. This is $-1\ne1$ exactly when $\operatorname{char}F\ne2$, which is the case of the real matrices the exercise has in mind; over a field of characteristic $2$ the entry $-1$ equals $1$ and the example degenerates to the identity.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
An orthogonal matrix has $\det A=\pm1$, and $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)$ is an orthogonal matrix of determinant $-1$.

::: pf-proof
The first assertion is step [](#unit){.pf-ref} and the example is step [](#example){.pf-ref}. Together they answer both parts of the exercise.
:::
