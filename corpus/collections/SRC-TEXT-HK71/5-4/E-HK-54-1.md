::: {.exercise}
Use the classical adjoint formula to compute the inverses of each of the following $3 \times 3$ real matrices.
$$
\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 3 & 2 \\ 6 & 0 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise; the argument is the card's own, checked against Hoffman--Kunze, *Linear Algebra* (1971), §5.4 Exercise 1, p. 162, the two matrices matching the printed ones. The determinant, the nine cofactors and the inverse of the first matrix were recomputed exactly, and the orthogonality of the second was checked with $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #det}
For $A=\begin{pmatrix}-2&3&2\\6&0&3\\4&1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ one has $\det A=72$.

::: pf-proof
Expanding along the first row: $-2\det\begin{pmatrix}0&3\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}-3\det\begin{pmatrix}6&3\\4&-1\end{pmatrix}+2\det\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\4&1\end{pmatrix}=-2(0-3)-3(-6-12)+2(6-0)=6+54+12=72$.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #cofactors}
The cofactor matrix of $A$ is $C=\begin{pmatrix}-3&18&6\\5&-6&14\\9&18&-18\end{pmatrix}$.

::: pf-proof
The $(i,j)$ cofactor is $(-1)^{i+j}$ times the determinant of $A$ with row $i$ and column $j$ deleted: for instance $C_{11}=+(0\cdot(-1)-3\cdot1)=-3$, $C_{12}=-(6\cdot(-1)-3\cdot4)=18$ and $C_{13}=+(6\cdot1-0\cdot4)=6$; the remaining six are computed the same way.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #inverse}
$A^{-1}=\tfrac1{72}C^{t}=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac1{24}&\tfrac5{72}&\tfrac18\\ \tfrac14&-\tfrac1{12}&\tfrac14\\ \tfrac1{12}&\tfrac7{36}&-\tfrac14\end{pmatrix}$.

::: pf-proof
The classical adjoint formula gives $A^{-1}=\det(A)^{-1}\operatorname{adj}(A)$ with $\operatorname{adj}(A)=C^{t}$, since $\det A\neq0$ by step [](#det){.pf-ref}; transposing the cofactor matrix of step [](#cofactors){.pf-ref} and dividing by $72$ gives the displayed entries.
:::
:::

::: {.pf-step #rotation}
For $R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&0&-\sin\theta\\0&1&0\\ \sin\theta&0&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ one has $R^{-1}=R^{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&0&\sin\theta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\theta&0&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$.

::: pf-proof
Expanding along the second row gives $\det R=1\cdot(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=1$, and the product $R^{t}R$ has entries $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ on the diagonal, $0$ off it, and the middle entry $1$; so $R^{t}$ is a two-sided inverse and equals $R^{-1}$ by uniqueness of the inverse. Equivalently the cofactor matrix of $R$ is $R$ itself, so the adjoint formula gives $R^{-1}=R^{t}$.
:::
