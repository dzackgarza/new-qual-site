::: {.exercise}
Let $F$ be a subfield of the complex numbers and let $T$, $D$ be the transformations on $F[x]$ defined by
\[
T\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n} c_i x^i\right) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \frac{c_i}{1+i} x^{i+1}
\]
and
\[
D\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n} c_i x^i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} i c_i x^{i-1}.
\]

(a) Show that $T$ is a non-singular linear operator on $F[x]$.
Show also that $T$ is not invertible.

(b) Show that $D$ is a linear operator on $F[x]$ and find its null space.

(c) Show that $DT = I$, and $TD \neq I$.

(d) Show that $T[(Tf)g] = (Tf)(Tg) - T[f(Tg)]$ for all $f, g$ in $F[x]$.

(e) State and prove a rule for $D$ similar to the one given for $T$ in (d).

(f) Suppose $V$ is a non-zero subspace of $F[x]$ such that $Tf$ belongs to $V$ for each $f$ in $V$.
Show that $V$ is not finite-dimensional.

(g) Suppose $V$ is a finite-dimensional subspace of $F[x]$.
Prove there is an integer $m \geq 0$ such that $D^m f = 0$ for each $f$ in $V$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Part (a): $T$ is non-singular but not invertible:
<2>1. If $T(f) = 0$ for $f = \sum_{i=0}^n c_i x^i$, then $\sum_{i=0}^n \frac{c_i}{1+i} x^{i+1} = 0$.
Equating polynomial coefficients yields $\frac{c_i}{1+i} = 0 \implies c_i = 0$ for all $i$, so $f = 0$.
Thus $\ker(T) = \{0\}$, which means $T$ is non-singular (injective).
Proof: uniqueness of polynomial coefficients.
<2>2. For every $f \in F[x]$, the polynomial $T(f) = \sum_{i=0}^n \frac{c_i}{1+i} x^{i+1}$ has no constant term (that is, $(Tf)(0) = 0$).
Thus the constant polynomial $1$ is not in the image $\operatorname{im}(T)$.
Therefore $T$ is not surjective, hence not invertible.
Proof: $T(f)(0) = 0 \neq 1$.

<1>2. Part (b): Linearity and null space of $D$:
<2>1. For $f = \sum a_i x^i$, $g = \sum b_i x^i$, and $c \in F$:
\[
D(cf + g) = \sum_{i=1}^n i(ca_i + b_i)x^{i-1} = c \sum_{i=1}^n i a_i x^{i-1} + \sum_{i=1}^n i b_i x^{i-1} = c D(f) + D(g).
\]
Thus $D$ is linear.
Proof: definition of formal derivative.
<2>2. If $D(f) = 0$, then $\sum_{i=1}^n i c_i x^{i-1} = 0 \implies i c_i = 0$ for all $i \ge 1$.
Since $F$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ (characteristic 0), $c_i = 0$ for all $i \ge 1$.
Thus $f(x) = c_0$ is a constant polynomial.
Therefore $\ker(D) = F$ (the subspace of constant polynomials).
Proof: characteristic 0 implies $i \neq 0$ for $i \ge 1$.

<1>3. Part (c): Compositions $DT$ and $TD$:
<2>1. For any monomial $x^k$ ($k \ge 0$):
\[
DT(x^k) = D\left(\frac{x^{k+1}}{k+1}\right) = (k+1) \frac{x^k}{k+1} = x^k.
\]
By linearity, $DT(f) = f$ for all $f \in F[x]$, so $DT = I$.
Proof: evaluation on basis $\{x^k\}$.
<2>2. For the constant polynomial $1$: $TD(1) = T(0) = 0 \neq 1$.
Thus $TD \neq I$ (in general, $TD(f) = f(x) - f(0)$).
Proof: $TD(1) = 0 \neq 1$.

<1>4. Part (d): Integration by parts formula for $T$:
<2>1. Note that $(Tf)(0) = 0$ and $(Tg)(0) = 0$, so the product $((Tf)(Tg))(0) = 0$.
Proof: $Tf$ has zero constant term.
<2>2. Apply the product rule for $D$ to $(Tf)(Tg)$:
\[
D\big((Tf)(Tg)\big) = \big(D(Tf)\big)(Tg) + (Tf)\big(D(Tg)\big) = f(Tg) + (Tf)g,
\]
since $DT = I$ by Part (c).
Proof: derivative product rule and <1>3.
<2>3. Applying $T$ to both sides and using $TD(h) = h(x) - h(0)$ with $h = (Tf)(Tg)$ (where $h(0) = 0$):
\[
(Tf)(Tg) = T\big(f(Tg)\big) + T\big((Tf)g\big).
\]
Proof: $TD(h) = h$ when $h(0) = 0$.
<2>4. Rearranging terms gives $T[(Tf)g] = (Tf)(Tg) - T[f(Tg)]$.
Proof: algebraic rearrangement.

<1>5. Part (e): Product rule for $D$:
<2>1. Rule: $D(fg) = (Df)g + f(Dg)$ for all $f, g \in F[x]$.
Proof: statement of Leibniz rule.
<2>2. Proof: On basis monomials $f = x^i, g = x^j$:
\[
D(x^i x^j) = D(x^{i+j}) = (i+j)x^{i+j-1} = (i x^{i-1})x^j + x^i (j x^{j-1}) = (Dx^i)x^j + x^i(Dx^j).
\]
By bilinearity, the identity holds for all polynomials $f, g \in F[x]$.
Proof: bilinearity of multiplication and linearity of $D$.

<1>6. Part (f): $T$-invariant non-zero subspace is infinite-dimensional:
<2>1. Let $V \neq \{0\}$ be a $T$-invariant subspace, and choose a non-zero $f \in V$ of degree $d = \deg(f) \ge 0$.
Proof: $V$ is non-zero.
<2>2. Since $V$ is $T$-invariant, $T^k(f) \in V$ for all $k \ge 0$.
Proof: mathematical induction on $T$-invariance.
<2>3. Since $\deg(T(h)) = \deg(h) + 1$ for any non-zero polynomial $h$, we have $\deg(T^k(f)) = d + k$.
Proof: definition of $T$.
<2>4. The set of polynomials $\{f, T(f), T^2(f), \dots, T^{m-1}(f)\} \subset V$ have strictly increasing degrees $d, d+1, \dots, d+m-1$, hence are linearly independent for every $m \ge 1$.
Proof: polynomials of distinct degrees are linearly independent.
<2>5. Thus $\dim(V) \ge m$ for all $m \in \mathbb{N}$, which proves $\dim(V) = \infty$.
Proof: existence of arbitrarily large linearly independent sets.

<1>7. Part (g): Nilpotency of $D$ on finite-dimensional subspaces:
<2>1. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional subspace of $F[x]$, and let $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ be a basis for $V$.
Proof: finite-dimensionality.
<2>2. Let $m = \max_{1 \le j \le k} \deg(v_j) + 1$.
Proof: maximum of finitely many integers is well-defined.
<2>3. Since $\deg(D(h)) = \deg(h) - 1$, $D^{\deg(h)+1}(h) = 0$ for every non-zero polynomial $h$.
Thus $D^m(v_j) = 0$ for all $j = 1, \dots, k$.
Proof: formal derivative decreases degree by 1.
<2>4. For any $f = \sum_{j=1}^k c_j v_j \in V$, linearity implies $D^m(f) = \sum_{j=1}^k c_j D^m(v_j) = 0$.
Proof: linearity of $D^m$.

<1>8. Conclusion:
All statements (a) through (g) are proved. Q.E.D.
Proof: <1>1 through <1>7.
:::
