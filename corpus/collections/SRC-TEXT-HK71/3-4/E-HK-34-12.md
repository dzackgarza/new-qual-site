::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space over the field $F$, and let $\mathfrak{B} = \{\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n\}$ be an ordered basis for $V$.

(a) According to Theorem 1, there is a unique linear operator $T$ on $V$ such that
$$
T\alpha_j = \alpha_{j+1}, \quad j = 1, \ldots, n-1, \quad T\alpha_n = 0.
$$
What is the matrix $A$ of $T$ in the ordered basis $\mathfrak{B}$?

(b) Prove that $T^n = 0$ but $T^{n-1} \neq 0$.

(c) Let $S$ be any linear operator on $V$ such that $S^n = 0$ but $S^{n-1} \neq 0$.
Prove that there is an ordered basis $\mathfrak{B}'$ for $V$ such that the matrix of $S$ in the ordered basis $\mathfrak{B}'$ is the matrix $A$ of part (a).

(d) Prove that if $M$ and $N$ are $n \times n$ matrices over $F$ such that $M^n = N^n = 0$ but $M^{n-1} \neq 0 \neq N^{n-1}$, then $M$ and $N$ are similar.
:::

::: {.solution}

The argument is the card's own. Throughout, $\mathfrak{B}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})$ is the ordered basis of $V$ fixed in the exercise and $A$ is the $n\times n$ matrix with ones immediately below the main diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #matrix}

(a) The matrix of $T$ in $\mathfrak{B}$ is $A$, whose $j$-th column is the coordinate tuple of $T\alpha_{j}$.

::: pf-proof

The matrix of a linear operator in an ordered basis has as its $j$-th column the coordinate vector of the image of the $j$-th basis vector. For $j\le n-1$ the hypothesis gives $T\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j+1}$, whose coordinate tuple is $e_{j+1}$, the $(j+1)$-st standard vector; for $j=n$ we have $T\alpha_{n}=0$, whose coordinate tuple is $0$. So column $j$ of the matrix is $e_{j+1}$ for $j<n$ and $0$ for $j=n$, which is $A$:
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&0\\1&0&\cdots&0&0\\0&1&\ddots&0&0\\\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&1&0\end{bmatrix}.}
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #powers}

(b) $T^{k}\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j+k}$ if $j+k\le n$, and $T^{k}\alpha_{j}=0$ if $j+k>n$.

::: pf-proof

Induction on $k$. For $k=0$ the assertion reads $\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j}$ when $j\le n$, which holds. Suppose it holds for $k$. If $j+k+1\le n$ then $j+k\le n-1\le n$, so $T^{k}\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j+k}$ and then $T^{k+1}\alpha_{j}=T\alpha_{j+k}=\alpha_{j+k+1}$ by the defining property of $T$, the index $j+k$ being at most $n-1$. If $j+k+1>n$, then either $j+k>n$, in which case $T^{k}\alpha_{j}=0$ and $T^{k+1}\alpha_{j}=0$, or $j+k=n$, in which case $T^{k}\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{n}$ and $T^{k+1}\alpha_{j}=T\alpha_{n}=0$. So the formula propagates.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #nilpotent}

(b) $T^{n}=0$ and $T^{n-1}\ne0$.

::: pf-proof

For every $j$ we have $j+n>n$, so $T^{n}\alpha_{j}=0$ by step [](#powers){.pf-ref}; the basis vectors span $V$, so $T^{n}$ vanishes on $V$. But $1+(n-1)=n$, so $T^{n-1}\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{n}\ne0$, a basis vector being nonzero. Hence $T^{n-1}$ is not the zero operator, even though $T^{n}$ is.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #cyclicvector}

(c) If $S^{n}=0$ and $S^{n-1}\ne0$, there is $v\in V$ with $S^{n-1}v\ne0$.

::: pf-proof

$S^{n-1}$ is not the zero operator, so some $v\in V$ has $S^{n-1}v\ne0$; fix such a $v$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #cyclicbasis}

(c) $\mathfrak{B}'=(v,Sv,\dots,S^{n-1}v)$ is an ordered basis of $V$.

::: pf-proof

There are $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space, so it suffices to prove them linearly independent. Let $\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}S^{j-1}v=0$. Applying $S^{n-1}$ gives $\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}S^{n+j-2}v=0$; every term with $j\ge2$ involves $S^{m}$ with $m\ge n$, hence vanishes because $S^{n}=0$, so $c_{1}S^{n-1}v=0$ and $c_{1}=0$ by the choice of $v$ in step [](#cyclicvector){.pf-ref}. Applying $S^{n-2}$ to the surviving relation $\sum_{j=2}^{n}c_{j}S^{j-1}v=0$ likewise kills all terms with $j\ge3$ and leaves $c_{2}S^{n-1}v=0$, so $c_{2}=0$. Continuing down to $S^{0}$, every $c_{j}=0$. Hence the list is independent and, having $n=\dim V$ elements, is a basis.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #samematrix}

(c) The matrix of $S$ in $\mathfrak{B}'$ is $A$.

::: pf-proof

In the ordered basis $\mathfrak{B}'$ the $j$-th basis vector is $S^{j-1}v$. For $j\le n-1$ its image under $S$ is $S^{j}v$, the $(j+1)$-st member of $\mathfrak{B}'$, so column $j$ is $e_{j+1}$; for $j=n$ the image is $S^{n}v=0$, so column $n$ is $0$. That is exactly the matrix $A$ of step [](#matrix){.pf-ref}. Hence
$$
\boxed{[S]_{\mathfrak{B}'}=A=[T]_{\mathfrak{B}}.}
$$

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #similar}

(d) If $M^{n}=N^{n}=0$ and $M^{n-1}\ne0\ne N^{n-1}$, then $M$ and $N$ are similar.

::: pf-proof

Let $S$ be the operator of $F^{n}$ whose matrix in the standard basis is $N$. Then $S^{n}=0$ and $S^{n-1}\ne0$, being the operator with matrix $N^{n}$ and $N^{n-1}$. By steps [](#cyclicbasis){.pf-ref} and [](#samematrix){.pf-ref} there is an ordered basis in which the matrix of $S$ is $A$, so $N$ is similar to $A$. The same argument applied to $M$ shows $M$ is similar to $A$. Similarity of matrices is an equivalence relation (it is $P\mapsto Q^{-1}PQ$ with $Q$ invertible), so $M$ is similar to $N$, as required.

:::
