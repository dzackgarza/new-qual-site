::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be an inner product space and $\beta, \gamma$ fixed vectors in $V$.
Show that $T\alpha = (\alpha \mid \beta)\gamma$ defines a linear operator on $V$.
Show that $T$ has an adjoint, and describe $T^*$ explicitly.

Now suppose $V$ is $C^n$ with the standard inner product, $\beta = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$, and $\gamma = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.
What is the $j, k$ entry of the matrix of $T$ in the standard ordered basis?
What is the rank of this matrix?
:::

::: solution
Define
$$
T\alpha=(\alpha\mid\beta)\gamma.
$$

<1>1. The map $T$ is linear.
::: proof
Hoffman--Kunze use the convention that the inner product is linear in its
first argument [@HK71]. Thus, for scalars $a,b$ and vectors $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
T(a\alpha_1+b\alpha_2)
&=(a\alpha_1+b\alpha_2\mid\beta)\gamma\\
&=a(\alpha_1\mid\beta)\gamma+b(\alpha_2\mid\beta)\gamma\\
&=aT\alpha_1+bT\alpha_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

<1>2. The adjoint is another rank-one operator:
$$
\boxed{T^*\delta=(\delta\mid\gamma)\beta.}
$$
::: proof
For arbitrary $\alpha,\delta\in V$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
(T\alpha\mid\delta)
&=((\alpha\mid\beta)\gamma\mid\delta)\\
&=(\alpha\mid\beta)(\gamma\mid\delta).
\end{aligned}
$$
On the other hand, conjugate-linearity in the second argument gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\alpha\mid(\delta\mid\gamma)\beta)
&=\overline{(\delta\mid\gamma)}(\alpha\mid\beta)\\
&=(\gamma\mid\delta)(\alpha\mid\beta).
\end{aligned}
$$
The two expressions agree for every $\alpha,\delta$, so the displayed formula
is the adjoint.
:::

<1>3. In the standard basis of $\mathbb C^n$, the $(j,k)$ entry is
$$
\boxed{x_j\overline{y_k}.}
$$
::: proof
Let $e_k$ be the $k$th standard basis vector. Since
$$
(e_k\mid\beta)=\overline{y_k},
$$
we have
$$
Te_k=\overline{y_k}\gamma
=\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\overline{y_k}\\
\vdots\\
x_n\overline{y_k}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The $k$th column of the matrix of $T$ is therefore the displayed vector. Hence
$$
[T]=xy^*,
$$
where $x=(x_j)^t$ and $y=(y_j)^t$.
:::

<1>4. The rank is zero exactly when one of the defining vectors is zero, and otherwise it is one.
::: proof
If $\beta=0$ or $\gamma=0$, then $T=0$ and the rank is $0$. Suppose both are
nonzero. Every value of $T$ is a scalar multiple of $\gamma$, so
$\operatorname{range}T\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{\gamma\}$. But
$$
T\beta=(\beta\mid\beta)\gamma\ne0,
$$
so $\gamma$ lies in the range. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{range}T=\operatorname{span}\{\gamma\},
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}T=1.}
$$
:::
:::
