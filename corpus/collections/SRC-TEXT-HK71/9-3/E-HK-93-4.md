::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space.
If $T$ and $U$ are positive linear operators on $V$, prove that $(T + U)$ is positive.
Give an example which shows that $TU$ need not be positive.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The sum of two positive operators is positive.
::: {.proof}
If $T$ and $U$ are positive, then both are self-adjoint, so
$$
(T+U)^*=T^*+U^*=T+U.
$$
For every nonzero $x$,
$$
((T+U)x\mid x)
=(Tx\mid x)+(Ux\mid x)>0.
$$
Hence $T+U$ is positive.
:::

<1>2. A product of positive operators need not be positive.
::: {.proof}
On $\mathbb R^2$ with the standard inner product, take
$$
T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The matrix $T$ is positive because its eigenvalues are $1,2$. The matrix $U$
is positive because its eigenvalues are $1,3$.

But
$$
TU
=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix},
$$
which is not symmetric. Therefore $TU$ is not self-adjoint, and hence cannot
be positive.
:::
:::
