::: {.exercise}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space.
If $T$ and $U$ are linear operators on $V$, we write $T < U$ if $U - T$ is a positive operator.
Prove the following:

(a) $T < U$ and $U < T$ is impossible.

(b) If $T < U$ and $U < S$, then $T < S$.

(c) If $T < U$ and $0 < S$, it need not be that $ST < SU$.
:::

::: solution
<1>1. The relations $T<U$ and $U<T$ cannot both hold.
::: proof
If both held, then $U-T$ and $T-U=-(U-T)$ would both be positive. For any
nonzero $x$ this would give
$$
((U-T)x\mid x)>0
$$
and also
$$
((T-U)x\mid x)=-((U-T)x\mid x)>0,
$$
which is impossible.
:::

<1>2. The relation is transitive.
::: proof
If $T<U$ and $U<S$, then $U-T$ and $S-U$ are positive. Their sum is
$$
(S-U)+(U-T)=S-T.
$$
The sum of positive operators is positive, so $T<S$.
:::

<1>3. Left multiplication by a positive operator need not preserve the order.
::: proof
On $\mathbb R^2$ with the standard inner product, let
$$
T=0,
\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Both $U$ and $S$ are positive: their eigenvalues are respectively $(1,3)$ and
$(1,2)$. Hence
$$
T<U,
\qquad
0<S.
$$
But
$$
SU=
\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix},
$$
which is not symmetric. Thus $SU$ is not positive. Since $ST=0$, we do not
have
$$
ST<SU.
$$
This proves the claimed failure of order preservation.
:::
:::
