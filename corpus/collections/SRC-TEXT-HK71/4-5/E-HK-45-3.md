::: {.exercise}
Let $D$ be the differentiation operator on the space of polynomials over the field of complex numbers.
Let $f$ be a monic polynomial over the field of complex numbers.
Prove that
$$
f = (x - c_1) \cdots (x - c_k)
$$
where $c_1, \ldots, c_k$ are distinct complex numbers if and only if $f$ and $Df$ are relatively prime.
In other words, $f$ has no repeated root if and only if $f$ and $Df$ have no common root.
(Assume the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.)
:::

::: {.solution}

The argument is the card's own: no copy of Hoffman and Kunze and no solution set for it is retained in `tmp/sources`, so nothing here is transcribed. The statement is the exercise the collection index records as Hoffman and Kunze, *Linear Algebra* §4.5 Exercise 3, and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra is used as the exercise permits.

Two nonzero polynomials over $\CC$ are relatively prime when their greatest common divisor is a nonzero constant, that is, when they have no nonconstant common divisor. A monic $f$ of degree $0$ is $f=1$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #product-rule}
For polynomials $g,h$ over $\CC$, $(gh)'=g'h+gh'$.

::: pf-proof
Both sides are linear in $g$ for fixed $h$ and in $h$ for fixed $g$, so it suffices to check the identity on monomials $g=x^{a}$, $h=x^{b}$: the left side is $(a+b)x^{a+b-1}$ and the right is $ax^{a-1}x^{b}+x^{a}bx^{b-1}=(a+b)x^{a+b-1}$. Linear extension gives the general case.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #factorization}
By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, $f=\prod_{j=1}^{r}(x-c_{j})^{m_{j}}$ with distinct $c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in\CC$ and positive integers $m_{j}$, where $r=0$ means $f=1$.

::: pf-proof
A monic polynomial over $\CC$ of positive degree splits into linear factors, and collecting equal roots gives the displayed form with distinct $c_{j}$ and $m_{j}\geq1$. A monic polynomial of degree $0$ is $1$, the empty product.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #derivative}
$f'=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}(x-c_{i})^{m_{i}-1}\prod_{l\neq i}(x-c_{l})^{m_{l}}$.

::: pf-proof
Apply the product rule of step [](#product-rule){.pf-ref} repeatedly to the $r$ factors of step [](#factorization){.pf-ref}: differentiating one factor and leaving the rest gives the $i$-th summand $m_{i}(x-c_{i})^{m_{i}-1}\prod_{l\neq i}(x-c_{l})^{m_{l}}$, and the derivative of the product is the sum of these.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #repeated}
If some $m_{j}\geq2$, then $f$ and $f'$ have the common divisor $x-c_{j}$ and are not relatively prime.

::: pf-proof
$x-c_{j}$ divides $f$ by step [](#factorization){.pf-ref}. In step [](#derivative){.pf-ref}, the summand with $i=j$ contains the factor $(x-c_{j})^{m_{j}-1}$, which is divisible by $x-c_{j}$ because $m_{j}-1\geq1$, and every summand with $i\neq j$ contains the factor $(x-c_{j})^{m_{j}}$, which is divisible by $x-c_{j}$ too. Hence $x-c_{j}$ divides every summand of $f'$, so it divides $f'$, and it is nonconstant: a common nonconstant divisor exists.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #simple}
If all $m_{j}=1$ and $r\geq1$, then no root of $f$ is a root of $f'$.

::: pf-proof
Fix $j$. With $q_{j}\coloneqq\prod_{l\neq j}(x-c_{l})^{m_{l}}$ we have $f=(x-c_{j})q_{j}$ and $q_{j}(c_{j})=\prod_{l\neq j}(c_{j}-c_{l})^{m_{l}}\neq0$, since the $c_{l}$ are distinct and a product of nonzero complex numbers is nonzero. By step [](#product-rule){.pf-ref}, $f'=q_{j}+(x-c_{j})q_{j}'$, so $f'(c_{j})=q_{j}(c_{j})\neq0$. As $j$ was arbitrary, no root of $f$ is a root of $f'$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #coprime}
If all $m_{j}=1$, then $f$ and $f'$ are relatively prime.

::: pf-proof
Let $d$ be a common divisor of $f$ and $f'$ of positive degree. Over $\CC$ every nonconstant polynomial has a root, by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, so $d$ has a root $\zeta$; then $d(\zeta)=0$ and, $d$ dividing both $f$ and $f'$, also $f(\zeta)=f'(\zeta)=0$. For $r\geq1$ this contradicts step [](#simple){.pf-ref}. For $r=0$ we have $f=1$, whose only divisors are constants, so no such $d$ exists. Hence no common divisor is nonconstant and the two polynomials are relatively prime.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$f$ has no repeated root if and only if $f$ and $Df$ are relatively prime.

::: pf-proof
The roots of $f$ are the $c_{j}$ with multiplicities $m_{j}$ by step [](#factorization){.pf-ref}, so "$f$ has no repeated root" says all $m_{j}=1$. Step [](#repeated){.pf-ref} gives the implication from a repeated root to a common nonconstant divisor, and step [](#coprime){.pf-ref} the converse; together they are the equivalence.
:::
