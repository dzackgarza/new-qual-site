::: {.exercise}
Is the matrix $A$ of Exercise 3 similar over the field of complex numbers to a diagonal matrix?
:::

::: {.solution}
The argument is the card's own: the collection's provenance is an archive.org record and no solution set for Hoffman and Kunze is retained in this checkout. Write $m_A$ for the minimal polynomial of $A$ and take $A$ to be the $4\times4$ matrix of Exercise 3 of this section, whose card (E-HK-63-3) proves
$$
\chi_A(x)=m_A(x)=x^2(x-1)^2 .
$$

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #conjugation}
If $A=SDS^{-1}$ then $q(A)=Sq(D)S^{-1}$ for every polynomial $q\in\CC[x]$.

::: pf-proof
Evaluation at a matrix is a ring homomorphism, and conjugation by the fixed invertible $S$ is a ring homomorphism $M_4(\CC)\to M_4(\CC)$: it is additive and multiplicative, since $(SXS^{-1})(SYS^{-1})=SXYS^{-1}$. Induction on the degree gives $q(SDS^{-1})=Sq(D)S^{-1}$ for every $q$, the constant term being unaffected because $SS^{-1}=I$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #annihilator}
If $A$ is similar to a diagonal matrix $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_4)$, then $q(x)=\prod_{\lambda\in\{\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_4\}}(x-\lambda)$, the product over the distinct diagonal entries, is a product of distinct linear factors and satisfies $q(A)=0$.

::: pf-proof
The matrix $q(D)$ is diagonal, and its $i$-th diagonal entry is $q(\lambda_i)$, which is $0$ because the product defining $q$ contains the factor $x-\lambda_i$. So $q(D)=0$, and step [](#conjugation){.pf-ref} gives $q(A)=S\cdot0\cdot S^{-1}=0$. The factors are distinct by construction and each is linear, and there are at most four of them.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #divides}
$m_A$ divides every polynomial that annihilates $A$; in particular, if $A$ is diagonalizable then $m_A$ is a product of distinct linear factors.

::: pf-proof
The set of annihilating polynomials is a nonzero ideal of $\CC[x]$, since it contains the characteristic polynomial by Cayley--Hamilton, and $\CC[x]$ is a principal ideal domain; its monic generator is by definition $m_A$. So every annihilating polynomial is a multiple of $m_A$. If $q$ from step [](#annihilator){.pf-ref} annihilates $A$, then $m_A\mid q$; as $q$ is a product of distinct linear factors, so is any monic divisor of it, because a factor of $q$ is a product of a subcollection of those factors.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #repeated}
$x^2(x-1)^2$ is not a product of distinct linear factors.

::: pf-proof
The factor $x$ occurs with multiplicity $2$: $x^2(x-1)^2$ is divisible by $x^2$ but not by a product of four distinct linear polynomials, and its derivative $2x(x-1)^2+2x^2(x-1)=2x(x-1)(2x-1)$ shares the factor $x(x-1)$ with it, so it is not squarefree. Equivalently, no product of distinct linear polynomials over $\CC$ vanishes at $0$ with multiplicity $2$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
$A$ is not similar over $\CC$ to a diagonal matrix.

::: pf-proof
If it were, step [](#divides){.pf-ref} would make $m_A$ a product of distinct linear factors. But $m_A=x^2(x-1)^2$ by the card for Exercise 3, and step [](#repeated){.pf-ref} says that this is not such a product. So $A$ is not diagonalizable over $\CC$, and the answer to the exercise is no. Only the implication "diagonalizable implies squarefree minimal polynomial" was used, so the same conclusion holds over any field, and not merely over $\CC$.
:::
