::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on an $n$-dimensional space, and suppose that $T$ has $n$ distinct characteristic values.
Prove that any linear operator which commutes with $T$ is a polynomial in $T$.
:::

::: solution
Let the distinct characteristic values of $T$ be
$$
\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n.
$$

<1>1. The corresponding eigenvectors form a basis, and a commuting operator preserves each eigenline.
::: proof
Choose a nonzero eigenvector $v_i$ with
$$
Tv_i=\lambda_i v_i.
$$
Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent
[@HK71]. Since there are $n$ of them in an $n$-dimensional space,
$v_1,\ldots,v_n$ form a basis.

Let $S$ be any linear operator satisfying $ST=TS$. Then
$$
T(Sv_i)=S(Tv_i)=S(\lambda_i v_i)=\lambda_i Sv_i.
$$
Thus $Sv_i$ lies in the $\lambda_i$-eigenspace of $T$. Because the $n$
distinct eigenspaces already contribute $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors,
each eigenspace is one-dimensional. Hence for some scalar $\mu_i\in F$,
$$
Sv_i=\mu_i v_i.
$$
:::

<1>2. Interpolate the scalars $\mu_i$ by a polynomial.
::: proof
Because the $\lambda_i$ are distinct, the Lagrange polynomial
$$
p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n
\mu_i\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}
$$
is well defined over $F$ and satisfies
$$
p(\lambda_i)=\mu_i
$$
for every $i$.
:::

<1>3. The interpolating polynomial gives $S=p(T)$.
::: proof
For every basis vector $v_i$,
$$
p(T)v_i=p(\lambda_i)v_i=\mu_i v_i=Sv_i.
$$
Therefore $p(T)$ and $S$ agree on the basis
$v_1,\ldots,v_n$, so they are the same operator:
$$
\boxed{S=p(T)}.
$$
Thus every operator commuting with $T$ is a polynomial in $T$.
:::
:::
