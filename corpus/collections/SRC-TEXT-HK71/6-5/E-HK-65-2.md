::: {.exercise}
Let $F$ be a commuting family of $3 \times 3$ complex matrices.
How many linearly independent matrices can $F$ contain?
What about the $n \times n$ case?
:::

::: solution
The maximum number is
$$
\boxed{\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor+1.}
$$
For $n=3$ this is $\boxed{3}$.

Let
$$
d_n=\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor+1.
$$

<1>1. It suffices to bound the dimension of a commuting vector subspace of $M_n(\mathbb C)$.
::: proof
If $\mathcal F$ is a pairwise commuting family, then its complex linear span
$$
\mathcal A=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}(\mathcal F)
$$
is again pairwise commuting, because bilinearity gives
$$
\left[\sum_i a_iA_i,\sum_j b_jA_j\right]
=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_j[A_i,A_j]=0.
$$
Thus the number of linearly independent members of $\mathcal F$ is at most
$\dim\mathcal A$. Conversely, a basis of any commuting vector subspace is a
commuting linearly independent family. Hence the problem is exactly to find
the largest possible dimension of a commuting vector subspace of
$M_n(\mathbb C)$.
:::

<1>2. Every commuting vector subspace of $M_n(\mathbb C)$ has dimension at most $d_n$.
::: proof
We argue by induction on $n$. For $n=1$ the assertion is immediate, since
$M_1(\mathbb C)$ has dimension $1=d_1$.

Assume the result for size $n-1$, and let $\mathcal A\subseteq M_n(\mathbb C)$
be a commuting vector subspace. By simultaneous triangularization of a
commuting family over the algebraically closed field $\mathbb C$, one change
of basis makes every element of $\mathcal A$ upper triangular [@HK71].
Conjugation is a vector-space isomorphism and preserves commutation, so we may
assume from the outset that every element of $\mathcal A$ is upper triangular.

Let
$$
\pi:\mathcal A\longrightarrow M_{n-1}(\mathbb C)
$$
send a matrix to its lower-right $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ principal block. The image
is a commuting vector subspace, so by induction
$$
k:=\dim\pi(\mathcal A)\le d_{n-1}.
$$
If $d=\dim\mathcal A$, rank-nullity gives
$$
\dim\ker\pi=d-k.
$$
Because matrices in $\mathcal A$ are upper triangular, a matrix in
$\ker\pi$ can have nonzero entries only in its first row. Choose a basis
$B_1,\ldots,B_{d-k}$ of $\ker\pi$, and write its first rows as
$r_1,\ldots,r_{d-k}\in\mathbb C^n$. These row vectors are linearly independent.

Similarly, let
$$
\rho:\mathcal A\longrightarrow M_{n-1}(\mathbb C)
$$
take the upper-left $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ principal block. Its image is commuting,
so
$$
s:=\dim\rho(\mathcal A)\le d_{n-1},
\qquad
\dim\ker\rho=d-s.
$$
An upper-triangular matrix in $\ker\rho$ can have nonzero entries only in its
last column. Choose a basis $C_1,\ldots,C_{d-s}$ of this kernel, with last
columns $c_1,\ldots,c_{d-s}\in\mathbb C^n$. These column vectors are linearly
independent.

Writing $e_1,e_n$ for the first and last standard basis columns, we have
$$
B_i=e_1r_i,
\qquad
C_j=c_je_n^{T}.
$$
Since $n>1$,
$$
C_jB_i=c_j(e_n^Te_1)r_i=0,
$$
whereas
$$
B_iC_j=(r_ic_j)e_1e_n^T.
$$
All elements of $\mathcal A$ commute, so $B_iC_j=C_jB_i$ and therefore
$$
r_ic_j=0
$$
for every $i,j$.

Let $R$ be the $(d-k)\times n$ matrix whose rows are the $r_i$. Their linear
independence gives
$$
\operatorname{rank}R=d-k.
$$
Every $c_j$ lies in $\ker R$, and the $c_j$ are linearly independent, hence
$$
\operatorname{nullity}R\ge d-s.
$$
Rank-nullity in $\mathbb C^n$ now yields
$$
n\ge(d-k)+(d-s)
\ge2(d-d_{n-1}).
$$
Thus
$$
d\le d_{n-1}+\frac n2.
$$
To get the exact integer bound, suppose instead that $d\ge d_n+1$. Then
$$
d-d_{n-1}
\ge
\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor
-\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^2}{4}\right\rfloor+1.
$$
If $n=2m$, the right side is $m+1$; if $n=2m+1$, it is again $m+1$.
Consequently
$$
2(d-d_{n-1})>n,
$$
contradicting the rank-nullity inequality above. Therefore $d\le d_n$.
:::

<1>3. The bound $d_n$ is attained.
::: proof
Put
$$
p=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor,
\qquad
q=n-p=\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil.
$$
For $a\in\mathbb C$ and arbitrary $B\in M_{p\times q}(\mathbb C)$, consider
$$
X(a,B)=
\begin{pmatrix}
aI_p&B\\
0&aI_q
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For two such matrices,
$$
X(a,B)X(b,C)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
abI_p&aC+bB\\
0&abI_q
\end{pmatrix}
=X(b,C)X(a,B).
$$
Hence these matrices form a commuting vector subspace. The scalar $a$ gives
one dimension and $B$ contributes $pq$ dimensions, so its dimension is
$$
1+pq
=1+\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor
=d_n.
$$
Thus the upper bound from step <1>2 is sharp. For $n=3$, it gives
$d_3=\lfloor9/4\rfloor+1=3$.
:::
