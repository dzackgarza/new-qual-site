::: {.exercise}
Let $\alpha = (x_1, x_2)$ and $\beta = (y_1, y_2)$ be vectors in $\RR^2$ such that
$$
x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2 = 0, \quad x_1^2 + x_2^2 = y_1^2 + y_2^2 = 1.
$$
Prove that $\mathfrak{B} = \{\alpha, \beta\}$ is a basis for $\RR^2$.
Find the coordinates of the vector $(a, b)$ in the ordered basis $\mathfrak{B} = \{\alpha, \beta\}$.
(The conditions on $\alpha$ and $\beta$ say, geometrically, that $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are perpendicular and each has length 1.)
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise; the book's text is retained as `assets/attachments/Hoffman-Kunze-Linear-Algebra.pdf` (Hoffman and Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, 2nd ed., 1971), where it is §2.4 Exercise 5 on printed p. 50. The argument is the card's own.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #orthonormal}
The hypotheses say that $\mathfrak{B}=\{\alpha,\beta\}$ is orthonormal for the standard inner product on $\RR^2$.

::: pf-proof
With $\inner{u}{v}=u_1v_1+u_2v_2$, the given $x_1^2+x_2^2=1$ and $y_1^2+y_2^2=1$ are $\inner{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$ and $\inner{\beta}{\beta}=1$, and the given $x_1y_1+x_2y_2=0$ is $\inner{\alpha}{\beta}=0$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #independent}
If $c_1\alpha+c_2\beta=(0,0)$, then $c_1=c_2=0$.

::: pf-proof
Taking inner products with $\alpha$ gives $0=\inner{0}{\alpha}=c_1\inner{\alpha}{\alpha}+c_2\inner{\beta}{\alpha}=c_1$, using $\inner{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$ and $\inner{\beta}{\alpha}=0$; taking them with $\beta$ gives $0=c_1\inner{\alpha}{\beta}+c_2\inner{\beta}{\beta}=c_2$ in the same way. So $\mathfrak{B}$ is linearly independent.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #basis}
$\mathfrak{B}$ is a basis of $\RR^2$.

::: pf-proof
$\dim\RR^2=2$ and $\mathfrak{B}$ holds two linearly independent vectors; in a finite-dimensional space a linearly independent set of $\dim V$ vectors spans $V$, so it is a basis.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #coordinates}
If $v=(a,b)=c_1\alpha+c_2\beta$, then $c_1=\inner{v}{\alpha}=ax_1+bx_2$ and $c_2=\inner{v}{\beta}=ay_1+by_2$.

::: pf-proof
Taking inner products of $v=c_1\alpha+c_2\beta$ with $\alpha$ gives $\inner{v}{\alpha}=c_1\inner{\alpha}{\alpha}+c_2\inner{\beta}{\alpha}=c_1$, and with $\beta$ gives $\inner{v}{\beta}=c_2$, by step [](#orthonormal){.pf-ref}. On the other hand $\inner{v}{\alpha}=ax_1+bx_2$ and $\inner{v}{\beta}=ay_1+by_2$ by the definition of the inner product. Substituting the two values into $v=c_1\alpha+c_2\beta$ gives the coordinate vector $[(a,b)]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{pmatrix} ax_1+bx_2\\ ay_1+by_2\end{pmatrix}$.
:::
