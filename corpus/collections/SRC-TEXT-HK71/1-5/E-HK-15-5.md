::: {.exercise}
Let
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Is there a matrix $C$ such that $CA = B$?
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. $C$ must be a $2 \times 3$ matrix (so that $CA$ is $2 \times 2$).
::: {.proof}
$B$ is $2 \times 2$ and $A$ is $3 \times 2$, so $C$ is $2 \times 3$.
:::

<1>2. $CA = B$ means each row of $B$ is a linear combination of the rows of $A$ (with coefficients given by the corresponding row of $C$).
::: {.proof}
the rows of $CA$ are linear combinations of the rows of $A$.
:::

<1>3. The rows of $A$ are $r_1 = (1, -1)$, $r_2 = (2, 2)$, $r_3 = (1, 0)$; the rows of $B$ are $b_1 = (3, 1)$ and $b_2 = (-4, 4)$.
::: {.proof}
read off the rows.
:::

<1>4. $b_1 = (3, 1)$ is in the row space of $A$.
::: {.proof}
$(3, 1) = 1 \cdot (1, -1) + 1 \cdot (2, 2) + 0 \cdot (1, 0) = (1+2, -1+2) = (3, 1)$.
:::

<1>5. $b_2 = (-4, 4)$ is in the row space of $A$.
::: {.proof}
solve $c_1(1,-1) + c_2(2,2) + c_3(1,0) = (-4, 4)$, i.e. $c_1 + 2c_2 + c_3 = -4$ and $-c_1 + 2c_2 = 4$; taking $c_1 = 0$, $c_2 = 2$, $c_3 = -8$ gives $0 \cdot (1,-1) + 2 \cdot (2,2) - 8 \cdot (1,0) = (4,4) - (8,0) = (-4, 4)$.
:::

<1>6. Hence both rows of $B$ are in the row space of $A$, so there is a matrix $C$ with $CA = B$.
::: {.proof}
<1>4 and <1>5.
:::

<1>7. Explicitly, $C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -8 \end{bmatrix}$.
::: {.proof}
row 1 of $C$ gives $b_1 = 1 \cdot r_1 + 1 \cdot r_2 + 0 \cdot r_3$; row 2 gives $b_2 = 0 \cdot r_1 + 2 \cdot r_2 - 8 \cdot r_3 = (4, 4) - (8, 0) = (-4, 4)$.
:::

<1>8. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
yes, such a $C$ exists (<1>7).
:::
:::
