::: {.exercise}
Let
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
Is there a matrix $C$ such that $CA = B$?
:::

::: {.solution}
No retained solution set covers this exercise; the book's text is retained as `assets/attachments/Hoffman-Kunze-Linear-Algebra.pdf` (Hoffman and Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, 2nd ed., 1971), where it is §1.5 Exercise 5 on printed p. 21; the exercise list there is §1.5's, the §1.6 header on that page notwithstanding, §1.6 beginning below it. The argument is the card's own.

::: pf

::: pf-step
$C$ must be a $2 \times 3$ matrix (so that $CA$ is $2 \times 2$).

::: pf-proof
$B$ is $2 \times 2$ and $A$ is $3 \times 2$, so $C$ is $2 \times 3$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
$CA = B$ means each row of $B$ is a linear combination of the rows of $A$ (with coefficients given by the corresponding row of $C$).

::: pf-proof
The rows of $CA$ are linear combinations of the rows of $A$, with coefficients the corresponding row of $C$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The rows of $A$ are $r_1 = (1, -1)$, $r_2 = (2, 2)$, $r_3 = (1, 0)$; the rows of $B$ are $b_1 = (3, 1)$ and $b_2 = (-4, 4)$.

::: pf-proof
Reading the entries off $A$ and $B$ gives the displayed rows.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #b1-in-row-space}
$b_1 = (3, 1)$ is in the row space of $A$.

::: pf-proof
$(3, 1) = 1 \cdot (1, -1) + 1 \cdot (2, 2) + 0 \cdot (1, 0) = (1+2, -1+2) = (3, 1)$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #b2-in-row-space}
$b_2 = (-4, 4)$ is in the row space of $A$.

::: pf-proof
Solving $c_1(1,-1) + c_2(2,2) + c_3(1,0) = (-4, 4)$, i.e. $c_1 + 2c_2 + c_3 = -4$ and $-c_1 + 2c_2 = 4$; taking $c_1 = 0$, $c_2 = 2$, $c_3 = -8$ gives $0 \cdot (1,-1) + 2 \cdot (2,2) - 8 \cdot (1,0) = (4,4) - (8,0) = (-4, 4)$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #explicit-c-matrix}
Hence a matrix $C$ with $CA = B$ exists, and explicitly
$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -8 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

::: pf-proof
The coefficients exhibited in steps [](#b1-in-row-space){.pf-ref} and [](#b2-in-row-space){.pf-ref} are taken as the rows of $C$: row 1 times $A$ is $1\cdot r_1+1\cdot r_2+0\cdot r_3=(3,1)=b_1$, and row 2 times $A$ is $0\cdot r_1+2\cdot r_2-8\cdot r_3=(4,4)-(8,0)=(-4,4)=b_2$. So $CA=B$, and the answer is yes.

:::
