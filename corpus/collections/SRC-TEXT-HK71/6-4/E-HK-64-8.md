::: {.exercise}
Let $T$ be a linear operator on $V$.
If every subspace of $V$ is invariant under $T$, then $T$ is a scalar multiple of the identity operator.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Every non-zero vector is an eigenvector of $T$:
<2>1. Let $v \in V \setminus \{0\}$. The 1-dimensional subspace $W = \operatorname{span}(v) = F v$ is a subspace of $V$.
By hypothesis, $W$ is invariant under $T$, so $T(W) \subseteq W$.
<2>2. Therefore $T(v) \in F v$, which implies there exists a scalar $c_v \in F$ such that:
\[
T(v) = c_v v.
\]

<1>2. Proof that the scalar $c_v$ is independent of $v$:
<2>1. If $\dim(V) \le 1$, then every linear operator on $V$ is a scalar multiple of the identity: when $\dim(V) = 0$ the only operator is the zero map, and when $\dim(V) = 1$ any operator sends a basis vector $e$ to $c e$ for some $c \in F$, hence acts as $c \operatorname{id}_V$. Assume $\dim(V) \ge 2$.
Let $u, v \in V \setminus \{0\}$.
<2>2. **Subcase 1: $\{u, v\}$ is linearly dependent.**
There exists a non-zero scalar $\alpha \in F^\times$ such that $v = \alpha u$.
Then by linearity:
\[
c_v v = T(v) = T(\alpha u) = \alpha T(u) = \alpha (c_u u) = c_u (\alpha u) = c_u v.
\]
Since $v \neq 0$, $c_v = c_u$.
<2>3. **Subcase 2: $\{u, v\}$ is linearly independent.**
Consider the sum vector $w = u + v \neq 0$.
By <1>1, there exists $c_w \in F$ such that $T(w) = c_w w = c_w u + c_w v$.
By linearity of $T$:
\[
T(w) = T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) = c_u u + c_v v.
\]
Equating both expressions gives:
\[
(c_u - c_w) u + (c_v - c_w) v = 0.
\]
Because $\{u, v\}$ is linearly independent, $c_u - c_w = 0$ and $c_v - c_w = 0$, which yields $c_u = c_v = c_w$.

<1>3. Conclusion:
Fix a non-zero vector $v_0 \in V$ and set $c = c_{v_0} \in F$.
Then $T(v) = c v$ for all $v \in V \setminus \{0\}$, and for $v = 0$, $T(0) = 0 = c \cdot 0$.
Thus $T = c \operatorname{id}_V$. Q.E.D.
:::
