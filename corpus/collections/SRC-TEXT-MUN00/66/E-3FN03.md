::: {.exercise}

This exercise assumes some familiarity with analysis on manifolds.

Theorem.
Let $C$ be a simple closed curve in $\RR^2$ that is a smooth submanifold of $\RR^2$; let $f: I \to C$ be a simple loop smoothly parameterizing $C$.
If $\mathbf{0}$ is a point of the bounded component of $\RR^2 - C$, then $n(f, \mathbf{0}) = \pm 1$.

Let $U$ be the bounded component of $\RR^2 - C$.
Let $B$ be a closed $\epsilon$-ball centered at 0 that lies in $U$; let $S = \operatorname{Bd} B$.
Let $M$ equal the closure of $U - B$.

(a) Show $M$ is a smooth 2-manifold with boundary $C \cup S$.

(b) Apply Green's theorem to show that $\int_C dz/z = \pm \int_S dz/z$, the sign depending on the orientations of $S$ and $C$.
[Hint: Set $P = -y/(x^2 + y^2)$ and $Q = x/(x^2 + y^2)$.]

(c) Show that the second integral equals $\pm 2\pi i$.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step
Part (a): Smooth manifold structure of $M = \overline{U \setminus B}$.

::: pf-proof
The subclaims identify the two boundary circles and produce a chart at each kind of point, which is what makes $M$ a manifold with boundary.

::: pf-step
The bounded component $U$ is an open domain in $\RR^2$ with smooth 1-dimensional boundary $\partial U = C$.

::: pf-proof
The components of $\RR^2\setminus C$ are open because $C$ is closed, and Theorem 63.4 identifies $C$ as the boundary of each of them; by hypothesis $C$ is a smooth submanifold of $\RR^2$ of dimension $1$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The ball $B = \overline{B}(\mathbf{0}, \varepsilon) \subset U$ is a closed disk with smooth boundary $\partial B = S$.

::: pf-proof
$S=\operatorname{Bd}B$ is by definition the circle of radius $\varepsilon$ about the origin, a smooth submanifold of $\RR^2$ of dimension $1$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The difference $M = \overline{U \setminus B} = \overline U \setminus \operatorname{Int}(B)$ is a compact subset of $\RR^2$.

::: pf-proof
$\operatorname{Int}B\subseteq B\subseteq U$ because $B$ is a closed ball inside the open set $U$, so a point of $\overline U\setminus\operatorname{Int}B$ is a limit of points of $U\setminus\operatorname{Int}B$, and the two closures agree; $\overline U$ is closed and bounded, hence so is $M$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Near any interior point $p \in \operatorname{Int}(M) = U \setminus B$, $M$ is locally diffeomorphic to $\RR^2$.

::: pf-proof
$U\setminus B$ is open in $\RR^2$, so a ball about such a point lies in $M$, and a ball is diffeomorphic to $\RR^2$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Near any boundary point $p \in C$, $M$ carries the smooth boundary chart of $\overline U$; near $p \in S$, it carries the chart of $\RR^2\setminus\operatorname{Int}B$.

::: pf-proof
Let $p\in C$. By the Jordan curve theorem, Theorem 63.4, $C$ is the boundary of each of the two components of $S^2-C$, so every neighbourhood of $p$ meets the bounded component $U$ and meets the unbounded component $V$. Since $C$ is a smooth submanifold of $\RR^2$, there is a diffeomorphism $\theta$ of a neighbourhood $W$ of $p$ onto an open disc about the origin carrying $C\cap W$ onto the trace of the horizontal axis, and $W$ may be taken to miss the closed ball $B$, which is a compact subset of the open set $U$ and does not contain $p$. The two open half-discs into which that axis divides the disc are connected and miss $C$, so each lies in a single component of $\RR^2-C$. They cannot both lie in $V$: then the disc, whose points off $C$ are their union, would meet $U$ in no point, though $p$ is a limit point of $U$. They cannot both lie in $U$ either: then the disc would meet $V$ in no point, though $p$ is a limit point of $V$. So one half-disc lies in $U$ and the other in $V$.

On $W$ the closure of $U$ is $U\cup C$, by Theorem 63.4, and $W$ misses $\operatorname{Int}B$, so
$$
W\cap M=W\cap\bigl(\overline U\setminus\operatorname{Int}B\bigr)=W\cap\bigl((U\cup C)\setminus\operatorname{Int}B\bigr)=W\cap(U\cup C),
$$
and $\theta$ restricted to that set carries a neighbourhood of $p$ in $M$ onto a half-disc with $C$ on its boundary.

At $p\in S$ the chart is the one that $\RR^2\setminus\operatorname{Int}B$ carries as a smooth $2$-manifold with boundary $S=\operatorname{Bd}B$. Since $S\subseteq B\subseteq U$ and $U$ is open, a neighbourhood of $p$ lies in $U$, and there $M=\overline{U\setminus B}=\overline U\setminus\operatorname{Int}B$ agrees with $\RR^2\setminus\operatorname{Int}B$. The exterior of the closed ball is the smaller space $\RR^2\setminus B$, which omits $S$ itself; $M$ contains $S$ and is the exterior of the open ball at $p$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Thus $M$ is a smooth compact 2-manifold with boundary $\partial M = C \sqcup S$.

::: pf-proof
The preceding subclaims supply a chart of the required kind at every point of $M$ — Euclidean at interior points, half-space at points of $C$ and of $S$ — and identify the boundary with the disjoint union of those two circles, which do not meet because $C$ bounds $U$ and $S\subseteq U$; compactness was shown in the third subclaim.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}
Part (b): Application of Green's / Stokes' Theorem.

::: pf-proof
The subclaims compute the form, show that it is closed on $M$, and apply Stokes to relate the integrals over the two boundary circles.

::: pf-step
Consider the differential 1-form on $\RR^2 \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$:
$$\omega = \frac{dz}{z} = \frac{x \, dx + y \, dy}{x^2 + y^2} + i \left(\frac{-y \, dx + x \, dy}{x^2 + y^2}\right) = \alpha + i \beta.$$

::: pf-proof
Writing $z=x+iy$ and $dz=dx+i\,dy$, the quotient $dz/z=\bar z\,dz/\abs{z}^2$ expands into the displayed real and imaginary parts, which are named $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Setting $P = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}$ and $Q = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}$, we compute the exterior derivative:
$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{(x^2+y^2) - 2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} - \frac{-(x^2+y^2) - (-y)(2y)}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2 + (x^2 - y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = 0.$$

::: pf-proof
The two quotients are differentiated by the quotient rule and collected over the common denominator $(x^2+y^2)^2$; the numerator is $y^2-x^2+x^2-y^2=0$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Similarly, $d\alpha = 0$, so $d\omega = 0$ on all of $M \subset \RR^2 \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

::: pf-proof
$\alpha=\tfrac12 d\log(x^2+y^2)$ is exact, hence closed; $\beta=P\,dx+Q\,dy$ is closed by the preceding subclaim; and $d\omega=d\alpha+i\,d\beta=0$. The origin is excluded from $M$ because $M\subseteq U$ and $B$ about $\mathbf{0}$ lies in $U$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By Stokes' Theorem, $\int_{\partial M} \omega = \iint_M d\omega = 0$.

::: pf-proof
$M$ is a compact smooth manifold with boundary by part (a), so Stokes' theorem applies to the form $\omega$ on it; the integral of $d\omega$ vanishes by the preceding subclaim.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
With standard induced boundary orientations where $C$ is traversed with $M$ on its left and $S$ is traversed with $M$ on its left (clockwise on $S$), $\partial M = C - S$, which gives:
$$\int_C \frac{dz}{z} = \pm \int_S \frac{dz}{z}$$
depending on whether $C$ and $S$ are parameterized with counterclockwise orientation.

::: pf-proof
Stokes gives $\int_{\partial M}\omega=0$; the boundary is the disjoint union of $C$ and $S$ with the orientations induced from $M$, so the integral over $\partial M$ is the integral over $C$ plus the integral over $S$ with the induced orientation, and reversing the orientation of one circle changes the sign of its integral. That is the displayed relation.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
Part (c): Integration along $S$ and winding number conclusion.

::: pf-proof
The subclaims evaluate the integral over the circle and transfer the value to the winding number.

::: pf-step
Parameterize the circle $S = \partial B(\mathbf{0}, \varepsilon)$ counterclockwise by $z(t) = \varepsilon e^{it}$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$.

::: pf-proof
This is the standard counterclockwise parameterization of the circle of radius $\varepsilon$ about the origin, and it traces $S$ once.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Then $dz = i \varepsilon e^{it} \, dt$, so:
$$\int_S \frac{dz}{z} = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{i \varepsilon e^{it}}{\varepsilon e^{it}} \, dt = \int_0^{2\pi} i \, dt = 2\pi i.$$

::: pf-proof
Differentiating the parameterization gives $dz=i\varepsilon e^{it}dt$, the quotient is the constant $i$, and the integral of a constant over an interval of length $2\pi$ is $2\pi i$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By step [](#s2){.pf-ref}, $\int_C \frac{dz}{z} = \pm 2\pi i$.

::: pf-proof
Substituting the value just computed into the relation of part (b).
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By Lemma 66.3, which applies to the piecewise-differentiable loop $f$ and to the point $\mathbf{0}$ outside its image, the winding number of printed p. 403, defined by lifting, equals the integral
$$n(f, \mathbf{0}) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_C \frac{dz}{z} = \frac{\pm 2\pi i}{2\pi i} = \pm 1.$$

::: pf-proof
Lemma 66.3 identifies the winding number of a piecewise-differentiable loop about a point outside its image with that normalized integral; substituting the value of the preceding subclaim gives $\pm1$, and the sign depends only on the orientation of $f$.
:::
