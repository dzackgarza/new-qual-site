::: {.exercise}

Theorem.
Let $J$ and $C$ be well-ordered sets; assume that there is no surjective function mapping a section of $J$ onto $C$. Then there exists a unique function $h \colon J \to C$ satisfying the equation

(\*)
$$
h(x) = \text{smallest}\,[C - h(S_{x})]
$$
for each $x \in J$, where $S_{x}$ is the section of $J$ by $x$.

(a) If $h$ and $k$ map sections of $J$, or all of $J$, into $C$ and satisfy (\*) for all $x$ in their respective domains, show that $h(x) = k(x)$ for all $x$ in both domains.

(b) If there exists a function $h \colon S_{\alpha} \to C$ satisfying (\*), show that there exists a function $k \colon S_{\alpha} \cup \{\alpha\} \to C$ satisfying (\*).

(c) If $K \subset J$ and for all $\alpha \in K$ there exists a function $h_{\alpha} \colon S_{\alpha} \to C$ satisfying (\*), show that there exists a function
$$
k \colon \bigcup_{\alpha \in K} S_{\alpha} \longrightarrow C
$$
satisfying (\*).

(d) Show by transfinite induction that for every $\beta \in J$, there exists a function $h_{\beta} \colon S_{\beta} \to C$ satisfying (\*). [Hint: If $\beta$ has an immediate predecessor $\alpha$, then $S_{\beta} = S_{\alpha} \cup \{\alpha\}$. If not, $S_{\beta}$ is the union of all $S_{\alpha}$ with $\alpha < \beta$.]

(e) Prove the theorem.
:::

::: {.solution}
The complete published solution is **Dan Whitman, *Topology, Second Edition by James Munkres: Solutions Manual* (30 November 2019), Exercise 10.10, printed pp. 115--117** (`tmp/sources/munkres-solutions-whitman.pdf`); all five parts are his, together with the lemma reducing proof by transfinite induction to Exercise 10.7.

Throughout, $J$ and $C$ are well ordered, and the standing hypothesis is that **no surjective function maps a section of $J$ onto $C$**. That hypothesis is what makes $(\ast)$ meaningful: if $h$ is defined on a subset of $J$ containing $S_{x}$ and takes values in $C$, then $h\restrict_{S_{x}}$ maps the section $S_{x}$ into $C$ and is not surjective, so $C-h(S_{x})\ne\varnothing$ and has a smallest element because $C$ is well ordered.

**Lemma** (proof by transfinite induction). Let $P(x)$ be a proposition with parameter $x\in J$, and suppose that $P(\alpha)$ holds whenever $P(x)$ holds for all $x\in S_{\alpha}$. Then $P(\beta)$ holds for every $\beta\in J$.

*Proof.* Let $J_{0}=\{x\in J\mid P(x)\}$. If $S_{\alpha}\subset J_{0}$ then $P(x)$ holds for every $x\in S_{\alpha}$, hence $P(\alpha)$ holds and $\alpha\in J_{0}$; so $J_{0}$ is inductive, and $J_{0}=J$ by Exercise 10.7.

(a) Let $h$ and $k$ be functions into $C$ satisfying $(\ast)$ on their domains $H$ and $K$, each a section of $J$ or all of $J$. Sections of a well-ordered set are nested and all of $J$ contains every section, so assume $H\subset K$. If $h$ and $k$ disagreed somewhere in $H$, there would be a smallest $x\in H$ with $h(x)\ne k(x)$, since $H\subset J$ is well ordered. Then $h(y)=k(y)$ for every $y\in S_{x}$, that is $h(S_{x})=k(S_{x})$, and both functions satisfy $(\ast)$ at $x$, so
$$
h(x)=\text{smallest}[C-h(S_{x})]=\text{smallest}[C-k(S_{x})]=k(x),
$$
a contradiction. Hence $h(x)=k(x)$ for all $x$ in both domains.

(b) Let $h:S_{\alpha}\to C$ satisfy $(\ast)$. Put $\bar S_{\alpha}=S_{\alpha}\cup\{\alpha\}$ and define $k:\bar S_{\alpha}\to C$ by
$$
k(x)=\begin{cases} h(x), & x\in S_{\alpha},\\ \text{smallest}[C-h(S_{\alpha})], & x=\alpha.\end{cases}
$$
This is well defined because $S_{\alpha}\cap\{\alpha\}=\varnothing$, and $C-h(S_{\alpha})\ne\varnothing$ by the standing hypothesis. For $x\in S_{\alpha}$ we have $k(y)=h(y)$ for all $y\in S_{x}\subset S_{\alpha}$, so $k(S_{x})=h(S_{x})$ and
$$
k(x)=h(x)=\text{smallest}[C-h(S_{x})]=\text{smallest}[C-k(S_{x})];
$$
for $x=\alpha$ the same identity holds because $k(y)=h(y)$ for all $y\in S_{\alpha}$. So $k$ satisfies $(\ast)$.

(c) Let $K\subset J$ and suppose that for every $\alpha\in K$ there is $h_{\alpha}:S_{\alpha}\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$. Put $k=\bigcup_{\alpha\in K}h_{\alpha}$, a set of ordered pairs.

$k$ is a function: if $(x,a),(x,b)\in k$ there are $\alpha,\beta\in K$ with $(x,a)\in h_{\alpha}$ and $(x,b)\in h_{\beta}$, so $x$ lies in the domains $S_{\alpha}$ and $S_{\beta}$ of both, and (a) gives $a=h_{\alpha}(x)=h_{\beta}(x)=b$. Its domain is $\bigcup_{\alpha\in K}S_{\alpha}$ by the definition of union of relations, and its values lie in $C$. If $x\in\bigcup_{\alpha\in K}S_{\alpha}$, say $x\in S_{\alpha}$, then $k(y)=h_{\alpha}(y)$ for all $y\in S_{x}$, because $S_{x}\subset S_{\alpha}$, so $k(S_{x})=h_{\alpha}(S_{x})$ and
$$
k(x)=h_{\alpha}(x)=\text{smallest}[C-h_{\alpha}(S_{x})]=\text{smallest}[C-k(S_{x})].
$$
So $k$ satisfies $(\ast)$ on $\bigcup_{\alpha\in K}S_{\alpha}$.

(d) We prove by transfinite induction on $\beta\in J$ that there is $h_{\beta}:S_{\beta}\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$. Let $\beta\in J$ and assume this holds for every $x\in S_{\beta}$.

If $\beta$ has an immediate predecessor $\alpha$, then $S_{\beta}=S_{\alpha}\cup\{\alpha\}$: for $x\in S_{\beta}$ we have $x<\beta$, so $x\le\alpha$ and $x\in S_{\alpha}$ or $x=\alpha$; conversely $x\in S_{\alpha}$ gives $x<\alpha<\beta$ and $x=\alpha$ gives $x<\beta$. Since $\alpha\in S_{\beta}$, the induction hypothesis gives $h_{\alpha}:S_{\alpha}\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$, and (b) extends it to $h_{\beta}$ on $S_{\beta}$.

If $\beta$ has no immediate predecessor, then $S_{\beta}=\bigcup_{\gamma<\beta}S_{\gamma}$: one inclusion is clear; for the other, if $x<\beta$ there is $\gamma$ with $x<\gamma<\beta$ (otherwise $x$ would be the immediate predecessor of $\beta$), so $x\in S_{\gamma}$. By the induction hypothesis each $\gamma<\beta$ has $h_{\gamma}$ satisfying $(\ast)$, and (c) applied to $K=S_{\beta}$ produces $h_{\beta}$ on $\bigcup_{\gamma\in S_{\beta}}S_{\gamma}=S_{\beta}$ satisfying $(\ast)$.

By the lemma, $h_{\beta}$ exists for every $\beta\in J$.

(e) If $J$ has no largest element then $J=\bigcup_{\alpha\in J}S_{\alpha}$: every $x\in J$ satisfies $x<y$ for some $y\in J$, so $x\in S_{y}$, and each $S_{\alpha}\subset J$. Part (d) gives $h_{\alpha}:S_{\alpha}\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$ for every $\alpha\in J$, and (c) applied to $K=J$ gives $h:J\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$.

If $J$ has a largest element $\beta$ then $J=S_{\beta}\cup\{\beta\}$: every $x\in J$ has $x<\beta$ or $x=\beta$. Part (d) gives $h_{\beta}:S_{\beta}\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$ and (b) extends it to $h:J\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$.

If $J=\varnothing$ the empty function $J\to C$ is the unique function $J\to C$ and satisfies $(\ast)$ vacuously.

In every case a function $h:J\to C$ satisfying $(\ast)$ exists, and it is unique by (a).
:::
