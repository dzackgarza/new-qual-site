::: {.exercise}

Let $X$ be a space that is written as the union of finitely many arcs $A_1, \ldots, A_n$, each pair of which intersect in at most a common end point.

(a) Show that $X$ is Hausdorff if and only if each arc $A_i$ is closed in $X$.

(b) Give an example to show that $X$ need not be Hausdorff.
[Hint: See [[E-29WP0]].]
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step
Part (a): $X$ is Hausdorff $\iff$ each $A_i$ is closed in $X$.

::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #forward}
If $X$ is Hausdorff then each arc $A_i$ is closed in $X$.

::: pf-proof
An arc is by definition a space homeomorphic to $[0,1]$, and $[0,1]$ is compact; compactness is a topological property, so each $A_i$ is a compact subspace of $X$. A compact subspace of a Hausdorff space is closed, so each $A_i$ is closed in $X$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #reverse}
If each $A_i$ is closed in $X$ then $X$ is Hausdorff.

::: pf-proof
- Suppose each $A_i$ is closed in $X$.
- Since $X = \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i$ is a finite union of closed sets, the topology of $X$ is coherent with $\{A_1, \dots, A_n\}$: a set $C \subseteq X$ is closed if and only if $C \cap A_i$ is closed in $A_i$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$.
- For each point $x \in X$, $\{x\} \cap A_i$ is either empty or a singleton, hence closed in $A_i$, so $\{x\}$ is closed in $X$ ($X$ is $T_1$).
- Let $x, y \in X$ be distinct points.
- Fix $i$ and a homeomorphism $h_i: [0, 1] \to A_i$, which exists because an arc is a space homeomorphic to $[0, 1]$.
- Let $E_i \subseteq [0, 1]$ be the finite set of points $h_i^{-1}(x)$ if $x \in A_i$, $h_i^{-1}(y)$ if $y \in A_i$, and $h_i^{-1}(z)$ for each end point $z$ of $A_i$ that lies in another arc $A_j$.
- Assign values on $E_i$ by $\psi_i(h_i^{-1}(x)) = 0$, $\psi_i(h_i^{-1}(y)) = 1$, and $\psi_i(t) = 1/2$ for every other point $t$ of $E_i$. The assignments of two arcs agree where the arcs meet: their intersection is at most a common end point $z$, which is an end point of both, and it receives $0$ from both if $z = x$, $1$ from both if $z = y$, and $1/2$ from both otherwise.
- Because $E_i$ is finite, $\psi_i$ extends to a continuous map $\phi_i: [0, 1] \to [0, 1]$, by linear interpolation between consecutive points of $E_i$ and the end values beyond the first and last of them, or by any values when $E_i$ is empty; equivalently Tietze's theorem, Theorem 35.1 , extends it from the finite closed subset $E_i$ of the normal space $[0, 1]$.
- Then $f_i = \phi_i \circ h_i^{-1}: A_i \to [0, 1]$ is continuous, takes the value $0$ at $x$ if $x \in A_i$, the value $1$ at $y$ if $y \in A_i$, and the value $1/2$ at every end point of $A_i$ lying in another arc; and $f_i, f_j$ agree on $A_i \cap A_j$.
- Each $A_i$ is closed in $X$ by hypothesis, so the pasting lemma for two closed sets, Theorem 18.3 , applied to the closed sets $A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{n-1}$ and $A_n$, and then inductively within the first of them, gives a continuous $f: X \to [0, 1]$ whose restriction to $A_i$ is $f_i$ for every $i$; in particular $f(x) = 0$ and $f(y) = 1$.
- The preimages $U = f^{-1}([0, 1/3))$ and $V = f^{-1}((2/3, 1])$ are disjoint open neighborhoods of $x$ and $y$ in $X$.
- Thus $X$ is Hausdorff
:::

::: pf-step
Part (b): Counterexample where $X$ is not Hausdorff.

::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #construction}
There is a space $X$ written as the union of two arcs $A_1$ and $A_2$.

::: pf-proof
Take the line with two origins: its points are the nonzero real numbers and two distinct origins $p,q$. A neighborhood of either origin contains both sufficiently small positive and sufficiently small negative nonzero numbers. Give
$$
X=[-1,0)\cup\{p,q\}\cup(0,1]
$$
the subspace topology it inherits from the line with two origins, and set
$$
A_1=[-1,0)\cup\{p\},\qquad A_2=\{q\}\cup(0,1].
$$
Each $A_i$ has the usual interval topology, so each is an arc
:::

:::


::: pf-step
The space $X = A_1 \cup A_2$ has $A_1 \cap A_2 = \emptyset$, so the intersection condition is vacuously satisfied.

::: pf-proof
The two sets have no common point: a point of $A_1$ is either a negative real or the origin $p$, and a point of $A_2$ is either a positive real or the origin $q$, with $p \ne q$; so the intersection is empty and the hypothesis that each pair of arcs meets in at most a common end point holds vacuously.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
However, every neighborhood of $p$ and every neighborhood of $q$ contain a common punctured interval around $0$. Thus $p$ and $q$ cannot be separated by disjoint open sets.

::: pf-proof
Let $U$ be a neighbourhood of $p$ and $V$ a neighbourhood of $q$ in the subspace topology. Each contains a basic neighbourhood inherited from the line with two origins, so there is $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\{p\} \cup ((-\varepsilon, 0) \cup (0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq U$ and $\delta > 0$ with the corresponding set for $q$ inside $V$; the real number $\min(\varepsilon,\delta)/2$ lies in both subsets of the nonzero reals, hence in $U \cap V$. So no such $U, V$ are disjoint and the space is not Hausdorff.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Also $q \in \overline{A_1} \setminus A_1$, as part (a) predicts.

::: pf-proof
Every neighbourhood of $q$ meets $A_1$ in the points of $(-\varepsilon, 0)$, which are negative reals and so belong to $A_1$, and $q$ itself is in $A_2$ and not in $A_1$; hence $q$ is a limit point of $A_1$ outside it. Part (a) accounts for this: the space is not Hausdorff by the previous step, so some $A_i$ fails to be closed, and this exhibits that failure for $A_1$.
:::
