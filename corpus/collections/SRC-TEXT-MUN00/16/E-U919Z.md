::: {.exercise}

Let $X$ be an ordered set.
If $Y$ is a proper subset of $X$ that is convex in $X$, does it follow that $Y$ is an interval or a ray in $X$?
:::

::: {.solution}

The counterexample is **Dan Whitman, *Topology, Second Edition by James Munkres: Solutions Manual* (30 November 2019), Exercise 16.7** (`tmp/sources/munkres-solutions-whitman.pdf`), who takes $X=\QQ$ and $Y=\theset{x\in\QQ\mid x^{2}<2}$; the elimination of the eight interval and ray forms is written out here in place of his "similar arguments show" for three of them, and one of his clauses is corrected: he says the upper rays contain elements larger than every element of $Y$, which is true only of those with $a<2$ and is not what rules them out, since such a ray is unbounded above in $\QQ$ whatever $a$ is.

The statement was checked against Munkres, *Topology* (2000), §16 Exercise 7, with the definition of convex on printed p. 90 and the four rays and the intervals of an ordered set in §14. Throughout $X=\QQ$ carries the usual order, so that $a<b$ in $X$ means $a<b$ as rationals,
$$
Y\coloneqq\theset{x\in X\mid x^{2}<2},
$$
and an *interval* of $X$ is one of $(a,b)$, $(a,b]$, $[a,b)$, $[a,b]$ with $a<b$ in $X$, while a *ray* of $X$ is one of $(-\infty,b)$, $(-\infty,b]$, $(a,+\infty)$, $[a,+\infty)$ with $a,b\in X$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #proper}

$Y$ is a proper subset of $X$.

::: pf-proof

$2\in X$ and $2^{2}=4>2$, so $2\notin Y$; thus $Y\neq X$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #convex}

$Y$ is convex in $X$.

::: pf-proof

Let $a<b$ lie in $Y$ and let $x\in X$ with $a<x<b$. If $x\geq0$ then $0\leq x<b$ and $b>0$, so $x^{2}<b^{2}<2$; if $x<0$ then $a<x<0$, so $\abs{x}<\abs{a}$ and $x^{2}<a^{2}<2$. In either case $x^{2}<2$, that is $x\in Y$; hence $(a,b)\subset Y$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #bounds}

$Y$ is nonempty and bounded above in $X$.

::: pf-proof

$0\in Y$ because $0^{2}=0<2$. If $y\in Y$ then $y^{2}<2$, so $\abs{y}<\sqrt2<2$ and in particular $y<2$; hence $2$ is an upper bound of $Y$ in $X$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #no-lub}

$Y$ has no least upper bound in $X$.

:::: pf-proof

Suppose $q\in X$ were a least upper bound of $Y$. By step [](#bounds){.pf-ref} the set $Y$ is nonempty and bounded above, so such a $q$ would be an upper bound, and the three steps below exclude every rational $q$.

::: {.pf-step #irrational}

A least upper bound $q$ of $Y$ in $X$ would satisfy $q^{2}\neq2$.

::: pf-proof

For $q\in\QQ$ the equality $q^{2}=2$ would make $q$ a rational square root of $2$, and no such rational exists: if $q=m/n$ in lowest terms then $m^{2}=2n^{2}$ forces $m$ even, say $m=2k$, whence $2k^{2}=n^{2}$ forces $n$ even as well, contradicting the choice of $m,n$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #too-big}

No $q$ with $q^{2}>2$ is a least upper bound of $Y$.

::: pf-proof

Let $q\in\QQ$ with $q^{2}>2$; then $q>0$. By the density of $\QQ$ in $\RR$ there is $q^{\prime}\in\QQ$ with $\sqrt2<q^{\prime}<q$. Every $y\in Y$ satisfies $\abs{y}<\sqrt2$ because $y^{2}<2$, so $y<\sqrt2<q^{\prime}$ and $q^{\prime}$ is an upper bound of $Y$; being smaller than $q$, it shows that $q$ is not the least one.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #too-small}

No $q$ with $q^{2}<2$ is an upper bound of $Y$.

::: pf-proof

Let $q\in\QQ$ with $q^{2}<2$; then $q<\sqrt2$, and by density there is $q^{\prime}\in\QQ$ with $q<q^{\prime}<\sqrt2$. Then $(q^{\prime})^{2}<2$, so $q^{\prime}\in Y$ and $q^{\prime}>q$; hence $q$ is not an upper bound of $Y$ at all.

:::

:::

::::

:::

::: {.pf-step #no-form}

$Y$ is neither an interval nor a ray of $X$.

:::: pf-proof

An interval or a ray of $X$ has one of the eight forms listed in the preamble; each is excluded.

::: {.pf-step #bounded-forms}

If $Y$ is one of $(a,b)$, $(a,b]$, $[a,b)$, $[a,b]$ with $a<b$ in $X$, or one of $(-\infty,b)$, $(-\infty,b]$, then $Y$ has the least upper bound $b$ in $X$.

::: pf-proof

In each case $b$ is an upper bound of the set. Let $u\in X$ with $u<b$; an element of the set strictly greater than $u$ exists: for the two rays take $(u+b)/2$, which is rational, lies strictly between $u$ and $b$, and so belongs to the ray; for the four intervals take $(u+b)/2$ if $u\geq a$, which lies in $(a,b)$ and hence in the set, and any element of $(a,b)$ if $u<a$. So no $u<b$ is an upper bound, and $b$ is the least.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #upper-rays}

If $Y$ is $(a,+\infty)$ or $[a,+\infty)$ with $a\in X$, then $Y$ is unbounded above in $X$.

::: pf-proof

Let $u\in X$ and put $v\coloneqq\max\theset{a,u}+1$, a rational number. Then $v>u$ and $v>a$, so $v$ lies in either ray; hence no rational bounds the ray above.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

$Y$ is none of the eight forms.

::: pf-proof

Were $Y$ one of the six forms of step [](#bounded-forms){.pf-ref}, it would have a least upper bound in $X$, which step [](#no-lub){.pf-ref} forbids; were it one of the two forms of step [](#upper-rays){.pf-ref}, it would be unbounded above in $X$, whereas $2$ is an upper bound of $Y$ by step [](#bounds){.pf-ref}. So $Y$ is neither an interval nor a ray of $X$.

:::

:::

::::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore a proper convex subset of an ordered set need not be an interval or a ray: $Y$ is a counterexample in $X=\QQ$.

::: pf-proof

By steps [](#proper){.pf-ref}, [](#convex){.pf-ref} and [](#no-form){.pf-ref} the set $Y$ is a proper subset of $X$ that is convex in $X$ and is neither an interval nor a ray of $X$. So the answer to the question posed in the exercise is no.

:::
