::: {.exercise}

Let $X$ be the adjunction space formed from the disjoint union of the normal, path-connected space $A$ and the unit ball $B^2$ by means of a continuous map $f: S^1 \to A$.
(See [[E-HYC1G]].) Show that $X$ satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 72.1. Where do you use the fact that $A$ is normal?
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Verify that the adjunction space $X = A \cup_f B^2$ satisfies the three structural hypotheses of Theorem 72.1 for cell attachment, and identify precisely where normality of $A$ is needed.

<1>1. Hypotheses of Theorem 72.1:
    A space $X$ obtained by attaching a 2-cell to a subspace $A$ must satisfy:
    1. $A$ is a closed subspace of $X$, and $X$ is Hausdorff.
    2. $X \setminus A$ is homeomorphic to the open 2-cell $\operatorname{Int} B^2 = \mathbb{B}^2$.
    3. There exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $A$ in $X$ that deformation retracts onto $A$.

<1>2. Verification of Hypothesis 1 ($A$ is closed and $X$ is Hausdorff):
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $\pi: A \sqcup B^2 \to X$ be the quotient projection.
    <2>2. $\pi^{-1}(A) = A \sqcup S^1$, which is closed in $A \sqcup B^2$. By the quotient topology, $A$ is a closed subspace of $X$, and $\pi|_A: A \to \pi(A) \cong A$ is a topological embedding.
    <2>3. **Use of Normality:** To prove $X$ is Hausdorff (and normal):
        - Disjoint closed sets in $A$ can be separated by Urysohn functions because $A$ is normal ($T_4$).
        - Since $B^2$ is compact Hausdorff (hence normal) and the attaching region $S^1 \subset B^2$ is closed, the adjunction space $X = A \cup_f B^2$ of a normal space $A$ along a compact metric space $B^2$ is normal, hence Hausdorff ($T_2$).
        - (If $A$ were not normal, the quotient $A \cup_f B^2$ could fail to be Hausdorff at boundary points of $f(S^1)$).

<1>3. Verification of Hypothesis 2 ($X \setminus A$ is an open 2-disk):
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. The restriction of $\pi$ to the open ball $\operatorname{Int} B^2$ is an injective continuous map onto $X \setminus A$.
    <2>2. Since $\operatorname{Int} B^2$ is saturated under $\pi$ (no points in $\operatorname{Int} B^2$ are identified with other points), $\pi|_{\operatorname{Int} B^2}: \operatorname{Int} B^2 \to X \setminus A$ is a homeomorphism.

<1>4. Verification of Hypothesis 3 (Deformation retract neighborhood $U$):
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $U = X \setminus \{\pi(0)\}$. Then $\pi^{-1}(U) = A \sqcup (B^2 \setminus \{0\})$, which is open in $A \sqcup B^2$, so $U$ is an open neighborhood of $A$ in $X$.
    <2>2. In $B^2 \setminus \{0\}$, define the standard straight-line deformation retraction onto $S^1$:
        $$r_t(x) = \left((1-t) + t \frac{1}{\|x\|}\right) x \quad \text{for } x \in B^2 \setminus \{0\}, \; t \in [0, 1].$$
    <2>3. Define $H_t: A \sqcup (B^2 \setminus \{0\}) \to A \sqcup (B^2 \setminus \{0\})$ by $H_t|_A = \operatorname{id}_A$ and $H_t|_{B^2 \setminus \{0\}} = r_t$.
    <2>4. Since $r_t|_{S^1} = \operatorname{id}_{S^1}$ for all $t$, $H_t$ respects the quotient identifications, inducing a continuous homotopy $\bar{H}_t: U \to U$ with $\bar{H}_0 = \operatorname{id}_U$ and $\bar{H}_1(U) = A$.
    <2>5. Thus $U$ deformation retracts onto $A$.

<1>5. Conclusion:
    $X$ satisfies all hypotheses of Theorem 72.1. Normality of $A$ is used specifically in <1>2 to guarantee that the adjunction space $X$ is Hausdorff. Q.E.D.
:::
