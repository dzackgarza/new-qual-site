::: {.exercise}

Check the details of Examples 1 and 2 of §40.
:::

::: {.solution}
We verify the assertions in Examples 1 and 2.

**Example 1.** Every open set $U\subset X$ is a $G_\delta$ set, since
$$
U=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U.
$$

Now suppose $X$ is first countable and Hausdorff, and fix $x\in X$. Let
$$
B_1,B_2,\ldots
$$
be a countable basis of open neighborhoods at $x$. Replacing $B_n$ by
$$
C_n=B_1\cap\cdots\cap B_n
$$
we may assume $C_{n+1}\subset C_n$. Then
$$
\{x\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}C_n.
$$
Indeed, $x$ belongs to every $C_n$. If $y\ne x$, Hausdorffness gives a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ missing $y$; some basis element $B_n\subset U$, hence $y\notin C_n$. Thus every singleton is $G_\delta$.

Finally, the top point $\Omega$ of $\overline S_\Omega=[0,\Omega]$ is not a $G_\delta$ set. If
$$
\{\Omega\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_n
$$
with each $U_n$ open and containing $\Omega$, then for each $n$ there is $\alpha_n<\Omega$ such that
$$
(\alpha_n,\Omega]\subset U_n.
$$
Because a countable supremum of countable ordinals is countable,
$$
\alpha=\sup_n\alpha_n<\Omega.
$$
Any $\beta$ with $\alpha<\beta<\Omega$ then belongs to every $U_n$, contradicting that their intersection is $\{\Omega\}$.

**Example 2.** The empty set is itself open, hence $G_\delta$. For a nonempty closed set $A$ in a metric space $(X,d)$, write
$$
U(A,\varepsilon)=\{x:d(x,A)<\varepsilon\}.
$$
Each $U(A,1/n)$ is open. Clearly $A\subset U(A,1/n)$ for every $n$. Conversely, if $x\notin A$, then $A$ is closed, so $d(x,A)>0$: otherwise $d(x,A)=0$ would imply $x\in\overline A=A$. Choose $n$ with $1/n<d(x,A)$. Then $x\notin U(A,1/n)$. Hence
$$
A=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U(A,1/n),
$$
so every closed subset of a metric space is $G_\delta$.
:::
