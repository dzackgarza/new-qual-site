::: {.exercise}

A collection $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $X$ is said to be locally discrete if each point of $X$ has a neighborhood that intersects at most one element of $\mathcal{A}$.
A collection $\mathcal{B}$ is countably locally discrete (or "$\sigma$-locally discrete") if it equals a countable union of locally discrete collections.
Prove the following.

Theorem (Bing metrization theorem).
A space $X$ is metrizable if and only if it is regular and has a basis that is countably locally discrete.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Prove the Bing Metrization Theorem: a topological space $X$ is metrizable if and only if $X$ is regular and possesses a countably locally discrete ($\sigma$-locally discrete) basis.

<1>1. Sufficiency ($\impliedby$): Regular spaces with $\sigma$-locally discrete bases are metrizable.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. A collection $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $X$ is locally finite if every point of $X$ has an open neighborhood intersecting only finitely many elements of $\mathcal{A}$.
    <2>2. If $\mathcal{A}$ is locally discrete, every point has a neighborhood intersecting at most one element of $\mathcal{A}$, so $\mathcal{A}$ is locally finite.
    <2>3. If $\mathcal{B} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \mathcal{B}_n$ is a countably locally discrete basis, each $\mathcal{B}_n$ is locally discrete, hence locally finite. Thus $\mathcal{B}$ is a countably locally finite ($\sigma$-locally finite) basis.
    <2>4. By the Nagata-Smirnov Metrization Theorem, any regular space with a countably locally finite basis is metrizable.
    <2>5. Therefore $X$ is metrizable.

<1>2. Necessity ($\implies$): Metrizable spaces possess a $\sigma$-locally discrete basis.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space. Metric spaces are $T_4$, hence regular.
    <2>2. For each $n \in \mathbb{Z}_+$, let $\mathcal{U}_n = \{B_d(x, \frac{1}{n}) : x \in X\}$ be the open cover of $X$ by balls of radius $\frac{1}{n}$.
    <2>3. Choose a well-ordering $<$ of $X$. For each $x \in X$, define:
        $$E_n(x) = B_d\left(x, \frac{1}{3n}\right) \setminus \bigcup_{y < x} B_d\left(y, \frac{1}{n}\right).$$
    <2>4. The collection $\mathcal{E}_n = \{E_n(x) : x \in X\}$ is discrete: for any $p \in X$, let $x_0$ be the first element in the well-ordering such that $p \in B_d(x_0, \frac{1}{n})$. Then the open ball $B_d(p, \frac{1}{3n})$ cannot intersect $E_n(z)$ for any $z > x_0$ (since $E_n(z)$ excludes $B_d(x_0, \frac{1}{n})$), and contains at most one point $z \le x_0$ since the centers in $E_n$ are $\frac{1}{3n}$-separated.
    <2>5. Expanding each set to an open set: define $V_n(x) = \bigcup_{p \in E_n(x)} B_d(p, \frac{1}{6n})$. Then $\mathcal{B}_n = \{V_n(x) : x \in X\}$ is a locally discrete collection of open sets, and $\operatorname{diam}(V_n(x)) \le \frac{1}{n}$.
    <2>6. The union $\mathcal{B} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \mathcal{B}_n$ is a countably locally discrete collection of open sets.
    <2>7. Basis property: For any open set $U \subseteq X$ and point $x \in U$, choose $n$ such that $B_d(x, \frac{1}{n}) \subseteq U$. Since $\bigcup_{x} E_n(x) = X$, there exists $y \in X$ such that $x \in V_n(y)$. Since $\operatorname{diam}(V_n(y)) \le \frac{1}{n}$, $x \in V_n(y) \subseteq B_d(x, \frac{1}{n}) \subseteq U$.
    <2>8. Thus $\mathcal{B}$ is a $\sigma$-locally discrete basis for $X$. Q.E.D.
:::
