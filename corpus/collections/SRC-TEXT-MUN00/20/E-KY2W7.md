::: {.exercise}

Show that $\RR \times \RR$ in the dictionary order topology is metrizable.
:::

::: {.solution}

The reduction is **"positron0802", *Solutions to Topology, Chapter 2* (1 January 2021), Exercise 20.2, PDF p. 17** (`tmp/sources/munkres-solutions-positron-ch2.pdf`), which identifies the dictionary order topology of $\RR\times\RR$ with $\RR_{d}\times\RR$ by Exercise 16.9 and then invokes the product metric; the product metric and its two ball inclusions are written out here. One citation is repaired: the earlier prose attributed the discrete metric and the discreteness of its topology to "Theorem 20.1/Example 1 of §20", and the book states neither there, so the metric is now defined on the card and its metric topology computed.

The statement was checked against Munkres, *Topology* (2000), §20 Exercise 2, with Exercise 16.9 on printed p. 92 and the definition of the metric topology in §20. Throughout, $\RR_{d}$ is $\RR$ with the discrete topology, $\RR\times\RR$ carries the dictionary order and its order topology (Example 11 of §3), $d(a,b)\coloneqq\abs{a-b}$ is the usual metric of $\RR$, and for metric spaces $(X,d)$ and $(Y,d^{\prime})$ the *product topology* on $X\times Y$ is the one generated by the sets $U\times V$ with $U$ open in $X$ and $V$ open in $Y$ (§15).

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #identification}

The dictionary order topology on $\RR\times\RR$ is the product topology $\RR_{d}\times\RR$.

::: pf-proof

This is Exercise 16.9: the dictionary order topology on $\RR\times\RR$ is the same as the product topology $\RR_{d}\times\RR$, where $\RR_{d}$ denotes $\RR$ in the discrete topology.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #discrete}

$\RR_{d}$ is metrizable.

::: pf-proof

Put $\delta(a,b)\coloneqq 0$ if $a=b$ and $\delta(a,b)\coloneqq 1$ otherwise. This is a metric: it is nonnegative, vanishes exactly on the diagonal, is symmetric, and the triangle inequality is immediate when the three points are distinct and otherwise reduces to $1\leq0+1$ or $0\leq0$. In its metric topology every one-point set is open, because $B_{\delta}(a,1)=\theset{a}$; hence every subset of $\RR$ is open, since it is a union of its points, and the metric topology is the discrete topology.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #usual}

$\RR$ is metrizable.

::: pf-proof

$d(a,b)=\abs{a-b}$ is a metric on $\RR$ whose metric topology is the usual topology, the open $\varepsilon$-balls about a point being the intervals $(a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon)$ (§20).

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #product}

For metric spaces $(X,d)$ and $(Y,d^{\prime})$, the maximum metric $\rho$ induces the product topology on $X\times Y$.

:::: pf-proof

Let $\rho(x\times y,w\times z)\coloneqq\max\theset{d(x,w),\ d^{\prime}(y,z)}$.

::: {.pf-step #metric}

$\rho$ is a metric on $X\times Y$.

::: pf-proof

$\rho$ is nonnegative, and $\rho(x\times y,w\times z)=0$ exactly when $d(x,w)=0$ and $d^{\prime}(y,z)=0$, that is exactly when $x=w$ and $y=z$, since $d$ and $d^{\prime}$ are metrics. It is symmetric because $d$ and $d^{\prime}$ are. For the triangle inequality, let $u\times v\in X\times Y$ be a third point: both $d(x,u)\leq d(x,w)+d(w,u)\leq\rho(x\times y,w\times z)+\rho(w\times z,u\times v)$ and $d^{\prime}(y,v)\leq d^{\prime}(y,z)+d^{\prime}(z,v)\leq\rho(x\times y,w\times z)+\rho(w\times z,u\times v)$, so their maximum, which is $\rho(x\times y,u\times v)$, obeys the same bound.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #balls-open}

Every open ball for $\rho$ is open in the product topology.

::: pf-proof

For a point $x\times y$ and $\delta>0$ one has $B_{\rho}(x\times y,\delta)=B_{d}(x,\delta)\times B_{d^{\prime}}(y,\delta)$: the condition $\max\theset{d(x,x^{\prime}),d^{\prime}(y,y^{\prime})}<\delta$ is exactly the conjunction of $d(x,x^{\prime})<\delta$ and $d^{\prime}(y,y^{\prime})<\delta$. The right-hand side is a product of open sets, hence open in the product topology, and a union of such balls is open there too.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #balls-inside}

Every point of every product-basic open set has a $\rho$-ball about it inside that set.

::: pf-proof

Let $U\times V$ be a product of open sets and let $x^{\prime}\times y^{\prime}\in U\times V$. Choose $\varepsilon_{1}>0$ and $\varepsilon_{2}>0$ with $B_{d}(x^{\prime},\varepsilon_{1})\subset U$ and $B_{d^{\prime}}(y^{\prime},\varepsilon_{2})\subset V$, which is possible because $U$ and $V$ are open in their metric topologies. Put $r\coloneqq\min\theset{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}>0$; then $B_{\rho}(x^{\prime}\times y^{\prime},r)\subset B_{d}(x^{\prime},\varepsilon_{1})\times B_{d^{\prime}}(y^{\prime},\varepsilon_{2})\subset U\times V$ by the identity proved in step [](#balls-open){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #coincide}

The $\rho$-metric topology on $X\times Y$ is the product topology.

::: pf-proof

Steps [](#balls-open){.pf-ref} and [](#balls-inside){.pf-ref} say that each topology has a basis contained in the other: every $\rho$-ball is open in the product topology, and about every point of every product-basic set there is a $\rho$-ball inside it. Two topologies with that relation coincide, since each is the union of the members of its basis and each basic set is open in the other topology.

:::

:::

::::

:::

::: pf-step

Therefore $\RR\times\RR$ in the dictionary order topology is metrizable.

::: pf-proof

By step [](#identification){.pf-ref} that topology is the product topology of $\RR_{d}$ and $\RR$, which are metrizable by steps [](#discrete){.pf-ref} and [](#usual){.pf-ref}; the maximum metric of step [](#product){.pf-ref} applied to their two metrics induces the product topology, so it is a metric whose metric topology is the dictionary order topology. A space is metrizable when some metric induces its topology, so the dictionary order plane is metrizable.

:::
