::: {.exercise}

If $X$ and $Y$ are spaces, define

$$
e: X \times \mathcal{C}(X, Y) \to Y
$$

by the equation $e(x, f) = f(x)$; the map $e$ is called the evaluation map.
Show that if $d$ is a metric for $Y$ and $\mathcal{C}(X, Y)$ has the corresponding uniform topology, then $e$ is continuous.
We shall generalize this result in §46.
:::

::: {.solution}
The argument is the card's own: no retained solution set covers this exercise, as the search record names.

The uniform topology on $\mathcal{C}(X, Y)$ is induced by the uniform metric $\bar{\rho}(f, g) = \sup_{x \in X} \bar{d}(f(x), g(x))$, where $\bar{d} = \min(d, 1)$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}
Reduction:
Let $(x_0, f_0) \in X \times \mathcal{C}(X, Y)$ and let $\varepsilon > 0$. We may assume without loss of generality that $\varepsilon < 1$.
It suffices to find an open neighborhood $U \subseteq X$ of $x_0$ and an open neighborhood $V \subseteq \mathcal{C}(X, Y)$ of $f_0$ such that $e(U \times V) \subseteq B_d(f_0(x_0), \varepsilon)$.

::: pf-proof
Continuity of $e$ at $(x_0, f_0)$ means that for every neighbourhood of $e(x_0,f_0) = f_0(x_0)$ there is a neighbourhood of $(x_0,f_0)$ mapped into it, and the $d$-balls $B_d(f_0(x_0), \varepsilon)$ form a neighbourhood base at that point, so it is enough to reach each of them; and if the claim holds for every $0 < \varepsilon < 1$ then it holds for every $\varepsilon > 0$, because for a given $\varepsilon$ one applies the small case to $\min(\varepsilon, 1)/2$ and uses that a smaller ball is contained in the larger one. So the two restrictions lose nothing.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #neighbourhoods}
There are an open neighbourhood $U$ of $x_0$ and an open neighbourhood $V$ of $f_0$ such that $d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)) < \varepsilon/2$ for $x \in U$ and $d(f(x), f_0(x)) < \varepsilon/2$ for $f \in V$ and all $x$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Since $f_0: X \to Y$ is continuous at $x_0$, there exists an open neighborhood $U \subseteq X$ of $x_0$ such that:
$$d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \quad \text{for all } x \in U.$$

::: pf-proof
Continuity of $f_0$ at $x_0$ in the metric space $(Y,d)$ says that for the positive number $\varepsilon/2$ there is a neighbourhood $U$ of $x_0$ with $d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)) < \varepsilon/2$ for every $x \in U$; take $U$ open inside that neighbourhood.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
In the uniform metric space $\mathcal{C}(X, Y)$, define the open ball $V = B_{\bar{\rho}}(f_0, \frac{\varepsilon}{2})$.

::: pf-proof
The uniform metric $\bar\rho$ is a metric on $\mathcal C(X,Y)$ and the uniform topology is the one it induces, so its open balls form a base; $V$ is such a ball, with centre $f_0$ and positive radius $\varepsilon/2$, hence is open and contains $f_0$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
For any $f \in V$, the uniform bound holds:
$$\bar{\rho}(f, f_0) = \sup_{x \in X} \bar{d}(f(x), f_0(x)) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

::: pf-proof
Membership $f \in V$ means $\bar\rho(f, f_0) < \varepsilon/2$, and $\bar\rho(f, g)$ is defined as the supremum over $x \in X$ of $\bar d(f(x), g(x))$; a supremum is an upper bound for the set it is taken over, so each term is at most it.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since $\frac{\varepsilon}{2} < 1$, $\bar{d}(f(x), f_0(x)) = d(f(x), f_0(x))$, which implies:
$$d(f(x), f_0(x)) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \quad \text{for all } x \in X.$$

::: pf-proof
The truncated metric is $\bar d = \min(d, 1)$, so $\bar d(f(x), f_0(x)) = 1$ whenever $d(f(x),f_0(x)) \ge 1$, and that value is not below $\varepsilon/2$ because $\varepsilon/2 < 1$; since the supremum is less than $\varepsilon/2$, no such $x$ exists and $\bar d(f(x),f_0(x)) = d(f(x),f_0(x))$ for every $x$. The bound on the supremum then gives $d(f(x), f_0(x)) < \varepsilon/2$ for every $x \in X$.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}
$e(U \times V) \subseteq B_d(f_0(x_0), \varepsilon)$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Let $(x, f) \in U \times V$.

::: pf-proof
The point $(x,f)$ is an arbitrary element of $U \times V$: nothing is assumed about it beyond membership, and $U \times V$ is open in the product topology as a product of open sets.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By the triangle inequality in the metric space $(Y, d)$:
$$d(e(x, f), e(x_0, f_0)) = d(f(x), f_0(x_0)) \le d(f(x), f_0(x)) + d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)).$$

::: pf-proof
The metric $d$ satisfies the triangle inequality $d(a, c) \le d(a, b) + d(b, c)$ for all $a,b,c \in Y$; applying it with $a = f(x)$, $b = f_0(x)$ and $c = f_0(x_0)$ gives the displayed bound, and $e(x,f) = f(x)$, $e(x_0,f_0) = f_0(x_0)$ by the definition of $e$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since $f \in V$, $d(f(x), f_0(x)) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.

::: pf-proof
The estimate $d(f(x), f_0(x)) < \varepsilon/2$ holds for every $x \in X$, hence for this $x$, and is the conclusion of the last step of the construction.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since $x \in U$, $d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.

::: pf-proof
The estimate $d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)) < \varepsilon/2$ holds for every $x \in U$ by the choice of $U$, and $x \in U$ by the choice of the point.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Summing these bounds gives:
$$d(e(x, f), e(x_0, f_0)) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.$$

::: pf-proof
Adding the two strict inequalities gives $d(f(x), f_0(x)) + d(f_0(x), f_0(x_0)) < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon$, and the triangle inequality of the earlier step bounds $d(e(x,f), e(x_0,f_0))$ by that sum.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Thus $e(U \times V) \subseteq B_d(f_0(x_0), \varepsilon)$.

::: pf-proof
The point $(x,f)$ was arbitrary in $U \times V$, and every such point satisfies $e(x,f) \in B_d(f_0(x_0), \varepsilon)$ by the strict inequality just proved, which is the inclusion claimed; so the required pair of neighbourhoods exists at this point.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
The evaluation map $e \colon X \times \mathcal{C}(X,Y) \to Y$ is continuous.

::: pf-proof
The point chosen at the outset was an arbitrary point of $X \times \mathcal{C}(X,Y)$, and steps [](#s1){.pf-ref} and [](#s3){.pf-ref} produce, at that point and for every $\varepsilon > 0$, a neighbourhood of it mapped into the ball of radius $\varepsilon$ about its image. That is continuity at that point; a map is continuous when it is continuous at every point of its domain.
:::
