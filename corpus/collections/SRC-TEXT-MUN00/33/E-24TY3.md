::: {.exercise}

Give a direct proof of the Urysohn lemma for a metric space $(X, d)$ by setting

$$
f(x) = \frac{d(x, A)}{d(x, A) + d(x, B)}
$$

for disjoint nonempty closed sets $A$ and $B$.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Prove the Urysohn Lemma for metric spaces directly using distance-to-a-set functions.

<1>1. Properties of the distance function $d(x, S)$:
    For any non-empty subset $S \subseteq X$, the function $d_S: X \to [0, \infty)$ defined by $d_S(x) = d(x, S) = \inf_{s \in S} d(x, s)$ is 1-Lipschitz continuous, and $d(x, S) = 0 \iff x \in \overline{S}$.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. For any $x, y \in X$ and $s \in S$, $d(x, s) \le d(x, y) + d(y, s)$.
    <2>2. Taking the infimum over $s \in S$ on both sides yields $d(x, S) \le d(x, y) + d(y, S) \implies d(x, S) - d(y, S) \le d(x, y)$.
    <2>3. Reversing the roles of $x$ and $y$ gives $|d(x, S) - d(y, S)| \le d(x, y)$, proving 1-Lipschitz continuity.
    <2>4. By definition of closure in metric spaces, $x \in \overline{S} \iff \forall \varepsilon > 0, B_d(x, \varepsilon) \cap S \neq \emptyset \iff \inf_{s \in S} d(x, s) = 0$.

<1>2. Well-definedness and bounds of $f(x)$:
    $f(x) = \frac{d(x, A)}{d(x, A) + d(x, B)}$ is well-defined and maps $X$ into $[0, 1]$.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Since $A$ and $B$ are closed and disjoint ($A \cap B = \emptyset$), no point $x \in X$ belongs to both $A$ and $B$.
    <2>2. By <1>1, $d(x, A) = 0 \iff x \in A$ and $d(x, B) = 0 \iff x \in B$.
    <2>3. Thus for every $x \in X$, at least one of $d(x, A)$ or $d(x, B)$ is strictly positive, which implies:
        $$d(x, A) + d(x, B) > 0.$$
    <2>4. The denominator is strictly positive everywhere on $X$, so $f$ is well-defined.
    <2>5. Since $0 \le d(x, A) \le d(x, A) + d(x, B)$, we have $0 \le f(x) \le 1$ for all $x \in X$.

<1>3. Continuity of $f$:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. The functions $x \mapsto d(x, A)$ and $x \mapsto d(x, B)$ are continuous by <1>1.
    <2>2. The sum $x \mapsto d(x, A) + d(x, B)$ is continuous and non-vanishing.
    <2>3. As a quotient of continuous functions with a non-vanishing denominator, $f: X \to [0, 1]$ is continuous.

<1>4. Separation values on $A$ and $B$:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. If $x \in A$, then $d(x, A) = 0$ and $d(x, B) > 0$, so:
        $$f(x) = \frac{0}{0 + d(x, B)} = 0.$$
    <2>2. If $x \in B$, then $d(x, B) = 0$ and $d(x, A) > 0$, so:
        $$f(x) = \frac{d(x, A)}{d(x, A) + 0} = 1.$$

<1>5. Conclusion:
    $f: X \to [0, 1]$ is a continuous function satisfying $f(A) = \{0\}$ and $f(B) = \{1\}$, establishing the Urysohn lemma for $(X, d)$. Q.E.D.
:::
