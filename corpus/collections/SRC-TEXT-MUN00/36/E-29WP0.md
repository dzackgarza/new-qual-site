::: {.exercise}

The Hausdorff condition is an essential part of the definition of a manifold; it is not implied by the other parts of the definition.
Consider the following space.
Let $X$ be the union of the set $\RR - \theset{0}$ and the two-point set $\theset{p, q}$.
Topologize $X$ by taking as basis the collection of all open intervals in $\RR$ that do not contain 0, along with all sets of the form $(-a, 0) \cup \theset{p} \cup (0, a)$ and all sets of the form $(-a, 0) \cup \theset{q} \cup (0, a)$, for $a > 0$.
The space $X$ is called the line with two origins.

(a) Check that this is a basis for a topology.

(b) Show that each of the spaces $X - \theset{p}$ and $X - \theset{q}$ is homeomorphic to $\RR$.

(c) Show that $X$ satisfies the $T_1$ axiom, but is not Hausdorff.

(d) Show that $X$ satisfies all the conditions for a 1-manifold except for the Hausdorff condition.
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step
Part (a): Basis verification.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Covering: For any $x \in \RR \setminus \{0\}$, $x \in (x - \frac{\abs{x}}{2}, x + \frac{\abs{x}}{2}) \subset \RR \setminus \{0\}$. For $p$ and $q$, $p \in (-1, 0) \cup \{p\} \cup (0, 1)$ and $q \in (-1, 0) \cup \{q\} \cup (0, 1)$.

::: pf-proof
The interval displayed contains $x$ and avoids $0$, and the two sets displayed contain $p$ and $q$ respectively.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Intersections:
- The intersection of two intervals in $\RR \setminus \{0\}$ is an interval in $\RR \setminus \{0\}$ or empty.
- The intersection of an interval in $\RR \setminus \{0\}$ with a set $U_a(p) = (-a, 0) \cup \{p\} \cup (0, a)$ is open in $\RR \setminus \{0\}$.
- $U_a(p) \cap U_b(p) = U_{\min\{a, b\}}(p)$, and similarly $U_a(q) \cap U_b(q) = U_{\min\{a, b\}}(q)$.
- $U_a(p) \cap U_b(q) = (-\min\{a, b\}, 0) \cup (0, \min\{a, b\})$, which is a union of two basic open intervals in $\RR \setminus \{0\}$.

::: pf-proof
Each bullet checks one kind of pair: the intersection of two basic sets is again a basic set, or a union of them, or empty.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Hence the collection forms a basis for a topology on $X$.

::: pf-proof
A family that covers the space and has the property that the intersection of two members is a union of members is a basis for a topology.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #part-b}
Part (b): Homeomorphisms with $\RR$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Define $f: X \setminus \{q\} \to \RR$ by $f(x) = x$ for $x \in \RR \setminus \{0\}$ and $f(p) = 0$.

::: pf-proof
This is a definition: the identity off the added points, with $p$ sent to the missing origin.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
$f$ is a bijection.

::: pf-proof
It is injective because the identity is injective and $p$ is the only point over $0$, and surjective because every real is the image of itself or of $p$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
$f$ maps basic intervals $(a, b) \subset \RR \setminus \{0\}$ to the identical intervals in $\RR$, and maps $U_a(p)$ to $(-a, a)$.

::: pf-proof
On $\RR\setminus\{0\}$ the map is the identity, and $U_a(p)$ consists of $(-a,0)$, $(0,a)$ and $p$, whose images are $(-a,0)$, $(0,a)$ and $0$, with union $(-a,a)$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Since open intervals not containing $0$ together with the symmetric intervals $(-a, a)$ generate the standard Euclidean topology on $\RR$, $f$ is a homeomorphism.

::: pf-proof
A bijection carrying a basis to a basis is a homeomorphism; the intervals avoiding $0$ together with the symmetric intervals about $0$ form a basis for the usual topology of $\RR$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
By the identical argument with $g(x) = x$ for $x \neq 0$ and $g(q) = 0$, $X \setminus \{p\} \cong \RR$.

::: pf-proof
The same map with the roles of $p$ and $q$ exchanged.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
Part (c): $X$ is $T_1$ but not Hausdorff.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
$T_1$ axiom: If $x\in\RR\setminus\{0\}$, each other real point has an interval avoiding $x$, while $p$ and $q$ have neighborhoods $U_a(p)$ and $U_a(q)$ avoiding $x$ when $a<\abs{x}$. Thus $X\setminus\{x\}$ is open. Every basic interval avoids $p$, and every $U_a(q)$ avoids $p$, so $X\setminus\{p\}$ is open; symmetrically $X\setminus\{q\}$ is open. Hence all singletons are closed.

::: pf-proof
For each point $x$ the set $X\setminus\{x\}$ is open, which is the $T_1$ condition; the cases of a real point, of $p$ and of $q$ are checked in turn.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Not Hausdorff: Let $U$ be any open neighborhood of $p$ and $V$ any open neighborhood of $q$.

::: pf-proof
This is arbitrary data of the separation problem.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
$U$ contains a basic neighborhood $U_a(p)$ and $V$ contains a basic neighborhood $U_b(q)$ for some $a, b > 0$.

::: pf-proof
A neighbourhood of $p$ contains a basic set containing $p$, and the basic sets containing $p$ are the $U_a(p)$; likewise for $q$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The intersection $U \cap V \supseteq U_a(p) \cap U_b(q) = (-\min\{a, b\}, 0) \cup (0, \min\{a, b\}) \neq \emptyset$.

::: pf-proof
The two basic sets share the intervals $(-\min\{a,b\},0)$ and $(0,\min\{a,b\})$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Thus $p$ and $q$ cannot be separated by disjoint open sets, so $X$ is not Hausdorff.

::: pf-proof
Every neighbourhood of $p$ meets every neighbourhood of $q$, so no disjoint pair separates them.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step
Part (d): $X$ is a non-Hausdorff 1-manifold.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step
Locally 1-Euclidean: By step [](#part-b){.pf-ref}, every point $x \in X \setminus \{q\}$ has the open neighborhood $X \setminus \{q\} \cong \RR$, and $q$ has the open neighborhood $X \setminus \{p\} \cong \RR$. Thus every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to $\RR^1$.

::: pf-proof
Each point lies in one of the two open sets of part (b), each homeomorphic to $\RR$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Second-countable: The countable collection of intervals $(a, b)$ with $a, b \in \QQ$ and $0 \notin [a, b]$, together with $U_{1/n}(p)$ and $U_{1/n}(q)$ for $n \in \ZZ_+$, forms a countable basis for $X$.

::: pf-proof
Every basic interval avoiding $0$ is a union of rational intervals avoiding $0$, and every $U_a$ contains some $U_{1/n}$; so the countable family is a basis.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
Therefore $X$ is locally 1-Euclidean and second-countable, satisfying all axioms of a 1-manifold except the Hausdorff condition.

::: pf-proof
Local Euclideanity and second countability are what remains of the definition of a $1$-manifold once the Hausdorff condition is dropped.
:::
