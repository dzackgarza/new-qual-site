::: {.exercise}

Consider the seventeen properties listed in Exercise 1 of the Supplementary Exercises (Review of the Basics).

Which of these properties are preserved under finite products?
Countable products?
Arbitrary products?
:::

::: {.solution}
The columns use the seventeen properties in Supplementary Exercise 1. “Yes” means that every product of spaces with that property, over an index set of that size, has it.

| Number | Property | Finite | Countable | Arbitrary |
| --- | --- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | Connected | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 2 | Path connected | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 3 | Locally connected | Yes | No | No |
| 4 | Locally path connected | Yes | No | No |
| 5 | Compact | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 6 | Limit point compact | No | No | No |
| 7 | Locally compact Hausdorff | Yes | No | No |
| 8 | Hausdorff | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 9 | Regular | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 10 | Completely regular | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 11 | Normal | No | No | No |
| 12 | First countable | Yes | Yes | No |
| 13 | Second countable | Yes | Yes | No |
| 14 | Lindelöf | No | No | No |
| 15 | Countable dense subset | Yes | Yes | No |
| 16 | Locally metrizable | Yes | No | No |
| 17 | Metrizable | Yes | Yes | No |

Two conventions are used throughout. A countably infinite index set is an arbitrary one, so a No in the countable column is already a No in the arbitrary column. To carry a No from the finite column into the other two, pad the finite family with one-point factors: a one-point space has all seventeen properties (it is compact, Hausdorff and metrizable), and the product of a family with a one-point factor is homeomorphic to the product of the rest. Every entry below is decided by a numbered result of the book.

**1. Connected: Yes in all three columns.** For finitely many factors this is Theorem 23.6. For an arbitrary index set it is Exercise 23.10, whose three steps are: for a fixed base point $\mathbf a$, the set of points differing from $\mathbf a$ in coordinates lying in a fixed finite set $K$ is connected (Theorems 23.3 and 23.6); the union $Y$ over all finite $K$ is connected (Theorem 23.3 again, all the pieces meeting at $\mathbf a$); and $X = \bar Y$, so $X$ is connected by Theorem 23.4. The product topology is what is meant here: in the box topology $\RR^\omega$ is already disconnected (Example 6 of §23).

**2. Path connected: Yes in all three columns.** Given points $(x_\alpha)$ and $(y_\alpha)$ of $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$, choose for each $\alpha$ a path $f_\alpha$ in $X_\alpha$ from $x_\alpha$ to $y_\alpha$, and put $f(t) = (f_\alpha(t))$. Each coordinate function $\pi_\alpha \circ f = f_\alpha$ is continuous, so $f$ is continuous by Theorem 19.6, and $f(0) = (x_\alpha)$, $f(1) = (y_\alpha)$. Theorem 19.6 puts no restriction on the number of factors, so one argument covers all three columns. Path connectedness is not lost in passing to uncountably many factors; what is lost there is first countability (entry 12).

**3. Locally connected: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.** For finitely many factors, connected neighbourhoods $V_\alpha$ of the coordinates, chosen inside prescribed open sets, give a neighbourhood $\prod V_\alpha$ of the point which is connected by Theorem 23.6, and such neighbourhoods form a basis. For the negative entries use the cube $\{0,1\}^\omega$, a product of discrete, hence locally connected, spaces. Two distinct points of the cube differ at some coordinate, and the cylinder fixing that coordinate is both open and closed, so the components of the cube are its points; if the cube were locally connected, Theorem 25.3 would make each component of the open set $\{0,1\}^\omega$ open, that is, the cube would be discrete, which it is not. The countable failure covers the arbitrary column by the first convention.

**4. Locally path connected: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.** The positive part is the same argument with path-connected neighbourhoods. For the negative part, a path in $\{0,1\}^\omega$ has connected image (Theorem 23.5), hence constant, so the path components are again single points and Theorem 25.4 would make them open. Alternatively, a locally path connected space is locally connected, and entry 3 already fails.

**5. Compact: Yes in all three columns.** Theorem 26.7 for finitely many factors, the Tychonoff theorem, Theorem 37.3, for countable and arbitrary ones. The instruction of the exercise set to assume the Tychonoff theorem is what this row needs, and it is printed at Exercise 1.

**6. Limit point compact: No in all three columns.** Munkres records after Exercise 28.3 (printed p. 181): “We comment that it is not in general true that the product of two limit point compact spaces is limit point compact, even if the Hausdorff condition is assumed. But the examples are fairly sophisticated. See [S-S], Example 112.” That remark is the whole of what this entry rests on, and it is what is cited here: it settles the finite column, and padding a finite family with one-point factors settles the countable and arbitrary columns. The example itself lives in Steen and Seebach's *Counterexamples in Topology*, which is not in this checkout and was not read, so no step below depends on its content. The compact factors of entries 1 and 5 are of no help: compactness implies limit point compactness (Theorem 28.1), so only a limit point compact space that is not compact can be a witness, and for a countable product the witnesses available from this chapter, such as $S_\Omega$, are useless — every countable subset of $S_\Omega$ lies in a compact initial segment, so in a product of copies of $S_\Omega$ a countable subset is bounded in each coordinate and its closure is a product of compact initial segments, compact by Tychonoff; such a product is countably compact and hence limit point compact by Exercise 28.4.

**7. Locally compact Hausdorff: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.** For finitely many factors, take compact neighbourhoods $C_\alpha$ of the coordinates; then $\prod C_\alpha$ is compact (Theorem 26.7) and contains the neighbourhood $\prod \operatorname{int} C_\alpha$ of the point, and the product is Hausdorff by Theorem 31.2(a). This is Exercise 29.2(b). For the negative entries, Example 2 of §29 (printed p. 182) shows that no basis element of $\RR^\omega$ lies in a compact subspace, since its closure is a product of compact intervals and copies of $\RR$; and Exercise 29.2(a) gives the general obstruction, that in a locally compact product all but finitely many factors are compact.

**8. Hausdorff: Yes in all three columns.** Theorem 31.2(a): a product of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff, for any index set.

**9. Regular: Yes in all three columns.** Theorem 31.2(b): a product of regular spaces is regular, for any index set.

**10. Completely regular: Yes in all three columns.** Theorem 33.2: a product of completely regular spaces is completely regular, for any index set.

**11. Normal: No in all three columns.** The finite column is settled by the Sorgenfrey plane $\RR_\ell^2$, a product of two normal spaces (Example 2 of §31) which is not normal (Example 3 of §31); $S_\Omega \times \bar S_\Omega$ is a second witness (Example 2 of §32), a product of two normal spaces, normal by Theorem 32.4, that is not normal. Padding with one-point factors gives the other two columns. No product theorem exists to appeal to: the book says only that normality passes to closed subspaces and that an uncountable product of normal spaces need not be normal (Example 1 of §32, Exercise 32.9).

**12. First countable: Yes for finitely many and for countably many factors, No otherwise.** Theorem 30.2. For an uncountable index set $J$, no countable family of neighbourhoods of a point of $\{0,1\}^J$ is a local base: a basic neighbourhood is determined by a finite set of coordinates, countably many basic neighbourhoods involve only countably many coordinates in all, and for a coordinate outside that countable set the cylinder fixing it is a neighbourhood containing none of them. The same diagonal argument applies to $\RR^J$, and Example 2 of §21 draws the metrizability consequence for $\RR^J$ from the sequence lemma.

**13. Second countable: Yes for finitely many and for countably many factors, No otherwise.** Theorem 30.2 for the positive entries; for the negative one, second countability implies first countability, and entry 12 fails.

**14. Lindelöf: No in all three columns.** The finite column is settled by Example 4 of §30: $\RR_\ell$ is Lindelöf (Example 3 of §30) and $\RR_\ell \times \RR_\ell$ is not. Padding gives the other two. The compact and metrizable entries of other rows do not transfer: the two factors here are Lindelöf, and their product is not.

**15. Countable dense subset: Yes for finitely many and for countably many factors, No otherwise.** If $D_\alpha$ is a countable dense subset of $X_\alpha$, then $\prod D_\alpha$ is countable and its closure is $\prod \bar D_\alpha = \prod X_\alpha$ by Theorem 19.5, so the product is separable; this is Exercise 30.10 for the countable case, and the finite case is that statement padded with one-point factors. For an arbitrary index set the positive half is the Hewitt–Marczewski–Pondiczery theorem, which Engelking states in general form as Theorem 2.3.15: if $d(X_s)\le m\ge\aleph_0$ for every $s\in S$ and $\cardinality{S}\le 2^m$, then $d(\prod_{s\in S}X_s)\le m$ (Ryszard Engelking, *General Topology*, revised and completed edition, Heldermann, 1989, §2.3 Cartesian products, printed p. 81, read from the page image, the volume being a scan, with $d$ the least cardinality of a dense subset). The case $m=\aleph_0$ is the corollary printed immediately after it on the same page, 2.3.16, “Separability is a $\mathfrak c$-multiplicative property”, $\mathfrak c$ being $2^{\aleph_0}$: a product of at most $2^{\aleph_0}$ separable spaces is separable. The entry fails beyond that cardinal: if $|J| > 2^{\aleph_0}$ and $D$ is a countable dense subset of $\{0,1\}^J$, the map $J \to \{0,1\}^D$ sending $\alpha$ to $(d(\alpha))_{d \in D}$ cannot be injective, so there are distinct $\alpha,\beta$ on which every $d \in D$ agrees, and then the nonempty basic open set $\{x : x(\alpha) = 0,\ x(\beta) = 1\}$ meets $D$ in no point. Exercise 30.16(b) is the same statement for $\RR^J$, and the two are the reason the entry is No: separability of products stops exactly where that theorem says it stops. Engelking records at printed p. 82 of the same section that the bound on the number of factors is essential, directing the reader to the first part of his Theorem 1.5.3 (printed p. 37) and to Exercise 2.3.F(c).

**16. Locally metrizable: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.** A finite product of metrizable neighbourhoods is metrizable, by the metric $\max_i d_i$ of Exercise 21.3(a). For the countable column: $S_\Omega$ is locally metrizable, one section at a time having a countable basis and hence a metric (Exercise 42.2(a)), but $S_\Omega^\omega$ is not, because a basic neighbourhood leaves infinitely many coordinates free and the slice obtained by fixing the others at a point of it is homeomorphic to $S_\Omega$, which is not metrizable (Example 3 of §28); a subspace of a metrizable space is metrizable (Exercise 21.1). The arbitrary column follows.

**17. Metrizable: Yes for finitely many and for countably many factors, No otherwise.** The finite and countable entries are the metrics of Exercise 21.3(a) and (b), the second being $D(x,y) = \sup_i \bar d_i(x_i,y_i)/i$; Example 1 of §30 records the countable basis that goes with them for $\RR^\omega$. For the arbitrary column, Example 2 of §21 (printed p. 133) shows that an uncountable product of copies of $\RR$ is not metrizable, by showing that the sequence lemma fails in it.
