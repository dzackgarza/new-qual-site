::: {.exercise}

Consider the seventeen properties listed in Exercise 1 of the Supplementary Exercises (Review of the Basics).

Which of these properties are preserved under finite products?
Countable products?
Arbitrary products?
:::

::: {.solution}

The table below is the card's own survey, each entry decided by the numbered result cited in the corresponding step; no published solution of supplementary Exercise 2 of §36 was located, and the sets searched are recorded in the audit trail. The columns use the seventeen properties of Exercise 1 of the Supplementary Exercises (Review of the Basics); "Yes" means that every product of spaces with that property, over an index set of that size, has it.

| Number | Property | Finite | Countable | Arbitrary |
| --- | --- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | Connected | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 2 | Path connected | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 3 | Locally connected | Yes | No | No |
| 4 | Locally path connected | Yes | No | No |
| 5 | Compact | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 6 | Limit point compact | No | No | No |
| 7 | Locally compact Hausdorff | Yes | No | No |
| 8 | Hausdorff | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 9 | Regular | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 10 | Completely regular | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 11 | Normal | No | No | No |
| 12 | First countable | Yes | Yes | No |
| 13 | Second countable | Yes | Yes | No |
| 14 | Lindelöf | No | No | No |
| 15 | Countable dense subset | Yes | Yes | No |
| 16 | Locally metrizable | Yes | No | No |
| 17 | Metrizable | Yes | Yes | No |

Two conventions fix the entries that are not read off directly. A countably infinite index set is an arbitrary one, so a No in the countable column is a No in the arbitrary column, and to carry a No from the finite column into the other two it suffices to pad the finite family with one-point factors: a one-point space has all seventeen properties, and the product of a family with a one-point factor is homeomorphic to the product of the rest.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #p1}
(1) Connected: Yes in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 23.6 for finitely many factors. For an arbitrary index set, fix a base point $\mathbf{a}$: for each finite set $K$ of coordinates the set of points differing from $\mathbf{a}$ in coordinates in $K$ is connected (Theorems 23.3 and 23.6), their union $Y$ over all finite $K$ is connected because all of them contain $\mathbf{a}$ (Theorem 23.3), and $X=\overline{Y}$, so $X$ is connected (Theorem 23.4). The product topology is meant; in the box topology $\RR^{\omega}$ is already disconnected (Example 6 of §23).
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p2}
(2) Path connected: Yes in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Given $(x_{\alpha})$ and $(y_{\alpha})$ in the product, choose a path $f_{\alpha}$ in $X_{\alpha}$ from $x_{\alpha}$ to $y_{\alpha}$ and put $f(t)=\big(f_{\alpha}(t)\big)$; each coordinate function $\pi_{\alpha}\circ f=f_{\alpha}$ is continuous, so $f$ is continuous (Theorem 19.6), and $f(0),f(1)$ are the two points. That theorem puts no restriction on the number of factors, so the one argument gives all three columns.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p3}
(3) Locally connected: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
For finitely many factors, connected neighbourhoods $V_{\alpha}$ of the coordinates chosen inside prescribed open sets give a neighbourhood $\prod V_{\alpha}$ that is connected (Theorem 23.6), and such neighbourhoods form a basis. For the negative entries use the cube $\{0,1\}^{\omega}$, a product of discrete and hence locally connected spaces: two distinct points differ in some coordinate, and the cylinder fixing that coordinate is both open and closed, so the components of the cube are its points; were the cube locally connected, Theorem 25.3 would make each component of the open set $\{0,1\}^{\omega}$ open, that is, the cube discrete, which it is not. The countable failure covers the arbitrary column by the first convention.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p4}
(4) Locally path connected: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
The positive part is the argument of step [](#p3){.pf-ref} with path connected neighbourhoods. For the negative part, a path in $\{0,1\}^{\omega}$ has connected image (Theorem 23.5), hence is constant, so the path components are again single points, and Theorem 25.4 would make them open; equivalently, a locally path connected space is locally connected, and step [](#p3){.pf-ref} already fails.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p5}
(5) Compact: Yes in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 26.7 for finitely many factors, and the Tychonoff theorem (Theorem 37.3) for countable and arbitrary ones; the exercise set instructs that the Tychonoff theorem be assumed, and prints that instruction at Exercise 1.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p6}
(6) Limit point compact: No in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Munkres records after Exercise 28.3 (printed p. 181) that the product of two limit point compact spaces need not be limit point compact, even when both are Hausdorff, and points to Example 112 of Steen and Seebach, *Counterexamples in Topology*, for the example; that recorded fact settles the finite column, and padding with one-point factors settles the other two. The example itself is not in this checkout and was not read, so no step here depends on its content. Compact factors are of no help: compactness implies limit point compactness (Theorem 28.1), so a witness must be limit point compact and not compact, and the spaces of this chapter, such as $S_{\Omega}$, are useless in a countable product, since every countable subset of $S_{\Omega}$ lies in a compact initial segment and the closure of a countable subset of a product of copies of $S_{\Omega}$ is then a product of compact initial segments, compact by Tychonoff, so that product is countably compact, hence limit point compact (Exercise 28.4).
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p7}
(7) Locally compact Hausdorff: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
For finitely many factors take compact neighbourhoods $C_{\alpha}$ of the coordinates; then $\prod C_{\alpha}$ is compact (Theorem 26.7) and contains the neighbourhood $\prod\operatorname{int}C_{\alpha}$ of the point, and the product is Hausdorff (Theorem 31.2(a)). This is Exercise 29.2(b). For the negative entries, Example 2 of §29 (printed p. 182) shows that no basis element of $\RR^{\omega}$ lies in a compact subspace, its closure being a product of compact intervals and copies of $\RR$, and Exercise 29.2(a) gives the general obstruction: in a locally compact product all but finitely many factors are compact.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p8}
(8) Hausdorff: Yes in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 31.2(a): a product of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff, for any index set.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p9}
(9) Regular: Yes in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 31.2(b): a product of regular spaces is regular, for any index set.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p10}
(10) Completely regular: Yes in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 33.2: a product of completely regular spaces is completely regular, for any index set.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p11}
(11) Normal: No in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
The finite column is settled by the Sorgenfrey plane $\RR_{\ell}^{2}$, a product of two normal spaces (Example 2 of §31) which is not normal (Example 3 of §31); $S_{\Omega}\times\overline{S}_{\Omega}$ is a second witness, a product of two normal spaces (Example 2 of §32 and Theorem 32.4) that is not normal. Padding with one-point factors gives the other two columns. There is no product theorem to appeal to: the book records only that normality passes to closed subspaces, and that an uncountable product of normal spaces need not be normal (Example 1 of §32 and Exercise 32.9).
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p12}
(12) First countable: Yes for finitely many and countably many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 30.2 for the two positive entries. For an uncountable index set $J$, no countable family of neighbourhoods of a point of $\{0,1\}^{J}$ is a local base: a basic neighbourhood is determined by finitely many coordinates, countably many basic neighbourhoods involve only countably many coordinates in all, and for a coordinate $\alpha$ outside that countable set the cylinder fixing the $\alpha$-coordinate is a neighbourhood containing none of them. The same diagonal argument applies to $\RR^{J}$, and Example 2 of §21 draws the metrizability consequence for $\RR^{J}$ from the sequence lemma.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p13}
(13) Second countable: Yes for finitely many and countably many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
Theorem 30.2 for the positive entries. For the negative one, a second countable space is first countable (Theorem 30.2 again), in particular at each of its points, and step [](#p12){.pf-ref} shows that an uncountable product fails to be first countable.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p14}
(14) Lindelöf: No in all three columns.

::: pf-proof
Example 4 of §30: the Sorgenfrey line $\RR_{\ell}$ is Lindelöf (Example 3 of §30) and $\RR_{\ell}\times\RR_{\ell}$ is not, which settles the finite column; padding gives the other two. Neither factor is compact or metrizable in a way that would help: both are Lindelöf and their product is not.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p15}
(15) Countable dense subset: Yes for finitely many and countably many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
If $D_{\alpha}$ is a countable dense subset of $X_{\alpha}$ then $\prod D_{\alpha}$ is countable and its closure is $\prod\overline{D_{\alpha}}=\prod X_{\alpha}$ (Theorem 19.5), so the product is separable; this is Exercise 30.10 for countably many factors, and the finite case follows by padding. For an arbitrary index set of cardinality at most $2^{\aleph_{0}}$ the positive half is the Hewitt--Marczewski--Pondiczery theorem, which Engelking states as Theorem 2.3.15 (*General Topology*, revised and completed edition, Heldermann, 1989, §2.3, printed p. 81): if $d(X_{s})\le m\ge\aleph_{0}$ for every $s\in S$ and $\cardinality{S}\le2^{m}$, then $d(\prod_{s\in S}X_{s})\le m$, where $d$ is the least cardinality of a dense subset; the case $m=\aleph_{0}$ is Corollary 2.3.16 printed immediately after it, that separability is $\mathfrak{c}$-multiplicative. The entry fails beyond that cardinal: if $\cardinality{J}>2^{\aleph_{0}}$ and $D$ is a countable dense subset of $\{0,1\}^{J}$, the map $J\to\{0,1\}^{D}$ sending $\alpha$ to $(d(\alpha))_{d\in D}$ cannot be injective, so there are distinct $\alpha,\beta$ on which every $d\in D$ agrees, and then the nonempty basic open set $\{x:x(\alpha)=0,\ x(\beta)=1\}$ meets $D$ in no point. Exercise 30.16(b) is the same statement for $\RR^{J}$, and Engelking notes at printed p. 82 that the bound on the number of factors is essential, directing the reader to Theorem 1.5.3 (printed p. 37) and Exercise 2.3.F(c).
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p16}
(16) Locally metrizable: Yes for finitely many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
A finite product of metrizable neighbourhoods is metrizable by the metric $\max_{i}d_{i}$ of Exercise 21.3(a). For the countable column, $S_{\Omega}$ is locally metrizable, one section at a time having a countable basis and hence a metric (Exercise 42.2(a)), but $S_{\Omega}^{\omega}$ is not: a basic neighbourhood leaves infinitely many coordinates free, and the slice obtained by fixing the others at a point of it is homeomorphic to $S_{\Omega}$, which is not metrizable (Example 3 of §28), while a subspace of a metrizable space is metrizable (Exercise 21.1). The arbitrary column follows.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #p17}
(17) Metrizable: Yes for finitely many and countably many factors, No otherwise.

::: pf-proof
The positive entries are the metrics of Exercise 21.3(a) and (b), the second being $D(x,y)=\sup_{i}\bar d_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})/i$, recorded together with the countable basis that goes with them for $\RR^{\omega}$ in Example 1 of §30. For the arbitrary column, Example 2 of §21 (printed p. 133) shows that an uncountable product of copies of $\RR$ is not metrizable, by showing that the sequence lemma fails in it.
:::
