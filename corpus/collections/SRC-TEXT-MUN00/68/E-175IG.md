::: {.exercise}

Let $G = G_1 * G_2$, where $G_1$ and $G_2$ are nontrivial groups.

(a) Show that $G$ is not abelian.

(b) If $x \in G$, define the length of $x$ to be the length of the unique reduced word in the elements of $G_1$ and $G_2$ that represents $x$.
Show that if $x$ has even length (at least 2), then $x$ does not have finite order.
Show that if $x$ has odd length, then $x$ is conjugate to an element of shorter length.

(c) Show that the only elements of $G$ that have finite order are the elements of $G_1$ and $G_2$ that have finite order, and their conjugates.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Prove non-commutativity, word-length reduction under conjugation, and the classification of torsion elements in a free product $G = G_1 \ast G_2$.

<1>1. Part (a): $G_1 \ast G_2$ is not abelian.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Since $G_1$ and $G_2$ are nontrivial, choose $g_1 \in G_1 \setminus \{e\}$ and $g_2 \in G_2 \setminus \{e\}$.
    <2>2. The words $w_1 = g_1 g_2$ and $w_2 = g_2 g_1$ are both reduced words of length 2.
    <2>3. By the Normal Form Theorem for free products, two reduced words represent the same element of $G_1 \ast G_2$ if and only if they are identical letter-by-letter.
    <2>4. The first letter of $w_1$ is $g_1 \in G_1$, while the first letter of $w_2$ is $g_2 \in G_2$. Since $G_1 \cap G_2 = \{e\}$ and $g_1, g_2 \neq e$, $w_1 \neq w_2$.
    <2>5. Thus $g_1 g_2 \neq g_2 g_1$, so $G$ is non-abelian.

<1>2. Part (b): Order of even length words and shortening of odd length words.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Even length words have infinite order:
        - Let $x = x_1 x_2 \cdots x_{2k}$ with $2k \ge 2$ be a reduced word of even length.
        - The letters alternate between $G_1 \setminus \{e\}$ and $G_2 \setminus \{e\}$. Because the length $2k$ is even, $x_1$ and $x_{2k}$ belong to different factors (e.g. $x_1 \in G_1$, $x_{2k} \in G_2$).
        - For any integer $m \ge 1$, the power $x^m = (x_1 \cdots x_{2k})(x_1 \cdots x_{2k}) \cdots (x_1 \cdots x_{2k})$ contains no adjacent letters from the same factor at the junctions between copies of $x$.
        - Thus $x^m$ is in reduced form with length $2km \ge 2m > 0$.
        - Therefore $x^m \neq e$ for all $m \ge 1$, so $x$ has infinite order.
    <2>2. Odd length words conjugate to shorter words:
        - Let $x = x_1 x_2 \cdots x_{2k+1}$ with $2k+1 \ge 3$ be a reduced word of odd length.
        - Since the length is odd, the first letter $x_1$ and last letter $x_{2k+1}$ belong to the same factor (say $G_1$).
        - Conjugate $x$ by $x_1$:
          $$y = x_1^{-1} x x_1 = x_2 x_3 \cdots x_{2k} (x_{2k+1} x_1).$$
        - The product $h = x_{2k+1} x_1$ is an element of $G_1$.
        - If $h \neq e$, then $y = x_2 x_3 \cdots x_{2k} h$ is a reduced word of length $2k < 2k+1$.
        - If $h = e$, then $y = x_2 x_3 \cdots x_{2k}$ is a reduced word of length $2k-1 < 2k+1$.
        - In either case, $x$ is conjugate to an element $y$ of strictly shorter length.

<1>3. Part (c): Torsion elements are conjugates of torsion elements in factors.
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $x \in G_1 \ast G_2$ be a non-trivial element of finite order.
    <2>2. By <1>2, $x$ cannot have even length $\ge 2$.
    <2>3. If $x$ has odd length $\ge 3$, <1>2 shows that $x$ is conjugate to an element $x'$ of strictly shorter length. Since conjugate elements have the same order, $x'$ also has finite order.
    <2>4. Applying this length reduction repeatedly, $x$ is conjugate to an element $z$ of length 1 having finite order.
    <2>5. A reduced word of length 1 is an element of $G_1 \setminus \{e\}$ or $G_2 \setminus \{e\}$.
    <2>6. Therefore, every element of finite order in $G$ is a conjugate of a finite order element of $G_1$ or $G_2$. Q.E.D.
:::
