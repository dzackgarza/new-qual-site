::: {.exercise}

Let $G = G_1 * G_2$, where $G_1$ and $G_2$ are nontrivial groups.

(a) Show that $G$ is not abelian.

(b) If $x \in G$, define the length of $x$ to be the length of the unique reduced word in the elements of $G_1$ and $G_2$ that represents $x$.
Show that if $x$ has even length (at least 2), then $x$ does not have finite order.
Show that if $x$ has odd length, then $x$ is conjugate to an element of shorter length.

(c) Show that the only elements of $G$ that have finite order are the elements of $G_1$ and $G_2$ that have finite order, and their conjugates.
:::

::: {.solution}

The last assertion of (b) is false as printed, at length $1$: if $x\in G_1\setminus\theset{1}$, then $x\neq1$, and every conjugate $cxc^{-1}$ is again $\neq1$, while the only element of length $0$ is $1$; so $x$ is conjugate to no element of shorter length. What (b) proves is the printed statement for odd length $\ge3$, and the length-one case is excluded with that reason. Parts (a) and (c) need no amendment.

::: pf

::: pf-step

(a) $G_1*G_2$ is not abelian.

::: pf-proof

Choose $g\in G_1\setminus\theset{1}$ and $h\in G_2\setminus\theset{1}$. The words $(g,h)$ and $(h,g)$ are reduced of length $2$ and are distinct, so by Theorem 68.2 (printed p. 415) they represent distinct elements; hence $gh\neq hg$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) An element of even length $2k\ge2$ has infinite order.

::: pf-proof

Let $x=a_1b_1\cdots a_kb_k$ be its reduced word, with $a_j\in G_1\setminus\theset{1}$ and $b_j\in G_2\setminus\theset{1}$; by the uniqueness of the reduced word we may name the factors so that the word begins in $G_1$ and ends in $G_2$. In the product $x^n$ the last letter of each copy and the first letter of the next lie in different factors, so no cancellation occurs and $x^n$ has reduced word $a_1b_1\cdots a_nb_n$ of length $2kn>0$; in particular $x^n\neq1$ and $x$ has infinite order.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) An element of odd length $2k+1\ge3$ is conjugate to an element of shorter length.

::: pf-proof

Let $x=a_1b_1\cdots a_kb_ka_{k+1}$ be reduced, with $a_j\in G_1\setminus\theset{1}$ and $b_j\in G_2\setminus\theset{1}$. Conjugating by $a_1^{-1}$,

$$
a_1^{-1}\,x\,a_1=b_1a_2\cdots b_ka_{k+1}a_1 ,
$$

whose length is $2k$ if $a_{k+1}a_1\neq1$ and $2k-1$ if $a_{k+1}a_1=1$; in either case the length is strictly less than $2k+1$, and the last letter $a_{k+1}a_1$ lies in $G_1$ as required for a reduced word.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(c) Every element of finite order is a conjugate of a finite-order element of $G_1$ or $G_2$.

::: pf-proof

Let $x\neq1$ have finite order. By step 2 its length is not even, so it is odd. By step 3, if its length exceeds $1$ it is conjugate to $y$ of strictly smaller length, and $y$ has the same finite order. Iterating, since lengths are non-negative integers, we reach a conjugate $z$ of $x$ of finite order and of minimal length; that length cannot be even by step 2 and cannot exceed $1$ by step 3, so $z$ has length $1$, that is, $z\in G_1$ or $z\in G_2$, with its finite order. Hence $x$ is conjugate to a finite-order element of one of the factors.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

Parts (a), (b) and (c) are steps 1--4; the printed odd-length clause of (b) holds for length at least $3$ and fails at length $1$, as the opening paragraph records.
