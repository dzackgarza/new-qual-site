::: {.problem}
Let $Y$ be the curve $y^2 = x^3 - x$ in $\AA^2$, and assume $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2$.
This exercise shows that $Y$ is not a rational curve, and hence that $K(Y)$ is not a purely transcendental extension of $k$.

(a) Show that $Y$ is nonsingular, and deduce that $A = A(Y) \cong k[x,y]/(y^2 - x^3 + x)$ is an integrally closed domain.

(b) Let $k[x]$ be the subring of $K = K(Y)$ generated by the image of $x$ in $A$.
   Show that $k[x]$ is a polynomial ring, and that $A$ is the integral closure of $k[x]$ in $K$.

(c) Show that there is an automorphism $\sigma: A \to A$ sending $y$ to $-y$ and fixing $x$.
   For $a \in A$, define the norm of $a$ to be $N(a) = a \cdot \sigma(a)$.
   Show that $N(a) \in k[x]$, that $N(1) = 1$, and that $N(ab) = N(a) N(b)$ for all $a, b \in A$.

(d) Using the norm, show that the units in $A$ are precisely the nonzero elements of $k$.
   Show that $x$ and $y$ are irreducible elements of $A$, and that $A$ is not a unique factorization domain.

(e) Prove that $Y$ is not a rational curve.
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
$$
F=y^2-x^3+x,qquad A=k[x,y]/(F),qquad K=\operatorname{Frac}(A).
$$
We write $x,y$ also for their images in $A$.

<1>1. The affine curve $Y=V(F)$ is nonsingular and irreducible; hence $A$ is an integrally closed domain.

::: {.proof}
The partial derivatives are
$$
F_x=1-3x^2,qquad F_y=2y.
$$
At a singular point we would have $y=0$, because $2\ne0$ in $k$, and the equation of the curve would give
$$
x(x-1)(x+1)=0.
$$
At $x=0$ we have $F_x=1$.
At $x=\pm1$ we have $F_x=-2$, which is also nonzero because $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$.
Thus $Y$ has no singular point.

The polynomial $F$, viewed as a monic quadratic in $y$ over $k[x]$, is irreducible.
Indeed, reducibility would make $x^3-x=x(x-1)(x+1)$ a square in $k[x]$; its three roots are distinct when $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$, so this is impossible.
Therefore $A$ is a domain.

Every local ring of the nonsingular curve $Y$ is a regular local ring of dimension one, hence a discrete valuation ring and therefore integrally closed.
Normality is local, so the affine coordinate ring $A$ is integrally closed in its fraction field.
This proves part (a).
:::

<1>2. The subring generated by $x$ is a polynomial ring $k[x]$, and $A$ is finite integral over it.

::: {.proof}
If a polynomial $p(T)\in k[T]$ satisfied $p(x)=0$ in $A$, then $p(x)$ would lie in the principal ideal $(F)\subseteq k[x,y]$.
Division by the monic polynomial $F$ in the variable $y$ leaves $p(x)$ as its own remainder, so $p=0$.
Thus $x$ is transcendental over $k$ and the indicated subring is a copy of $k[x]$.

The relation
$$
y^2=x^3-x
$$
is monic in $y$.
Hence $y$ is integral over $k[x]$ and
$$
A=k[x]\oplus k[x]y
$$
as a $k[x]$-module.
In particular, $A$ is finite and integral over $k[x]$.
:::

<1>3. The ring $A$ is the integral closure of $k[x]$ in $K$.

::: {.proof}
Every element of $A$ is integral over $k[x]$ by step <1>2.
Conversely, let $u\in K$ be integral over $k[x]$.
The same monic equation also has its coefficients in the larger ring $A$, so $u$ is integral over $A$.
Step <1>1 says that $A$ is integrally closed in $K$, hence $u\in A$.
Thus $A$ is exactly the integral closure, proving part (b).
:::

<1>4. The involution $\sigma(x)=x$, $\sigma(y)=-y$ defines an automorphism of $A$, and for $a=p(x)+yq(x)$,
$$
\boxed{N(a)=p(x)^2-(x^3-x)q(x)^2}.
$$

::: {.proof}
The substitution $y\mapsto-y$ fixes the relation $F$, so it induces a $k$-algebra automorphism $\sigma$ of $A$ with $\sigma^2=1$.
The decomposition in step <1>2 is unique, so every $a\in A$ is $p+yq$ with $p,q\in k[x]$.
Then
$$
N(a)=a\sigma(a)=(p+yq)(p-yq)=p^2-y^2q^2=p^2-(x^3-x)q^2\in k[x].
$$
Also $N(1)=1$, and because $A$ is commutative and $\sigma$ is an automorphism,
$$
N(ab)=ab\,\sigma(a)\sigma(b)=N(a)N(b).
$$
This proves part (c).
:::

<1>5. The units of $A$ are precisely $k^\times$.

::: {.proof}
If $a\in A^\times$, then
$$
N(a)N(a^{-1})=N(1)=1,
$$
so $N(a)$ is a unit of $k[x]$, hence a nonzero constant.
Write $a=p+yq$ as in step <1>4.
If $q\ne0$, the two terms of
$$
N(a)=p^2-(x^3-x)q^2
$$
have degrees $2\deg p$ and $3+2\deg q$ when nonzero.
One degree is even and the other odd, so their leading terms cannot cancel; the norm then has positive degree.
Thus $q=0$.
Now $N(a)=p^2$ is constant, so $p\in k^\times$.
Conversely every nonzero constant is a unit.
:::

<1>6. The elements $x$, $x-1$, $x+1$, and $y$ are irreducible in $A$.

::: {.proof}
First note that every nonunit $a\in A$ has a nonconstant norm by step <1>5.
Moreover, the norm formula in step <1>4 shows that a nonconstant norm cannot have degree one: if $q=0$ its degree is the positive even integer $2\deg p$, while if $q\ne0$ its degree is the maximum of an even integer and the odd integer $3+2\deg q$, hence at least two.

For $c\in\{0,1,-1\}$,
$$
N(x-c)=(x-c)^2.
$$
If $x-c=ab$ with both $a,b$ nonunits, multiplicativity would give a factorization of this degree-two polynomial into the two nonconstant polynomials $N(a),N(b)$, each of degree at least two, which is impossible.
Thus each $x-c$ is irreducible.

Similarly,
$$
N(y)=-(x^3-x)=-x(x-1)(x+1)
$$
has degree three.
If $y=ab$ with both factors nonunits, then $N(a)$ and $N(b)$ would again both have degree at least two, so their product would have degree at least four, contradiction.
Hence $y$ is irreducible.
:::

<1>7. The domain $A$ is not a unique factorization domain.

::: {.proof}
The defining equation gives two factorizations
$$
y\cdot y=x(x-1)(x+1).
$$
All five displayed factors are irreducible by step <1>6.
By step <1>5 the only units are nonzero constants, so $y$ is not associate to any of $x,x-1,x+1$, and those three are pairwise nonassociate.
Thus the two sides are genuinely different factorizations into irreducibles.
Therefore $A$ is not a unique factorization domain, completing part (d).
:::

<1>8. The curve $Y$ is not rational.

::: {.proof}
The curve is nonsingular by step <1>1 and affine by definition.
It is not isomorphic to $\PP^1$: the nonconstant element $x\in A=\Gamma(Y,\OO_Y)$ is a global regular function, whereas every global regular function on $\PP^1$ is constant.

If $Y$ were rational, [[P-AGH61RATNONSINGCURVE|Exercise I.6.1]] would therefore apply and would imply that its affine coordinate ring $A(Y)$ is a unique factorization domain.
This contradicts step <1>7.
Hence $Y$ is not rational.
Since a rational curve has function field $k(t)$, it follows equivalently that $K(Y)$ is not purely transcendental over $k$.
This proves part (e).
:::

<1>9. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 proves part (a), steps <1>2--<1>3 prove part (b), step <1>4 proves part (c), steps <1>5--<1>7 prove part (d), and step <1>8 proves part (e).
:::
