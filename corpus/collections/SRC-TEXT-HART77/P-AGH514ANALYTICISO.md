::: {.problem}
(a) If $P \in Y$ and $Q \in Z$ are analytically isomorphic plane curve singularities, show that the multiplicities $\mu_P(Y)$ and $\mu_Q(Z)$ are the same.

(b) Let $f = f_r + f_{r+1} + \cdots \in k[[x,y]]$, and suppose the leading form $f_r$ of $f$ factors as $f_r = g_s h_t$, where $g_s$ and $h_t$ are homogeneous of degrees $s$ and $t$ respectively and have no common linear factor.
   Show that there are formal power series
$$
g = g_s + g_{s+1} + \cdots, \qquad h = h_t + h_{t+1} + \cdots
$$
in $k[[x,y]]$ such that $f = gh$.

(c) Let $Y$ be defined by $f(x,y) = 0$ in $\AA^2$, and let $P = (0,0)$ be a point of multiplicity $r$ on $Y$, so that $f = f_r + (\text{higher terms})$.
Call $P$ an ordinary $r$-fold point if $f_r$ is a product of $r$ distinct linear factors.
Show that any two ordinary double points are analytically isomorphic, and likewise for ordinary triple points.
Show however that there is a one-parameter family of mutually nonisomorphic ordinary $4$-fold points.

(d) Assume $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2$.
Show that any double point of a plane curve is analytically isomorphic to the singularity at $(0,0)$ of the curve $y^2 = x^r$, for a uniquely determined $r \geq 2$.
If $r = 2$ it is a node; if $r = 3$ it is a cusp; if $r = 4$ it is a tacnode.
:::

::: {.solution}
Translate the chosen point to the origin. For a nonzero series $F\in k[[x,y]]$, write
$$
\operatorname{ord}(F)=\min\{i+j:[x^iy^j]F\ne0\},
$$
and call its homogeneous term of that degree its initial form.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #s1}

Analytic isomorphism preserves the multiplicity of a plane curve singularity.

::: pf-proof

Let
$$
A=k[[x,y]]/(f),\qquad \mathfrak m=(x,y)A,
$$
where $r=\operatorname{ord}(f)$ and $f_r$ is the initial form.
If $r=1$, then $A$ is a regular local ring of dimension one: after a linear change, the initial form is $y$, and the formal inverse-function argument solves for $y$ as a series in $x$.
Regularity is intrinsic to the complete local ring, so an analytically isomorphic plane curve point also has multiplicity one.

Assume now that $r\ge2$.
Then $\dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=2$, and the images of $x,y$ give an identification
$\operatorname{Sym}_k(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)\cong k[x,y]$.
For the $\mathfrak m$-adic filtration,
$$
\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak m}A
\cong k[x,y]/(f_r).
$$
Indeed, the initial ideal of the principal ideal $(f)$ is generated by $f_r$: if $u$ has initial form $u_d$, then the initial form of $uf$ is $u_df_r$.

The smallest degree in which the natural map
$$
\operatorname{Sym}_k(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)
\longrightarrow \operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak m}A
$$
has a nonzero kernel is therefore exactly $r$.
An isomorphism of complete local $k$-algebras carries the maximal ideal to the maximal ideal and induces an isomorphism of associated graded rings, preserving that smallest degree.
For a plane curve, this order $r$ is precisely the multiplicity at the point.
Hence analytically isomorphic plane curve singularities have the same multiplicity, proving part (a).

:::

::: {.pf-step #s2}

If homogeneous forms $g_s,h_t\in k[x,y]$ have no common linear factor, then for every $d\ge s+t-1$,
$$
k[x,y]_d=g_s k[x,y]_{d-s}+h_t k[x,y]_{d-t}.
$$

::: pf-proof

Because every nonconstant homogeneous polynomial in two variables factors into linear forms over the algebraically closed field $k$, the hypothesis says $\gcd(g_s,h_t)=1$.
Consequently the syzygies of $(g_s,h_t)$ are generated by $(h_t,-g_s)$, and there is an exact sequence
$$
0\longrightarrow k[x,y](-s-t)
\xrightarrow{q\mapsto(qh_t,-qg_s)}
k[x,y](-s)\oplus k[x,y](-t)
\xrightarrow{(a,b)\mapsto ag_s+bh_t}
(g_s,h_t)\longrightarrow0.
$$
Taking degree $d$ and using $\dim_k k[x,y]_m=m+1$ for $m\ge0$ shows that the degree-$d$ quotient by $(g_s,h_t)$ has dimension zero once $d\ge s+t-1$.
For $d=s+t-1$ this is
$$
(s+t)-(t)-(s)=0,
$$
and for $d\ge s+t$ it is
$$
(d+1)-(d-s+1)-(d-t+1)+(d-s-t+1)=0.
$$
Thus every form of degree at least $s+t-1$ has the asserted expression.

:::

::: {.pf-step #s3}

The factors $g$ and $h$ required in part (b) can be constructed degree by degree.

::: pf-proof

Since $f_r=g_sh_t$, we have $r=s+t$.
Suppose for some $n\ge1$ that homogeneous terms
$$
g_s,\ldots,g_{s+n-1},\qquad h_t,\ldots,h_{t+n-1}
$$
have been chosen so that
$$
f-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g_{s+i}\right)
  \left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}h_{t+j}\right)
$$
has no homogeneous term of degree below $r+n$.
Let $R_{r+n}$ be its degree-$(r+n)$ term.
By step [](#s2){.pf-ref}, because $r+n=s+t+n\ge s+t-1$, there are forms
$$
g_{s+n}\in k[x,y]_{s+n},\qquad h_{t+n}\in k[x,y]_{t+n}
$$
such that
$$
R_{r+n}=g_{s+n}h_t+g_sh_{t+n}.
$$
Adding these two terms therefore kills the discrepancy in degree $r+n$ without changing lower degrees.

Induction defines
$$
g=\sum_{i\ge0}g_{s+i},\qquad h=\sum_{j\ge0}h_{t+j}.
$$
For each total degree only finitely many summands contribute, so these are well-defined formal power series.
The construction makes every homogeneous component of $f-gh$ zero; hence $f=gh$.
This proves part (b).

:::

::: {.pf-step #s4}

An ordinary double point is analytically isomorphic to $xy=0$, and an ordinary triple point is analytically isomorphic to $xy(x-y)=0$.

::: pf-proof

For an ordinary $r$-fold point, repeatedly apply step [](#s3){.pf-ref}, separating one tangent factor from the product of the others.
This gives
$$
f=g_1\cdots g_r,
$$
where each $g_i$ has a distinct nonzero linear initial form $L_i$.

For $r=2$, the two linear forms are independent.
Thus $(X,Y)=(g_1,g_2)$ is a formal change of coordinates: its linear part is invertible, so the inverse is constructed recursively degree by degree.
In these coordinates $(f)=(XY)$.

For $r=3$, first use $(g_1,g_2)$ as coordinates $x,y$.
The third factor then has the form
$$
g_3=ax+by+q,
$$
where $a,b\ne0$ and $q\in(x,y)^2$, because its tangent line is distinct from both coordinate axes.
Write $q=xq_1+yq_2$ and put
$$
X=x(1+q_1/a),\qquad Y=y(1+q_2/b).
$$
This is another formal coordinate change, and $g_3=aX+bY$.
Up to multiplication by a unit, the equation is therefore $XY(aX+bY)$.
A linear change of $X,Y$ carries its three distinct tangent lines to $x=0$, $y=0$, and $x-y=0$.
Thus all ordinary double points, respectively all ordinary triple points, have the same analytic type.

:::

::: {.pf-step #s5}

Ordinary quadruple points have a one-dimensional family of analytic types.

::: pf-proof

For $\lambda\in k\setminus\{0,1\}$ consider
$$
A_\lambda
=k[[x,y]]/\bigl(xy(x-y)(x-\lambda y)\bigr).
$$
Each is an ordinary quadruple point.
An isomorphism $A_\lambda\cong A_\mu$ induces an isomorphism of associated graded rings by step [](#s1){.pf-ref}, hence an invertible linear map of the tangent space carrying the unordered four tangent lines of $A_\lambda$ to those of $A_\mu$.
After identifying lines through the origin with points of $\PP^1$, this says that the unordered configurations
$$
\{0,\infty,1,\lambda\},\qquad \{0,\infty,1,\mu\}
$$
are projectively equivalent.

The cross ratio of four ordered points is invariant under $\operatorname{PGL}_2(k)$.
Permuting the four points changes $\lambda$ only among
$$
\lambda,\quad 1-\lambda,\quad \lambda^{-1},\quad
(1-\lambda)^{-1},\quad \frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1},\quad
\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}.
$$
Therefore $A_\lambda\cong A_\mu$ can hold only when $\mu$ lies in this finite orbit of $\lambda$.
The parameter line $k\setminus\{0,1\}$ modulo these finite orbits is one-dimensional; choosing one representative from each orbit gives a one-parameter collection of mutually nonisomorphic ordinary quadruple points.
This proves part (c).

:::

::: {.pf-step #s6}

Assume $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$. Every double point is analytically isomorphic to $y^2=x^r$ for some $r\ge2$.

::: pf-proof

Let $f=f_2+f_3+\cdots$ define the double point.
If $f_2$ is a product of two distinct linear forms, step [](#s4){.pf-ref} gives the node $xy=0$.
Since $k$ is algebraically closed and $2$ is invertible, the linear change
$$
x=Y-X,\qquad y=Y+X
$$
identifies $xy=0$ with $Y^2=X^2$, so this is the case $r=2$.

Otherwise $f_2$ is the square of one linear form.
After a linear change and multiplication of $f$ by a nonzero scalar, write
$$
f=y^2+\text{terms of total degree at least }3.
$$
We now eliminate every higher-degree term containing $y$.
Suppose inductively that through degree $N-1$ the equation has the form
$$
y^2-a(x),
$$
and let $R_N(x,y)$ be the remaining homogeneous term of degree $N$.
Write uniquely
$$
R_N(x,y)=yB_{N-1}(x,y)+c_Nx^N.
$$
Replacing $y$ by $y-\tfrac12B_{N-1}(x,y)$ changes $y^2$ by
$-yB_{N-1}$ in degree $N$; all other new terms have degree greater than $N$.
Thus it kills the $y$-divisible part and absorbs $c_Nx^N$ into $a(x)$.
The successive coordinate changes have identity linear part and orders tending to infinity, so their infinite composition converges in the $(x,y)$-adic topology to a formal automorphism.
It transforms the equation into
$$
y^2-a(x)=0
$$
for a series $a(x)\in x^3k[[x]]$.

The series $a$ is nonzero.
Indeed, the local ring of the plane curve variety is reduced and essentially of finite type over $k$.
Its completion map has geometrically regular formal fibres, as recorded in [[D-MORNORMREG|the regularity of completion for rings essentially of finite type over a field]], so reducedness ascends to the completion.
If $a=0$, the transformed completed local ring would be $k[[x,y]]/(y^2)$, which is not reduced.
Write
$$
a(x)=x^ru(x),\qquad r\ge3,
$$
with $u(0)\ne0$.
Because $k$ is algebraically closed, choose a square root of $u(0)$.
Since $2$ is invertible, recursively comparing coefficients gives a unit $v(x)\in k[[x]]^\times$ with $v(x)^2=u(x)$.
The coordinate change $Y=y/v(x)$ gives
$$
Y^2=x^r.
$$
Together with the distinct-tangent case, this proves existence for every $r\ge2$ that occurs.

:::

::: {.pf-step #s7}

The exponent $r$ in step [](#s6){.pf-ref} is an invariant of the completed local ring.

::: pf-proof

Put
$$
A_r=k[[x,y]]/(y^2-x^r).
$$
If $r=2m$ is even, $A_r$ has exactly two minimal primes
$$
\mathfrak p_+=(y-x^m),\qquad \mathfrak p_-=(y+x^m).
$$
Their intersection multiplicity is the intrinsic length
$$
\operatorname{length}\bigl(A_r/(\mathfrak p_++\mathfrak p_-)\bigr)
=\operatorname{length}\bigl(k[[x]]/(2x^m)\bigr)=m.
$$
An isomorphism permutes the minimal primes and preserves this length, so it determines $m$ and hence $r$.

If $r$ is odd, $A_r$ is a domain and its normalization is $k[[t]]$ under
$$
x=t^2,\qquad y=t^r.
$$
Thus the set of orders of nonzero elements of $A_r$ is the numerical semigroup
$$
\{2a+rb:a,b\ge0\}.
$$
In particular, $r$ is its least odd positive element.
Any isomorphism of the domains $A_r$ and $A_{r'}$ extends to an isomorphism of their normalizations.
An automorphism of the DVR $k[[t]]$ preserves its $t$-adic valuation, so it preserves this value semigroup and hence its least odd element.
Therefore $r=r'$ in the odd case as well.

Finally, an even $r$ cannot be isomorphic to an odd $r'$ because the former ring has two minimal primes and the latter is a domain.
Hence the exponent is uniquely determined.

:::
