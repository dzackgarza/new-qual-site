::: {.problem}
Describe $\Spec \RR[x]$.
How does its topological space compare to the set $\RR$?
To $\CC$?
:::

::: {.solution}
Put
\[
X=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb R[x].
\]

<1>1. The prime ideals of $\mathbb R[x]$ are
\[
(0)
\]
and the principal ideals
\[
(p(x))
\]
generated by irreducible polynomials $p(x)$.
::: {.proof}
The polynomial ring $\mathbb R[x]$ is a principal ideal domain.  Every nonzero prime ideal in a PID is generated by an irreducible element.  Since $\mathbb R[x]$ is an integral domain, $(0)$ is prime as well.
:::

<1>2. Every irreducible polynomial in $\mathbb R[x]$ has degree $1$ or $2$.
The irreducibles are, up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar,
\[
x-a,
\qquad a\in\mathbb R,
\]
and
\[
(x-a)^2+b^2,
\qquad a\in\mathbb R,
\quad b>0.
\]
::: {.proof}
By the fundamental theorem of algebra, every nonconstant real polynomial factors over $\mathbb C$ into linear factors.  Nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs.

If a real polynomial is irreducible and has a real root, it must be linear.  If it has no real root, choose a nonreal root
\[
a+ib,
\qquad b\ne0.
\]
Its conjugate $a-ib$ is also a root, so the real quadratic
\[
(x-a-ib)(x-a+ib)
=(x-a)^2+b^2
\]
divides the polynomial.  Irreducibility forces equality up to a scalar.  Taking $b>0$ makes the representation unique.
:::

<1>3. Thus the points of $X$ are
\[
\boxed{
\eta=(0),
\quad
x_a=(x-a)\ (a\in\mathbb R),
\quad
x_{a,b}=((x-a)^2+b^2)\ (a\in\mathbb R,b>0).
}
\]
The point $\eta$ is generic and all the other points are closed.
::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 and <1>2 list all prime ideals.  The closure of $(0)$ is all of $X$, since
\[
V(0)=X.
\]
Every nonzero prime ideal in the PID $\mathbb R[x]$ is maximal, so each corresponding point is closed.
:::

<1>4. The residue fields at the closed points are
\[
\boxed{
\kappa(x_a)=\mathbb R,
\qquad
\kappa(x_{a,b})\cong\mathbb C.
}
\]
::: {.proof}
For a maximal ideal generated by an irreducible polynomial $p$,
\[
\kappa((p))
=\mathbb R[x]/(p).
\]
For $p=x-a$ this quotient is $\mathbb R$.

For
\[
p=(x-a)^2+b^2,
\qquad b>0,
\]
the class of
\[
\frac{x-a}{b}
\]
squares to $-1$, so the quotient is isomorphic to $\mathbb C$.
:::

<1>5. Every proper closed subset of $X$ is finite and consists only of closed points.
::: {.proof}
Every closed subset is
\[
V(I)=V(f)
\]
for some $f\in\mathbb R[x]$, because the ring is a PID.

If $f=0$, then
\[
V(f)=X.
\]
If $f\ne0$, the generic prime $(0)$ does not contain $f$.  A closed point $(p)$ lies in $V(f)$ exactly when
\[
p\mid f.
\]
A nonzero polynomial has only finitely many irreducible factors.  Hence $V(f)$ is a finite set of closed points.
:::

<1>6. The real line $\mathbb R$ identifies with the subset of real closed points
\[
\{(x-a):a\in\mathbb R\}\subseteq X,
\]
and the induced Zariski topology on this subset is the cofinite topology.
::: {.proof}
The map
\[
a\longmapsto(x-a)
\]
is a bijection from $\mathbb R$ to the closed points whose residue field is $\mathbb R$.

By <1>5, the intersection of a proper closed subset of $X$ with these points is finite.  Conversely, any finite set
\[
\{a_1,\ldots,a_r\}
\]
is the zero set of
\[
\prod_{i=1}^r(x-a_i).
\]
Thus the closed subsets of the induced topology are exactly the finite subsets and the whole set.

So this is the Zariski, not the Euclidean, topology on the real line.
:::

<1>7. Set-theoretically, the closed points of $X$ are naturally
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb C/\{z\sim\bar z\}.
}
\]
::: {.proof}
A real point $a\in\mathbb R$ is fixed by conjugation and corresponds to the linear prime
\[
(x-a).
\]
A nonreal conjugate pair
\[
\{a+ib,a-ib\},
\qquad b>0,
\]
corresponds to its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb R$,
\[
(x-a)^2+b^2.
\]
Step <1>2 shows that every closed point occurs uniquely in one of these ways.
:::

<1>8. More canonically, the finite integral extension
\[
\mathbb R[x]\hookrightarrow\mathbb C[x]
\]
induces a surjective closed map
\[
\pi:
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[x]
\longrightarrow
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb R[x]
\]
which sends
\[
(x-z)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(x-\bar z)
\]
to the same point when $z\notin\mathbb R$ and is one-to-one on the real points and on the generic point.
::: {.proof}
The extension $\mathbb C[x]/\mathbb R[x]$ is finite, hence integral.  Lying over makes the induced map on spectra surjective, and integral morphisms are closed.

For $z\in\mathbb C$, the contraction of
\[
(x-z)\subseteq\mathbb C[x]
\]
to $\mathbb R[x]$ is the kernel of evaluation
\[
\mathbb R[x]\to\mathbb C,
\qquad
f\mapsto f(z).
\]
If $z=a\in\mathbb R$, this kernel is $(x-a)$.  If
\[
z=a+ib,
\qquad b\ne0,
\]
the kernel is generated by the real minimal polynomial
\[
(x-a)^2+b^2,
\]
which is also the contraction of $(x-\bar z)$.

The zero ideal contracts to the zero ideal, so the generic point maps to the generic point.
:::

<1>9. Thus one may picture $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb R[x]$ as
\[
\boxed{
\text{one generic point together with the conjugation quotient of }\mathbb C
\text{ as its closed points},
}
\]
with every proper closed subset finite.
::: {.proof}
This combines <1>3, <1>5, and <1>7--<1>8.
:::

<1>10. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
Steps <1>3--<1>9 give the requested description and comparison with $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$.
:::
