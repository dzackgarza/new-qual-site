::: {.problem}
In the category of varieties, the Zariski topology on a product is not the product topology.
In the category of schemes, the underlying point set of a product is not even the product set.

a. Let $k$ be a field and let $\AA_k^1 = \Spec k[x]$ be the affine line over $k$.
Show that $\fiberprod{\AA_k^1}{\Spec k}{\AA_k^1} \cong \AA_k^2$, and show that the underlying point set of the product is not the product of the underlying point sets of the factors, even if $k$ is algebraically closed.

b. Let $k$ be a field and let $s$ and $t$ be indeterminates over $k$.
Then $\Spec k(s)$, $\Spec k(t)$, and $\Spec k$ are all one-point spaces.
Describe the product scheme $\fiberprod{\Spec k(s)}{\Spec k}{\Spec k(t)}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The product of the two affine lines over $k$ is
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb A^1_k\times_{\Spec k}\mathbb A^1_k
\cong
\Spec k[x,y]
=\mathbb A^2_k.}
\]
::: {.proof}
For affine schemes, fibre products are computed by tensor products of coordinate rings.  Thus
\[
\Spec k[x]
\times_{\Spec k}
\Spec k[y]
\cong
\Spec\bigl(k[x]\otimes_k k[y]\bigr).
\]
The natural map
\[
k[x]\otimes_k k[y]\longrightarrow k[x,y],
\qquad
p(x)\otimes q(y)\longmapsto p(x)q(y),
\]
is an isomorphism, giving the result.
:::

<1>2. Even when $k$ is algebraically closed, the underlying point set of $\mathbb A^2_k$ is not the Cartesian product of the underlying point sets of the two copies of $\mathbb A^1_k$.
::: {.proof}
Let $\eta\in\Spec k[x]$ and $\eta'\in\Spec k[y]$ be the generic points, corresponding to the zero ideals.

Under the two projections
\[
\Spec k[x,y]\longrightarrow\Spec k[x],
\qquad
\Spec k[x,y]\longrightarrow\Spec k[y],
\]
both of the distinct primes
\[
(0)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(x-y)
\]
map to
\[
(\eta,\eta').
\]
Indeed,
\[
(x-y)\cap k[x]=(0),
\qquad
(x-y)\cap k[y]=(0).
\]
Thus the natural map of point sets
\[
|\mathbb A^2_k|
\longrightarrow
|\mathbb A^1_k|\times|\mathbb A^1_k|
\]
is not injective.  Hence the point set of the scheme product is not the set-theoretic product, regardless of algebraic closedness of $k$.
:::

<1>3. For part (b),
\[
\boxed{
\Spec k(s)\times_{\Spec k}\Spec k(t)
\cong
\Spec\bigl(k(s)\otimes_k k(t)\bigr).}
\]
::: {.proof}
This is again the affine fibre-product formula.
:::

<1>4. The tensor product in <1>3 is the localization
\[
R=k(s)\otimes_k k(t)
\cong
S^{-1}k[s,t],
\]
where $S$ is the multiplicative set generated by all nonzero polynomials in $k[s]$ and all nonzero polynomials in $k[t]$.
::: {.proof}
Write
\[
k(s)=\bigl(k[s]\bigr)_{k[s]\setminus\{0\}},
\qquad
k(t)=\bigl(k[t]\bigr)_{k[t]\setminus\{0\}}.
\]
Tensor product commutes with localization, so localizing the two factors before tensoring is the same as first forming
\[
k[s]\otimes_k k[t]\cong k[s,t]
\]
and then inverting the images of all nonzero elements from the two one-variable polynomial rings.  This is exactly $S^{-1}k[s,t]$.
:::

<1>5. The primes of $R$ correspond to the prime ideals
\[
\mathfrak p\subseteq k[s,t]
\]
such that
\[
\mathfrak p\cap k[s]=(0),
\qquad
\mathfrak p\cap k[t]=(0).
\]
::: {.proof}
Prime ideals of a localization $S^{-1}A$ correspond to prime ideals of $A$ disjoint from $S$.  Here being disjoint from $S$ means containing no nonzero polynomial in $s$ alone and no nonzero polynomial in $t$ alone, which is precisely the displayed pair of contraction conditions.
:::

<1>6. The primes in <1>5 are exactly
\[
(0)
\]
and the height-one primes
\[
(h(s,t)),
\]
where $h\in k[s,t]$ is irreducible and depends nontrivially on both $s$ and $t$.
::: {.proof}
The zero ideal clearly satisfies the contraction conditions.

Let $\mathfrak p\ne(0)$ satisfy them.  Since $k[s,t]$ has dimension $2$, the prime has height $1$ or $2$.

It cannot have height $2$: then it is maximal, and the field
\[
k[s,t]/\mathfrak p
\]
is a finitely generated $k$-algebra.  By Zariski's lemma it is a finite algebraic extension of $k$.  The image of $s$ would therefore be algebraic over $k$, so some nonzero polynomial in $k[s]$ would lie in $\mathfrak p$, contradicting the contraction condition.

Hence $\mathfrak p$ has height $1$.  Since $k[s,t]$ is a unique factorization domain, every height-one prime is generated by an irreducible polynomial:
\[
\mathfrak p=(h).
\]
If $h$ depended only on $s$, or only on $t$, the corresponding contraction would be nonzero.  Thus $h$ must involve both variables.

Conversely, let $h$ be irreducible and involve both variables.  If a nonzero $p(s)\in k[s]$ lay in $(h)$, then $h$ would divide $p(s)$ in the UFD $k[s,t]$, forcing $h$ to be associated to a polynomial in $s$ alone, contradiction.  Thus
\[
(h)\cap k[s]=(0),
\]
and similarly for $k[t]$.  Hence $(h)$ survives the localization.
:::

<1>7. Therefore the product in part (b) is a one-dimensional scheme with one generic point and closed points indexed by irreducible mixed polynomials $h(s,t)$, up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar.
::: {.proof}
By <1>6, the zero prime is the unique generic point.  Every surviving nonzero prime has height one, and there are no larger surviving primes, so it becomes maximal in $R$ and hence is a closed point.

There are many such points.  For example,
\[
t-s^n,
\qquad n\ge1,
\]
are irreducible mixed polynomials and give distinct closed points.
:::

<1>8. At the closed point defined by an irreducible mixed polynomial $h(s,t)$, the residue field is
\[
\boxed{
\kappa(h)
\cong
\operatorname{Frac}\bigl(k[s,t]/(h)\bigr).}
\]
::: {.proof}
Because $h$ involves $t$, it is primitive as a polynomial in $t$ over $k[s]$ after multiplication by a scalar, and irreducibility in $k[s,t]$ implies irreducibility over $k(s)[t]$ by Gauss's lemma.  Localizing
\[
k[s,t]/(h)
\]
at all nonzero elements of $k[s]$ therefore gives the field
\[
k(s)[t]/(h).
\]
This is the fraction field of $k[s,t]/(h)$.  Further localization by nonzero elements from $k[t]$ does not change the field.  Hence it is the residue field of the corresponding maximal ideal of $R$.
:::

<1>9. In particular, although each of
\[
\Spec k(s),\qquad\Spec k(t),\qquad\Spec k
\]
has one point, their fibre product has infinitely many points.
::: {.proof}
Step <1>7 exhibits the generic point and infinitely many distinct closed points $(t-s^n)$.
:::

<1>10. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
Steps <1>1--<1>2 prove part (a), and steps <1>3--<1>9 describe the product in part (b).
