::: {.problem}
Let $Y^r \subseteq \PP^n$ be a variety of degree $2$.
Show that $Y$ is contained in a linear subspace $L$ of dimension $r + 1$ in $\PP^n$.
Thus $Y$ is isomorphic to a quadric hypersurface in $\PP^{r+1}$ (Ex. 5.12).
:::

::: {.solution}
::: pf

::: pf-step
The variety $Y$ has a nonsingular point.

::: pf-proof
By Theorem I.5.3 (printed p. 33), the singular locus of a variety is a proper closed subset.
Hence its complement is a nonempty open subset of $Y$.
Choose
$$
P\in\Reg Y.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #join-degree-one}
Let $X$ be the join of $P$ with $Y$ from Exercise I.7.7. Then
$$
\dim X=r+1,
\qquad
\deg X=1.
$$

::: pf-proof
Apply [[P-AGH77CONEDEGDROP|Exercise I.7.7]] to the degree-two variety $Y$ and the nonsingular point $P$: its hypotheses are those of the exercise, $Y$ being a variety of dimension $r$ and degree $2>1$ and $P$ a nonsingular point of it.
It gives
$$
\dim X=r+1
$$
and
$$
0<\deg X<\deg Y=2.
$$
The degree of a nonempty projective variety is a positive integer by Proposition I.7.6(a), printed p. 52, so necessarily
$$
\deg X=1.
$$
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #join-is-linear-span}
The variety $X$ is a linear subspace
$$
\boxed{L\cong\PP^{r+1}\subseteq\PP^n}
$$
containing $Y$.

::: pf-proof
By definition of the join, $Y\subseteq X$.
Step [](#join-degree-one){.pf-ref} makes $X$ a projective variety of degree one.

That a variety of degree one is linear is Borcherds, Exercise 7.6, PDF p. 9: "Any linear variety obviously has degree 1 (by calculating its Hilbert polynomial). Assume that $Y$ has degree 1. ... By theorem 7.7 if $H$ is any hyperplane then $Y\cap H$ also has degree 1 (or $Y\subset H$, in which case $Y$ is linear by induction on $n$). Therefore $Y\cap H$ is linear for every hyperplane $H$, and therefore for every linear variety $H$. In particular if $p,q\in Y$, then the intersection of $Y$ with the line $pq$ is linear and therefore is the line joining $p$ and $q$. Hence $Y$ contains any line joining two of its points, and is therefore linear."
His theorem 7.7 is the Bézout theorem of the book, printed p. 53: for a hypersurface $H$ not containing the variety,
$$
\sum_j i(Y,H;Z_j)\deg Z_j=(\deg Y)(\deg H),
$$
so that $\deg(Y\cap H)=1$ when $\deg Y=1$. His induction on $n$ is on the ambient dimension, and its hypothesis $\deg(Y\cap H)=1$ makes the section a single variety of degree one in $H\cong\PP^{n-1}$, to which the induction applies; when $Y$ lies in a hyperplane the same induction applies in that hyperplane.

His last sentence, made explicit: if $p,q\in X$, the line $\overline{pq}$ meets $X$ in a linear set containing $p$ and $q$, hence equals $\overline{pq}$; so the span of any finite set of points of $X$ lies in $X$, by induction on the number of points, that span being the union of the joins of the last point with the spans of the earlier ones. Since $X$ is spanned by finitely many of its own points, $X$ equals that span.

Thus $X$ is a linear subspace $L\subseteq\PP^n$.
Its dimension is $r+1$ by step [](#join-degree-one){.pf-ref}, and it contains $Y$.
This proves the first assertion.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #y-is-quadric-hypersurface}
Inside $L\cong\PP^{r+1}$, the variety $Y$ is a quadric hypersurface.

::: pf-proof
The inclusion
$$
Y^r\subseteq L^{r+1}
$$
has codimension one, and a variety of pure codimension one in $\PP^{r+1}$ is a hypersurface: Harris, Example 11.7, printed p. 136, "a variety $X\subset\PP^n$ of pure codimension 1 is a hypersurface. This follows from the fact that a minimal prime ideal in the ring $K[Z_0,\ldots,Z_n]$ is principal. It also follows that the homogeneous ideal of a hypersurface is generated by a single element."
So $Y=V(F)$ for an irreducible homogeneous polynomial $F$ on $L$, its homogeneous coordinate ring being the polynomial ring $k[x_0,\ldots,x_{r+1}]$.

For a hypersurface, the degree is the degree of its defining form: Harris, the paragraphs before Definition 18.1, printed p. 225, "if $X\subset\PP^{k+1}$ is a hypersurface, given as the zero locus of an irreducible polynomial $F(Z)$, then the degree of $X$ is the degree of the polynomial $F$"; equivalently Proposition I.7.6(d), printed p. 52. That proposition computes the degree of $Y$ as a subvariety of $L\cong\PP^{r+1}$, and this is the degree of $Y$ as a subvariety of $\PP^n$ because the two graded rings are the same: after a linear change of coordinates $L=V(x_{r+2},\ldots,x_n)$, and then
$$
S(Y)=\frac{k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]}{(I(Y),x_{r+2},\ldots,x_n)}\cong\frac{k[x_0,\ldots,x_{r+1}]}{I_L(Y)},
$$
so the Hilbert function, and with it the degree $r!$ times the leading coefficient of the Hilbert polynomial (the definition of degree in §I.7, text version `tmp/sources/hartshorne-1977-algebraic-geometry.md`, line 1779), is unchanged by passing from $L$ to $\PP^n$.
Since
$$
\deg Y=2,
$$
we obtain
$$
\deg F=2.
$$
Thus $Y$ is a quadric hypersurface in $L\cong\PP^{r+1}$.
:::
