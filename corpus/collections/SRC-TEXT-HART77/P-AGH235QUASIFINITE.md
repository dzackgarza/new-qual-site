::: {.problem}
A morphism $f: X \to Y$ is **quasi-finite** if for every point $y \in Y$ the fibre $\inverseof{f}(y)$ is a finite set.

a. Show that a finite morphism is quasi-finite.

b. Show that a finite morphism is closed, that is, the image of any closed subset is closed.

c. Show by example that a surjective, finite-type, quasi-finite morphism need not be finite.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Let $f:X\to Y$ be finite and let $y\in Y$.
Then the scheme-theoretic fibre
\[
X_y=X\times_Y\Spec\kappa(y)
\]
is the spectrum of a finite-dimensional $\kappa(y)$-algebra.

::: {.proof}
Choose an affine neighborhood
\[
V=\Spec B\subseteq Y
\]
of $y$.
Since $f$ is finite, Hartshorne II.3.4 gives
\[
f^{-1}(V)=\Spec A
\]
with $A$ finite as a $B$-module.

The fibre is
\[
X_y
\cong
\Spec\bigl(A\otimes_B\kappa(y)\bigr).
\]
Because $A$ is generated by finitely many elements as a $B$-module, the tensor product is finite-dimensional as a vector space over $\kappa(y)$.
:::

<1>2. A finite-dimensional algebra over a field has only finitely many prime ideals.

::: {.proof}
Such an algebra is Artinian as a ring.  In an Artinian ring every prime ideal is maximal, and there are only finitely many maximal ideals: otherwise the descending chain obtained from successive finite products of distinct maximal ideals would not stabilize.  Hence its spectrum is a finite set.
:::

<1>3. Every finite morphism is quasi-finite.

::: {.proof}
For each $y\in Y$, <1>1--<1>2 show that the fibre $X_y$ has finitely many points.  Its underlying set is naturally the set-theoretic fibre $f^{-1}(y)$.  Thus every fibre of $f$ is finite.
:::

<1>4. Let $f:X\to Y$ be finite.
Then $f$ is a closed map.

::: {.proof}
It suffices to check closedness over an affine open cover of $Y$.
Let
\[
V=\Spec B\subseteq Y,
\qquad
f^{-1}(V)=\Spec A,
\]
with $A$ finite over $B$.
Let
\[
Z\cap f^{-1}(V)=V(J)\subseteq\Spec A
\]
for an ideal $J\subseteq A$.

The quotient $A/J$ is integral over the image of $B/(J\cap B)$ because finite ring extensions are integral and integrality passes to quotients.
The induced map
\[
B/(J\cap B)\hookrightarrow A/J
\]
is injective.
By lying over, every prime of $B/(J\cap B)$ is the contraction of a prime of $A/J$.
Hence the image of $V(J)$ in $\Spec B$ is exactly
\[
V(J\cap B),
\]
which is closed.

Thus $f(Z)\cap V$ is closed in every affine member $V$ of an open cover of $Y$.  Therefore $f(Z)$ is closed in $Y$.
:::

<1>5. Let $k$ be a field and define
\[
X=\mathbb G_{m,k}\amalg\Spec k,
\qquad
Y=\mathbb A^1_k.
\]
Map the first component to $Y$ by the usual open immersion
\[
\mathbb G_m=D(t)\hookrightarrow\mathbb A^1
\]
and map the second component to the origin.
Then
\[
f:X\longrightarrow Y
\]
is surjective, of finite type, and quasi-finite.

::: {.proof}
The image of the first component is $Y\setminus\{0\}$, and the second component maps to $0$, so $f$ is surjective.

Both component maps are of finite type: the open immersion corresponds to
\[
k[t]\longrightarrow k[t,t^{-1}],
\]
which is generated as a $k[t]$-algebra by $t^{-1}$, while the point inclusion corresponds to the quotient
\[
k[t]\longrightarrow k[t]/(t)\cong k.
\]
Since $X$ is the finite disjoint union of these two components, $f$ is of finite type.

For $a\ne0$, the fibre over $a$ consists of the single point $a\in\mathbb G_m$.  The fibre over $0$ consists of the single extra point $\Spec k$.  Hence every fibre is finite, so $f$ is quasi-finite.
:::

<1>6. The morphism in <1>5 is not finite.

::: {.proof}
The component
\[
\mathbb G_m\subseteq X
\]
is closed, because it is a component of a disjoint union.
Its image is
\[
\mathbb A^1\setminus\{0\},
\]
which is not closed in $\mathbb A^1$.

By <1>4, every finite morphism is closed.  Therefore this morphism cannot be finite.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>3 proves part (a), step <1>4 proves part (b), and steps <1>5--<1>6 give the counterexample required in part (c).
:::
