::: {.problem}
Let $k$ be algebraically closed and let $Y \subseteq \AA_k^3$ be the curve given parametrically by $x = t^3$, $y = t^4$, $z = t^5$.
Show that $I(Y)$ is a prime ideal of height $2$ in $k[x,y,z]$ which cannot be generated by $2$ elements.
We say $Y$ is not a local complete intersection; cf. (Ex. 2.17).
:::

::: {.solution}
Put $S=k[x,y,z]$, $\mathfrak m=(x,y,z)$, and define
$$
\phi:S\longrightarrow k[t],\qquad
x\longmapsto t^3,\quad y\longmapsto t^4,\quad z\longmapsto t^5.
$$
Since $k$ is infinite, a polynomial in $k[t]$ vanishing at every element of $k$ is zero.
Thus $I(Y)=\ker\phi$.

<1>1. The defining ideal is
$$
\boxed{I(Y)=(y^2-xz,\ yz-x^3,\ z^2-x^2y).}
$$

::: {.proof}
Let $J$ be the ideal displayed in the claim.
Substitution in each generator gives zero, so $J\subseteq\ker\phi$.
Modulo $J$, the replacements
$$
y^2=xz,\qquad yz=x^3,\qquad z^2=x^2y
$$
reduce the total exponent of $y$ and $z$ in a monomial whenever that exponent is at least two.
Repeating these replacements therefore expresses every element of $S/J$ as
$$
p(x)+q(x)y+r(x)z\qquad(p,q,r\in k[x]).
$$
Its image under $\phi$ is
$$
p(t^3)+t^4q(t^3)+t^5r(t^3).
$$
The exponents in these three summands are congruent respectively to $0,1,2$ modulo $3$.
They cannot cancel between summands, and distinct powers within each summand are also linearly independent over $k$.
Hence a zero image forces $p=q=r=0$.
It follows that $S/J\to k[t]$ is injective, giving $J=\ker\phi$ as claimed.

The parametrized image is itself closed.
Indeed, at a common zero of these equations with $x\ne0$, set $t=y/x$.
The equations give $t^3=x$, then $t^4=y$ and $t^5=z$.
If $x=0$, the equations $y^2=0$ and $z^2=0$ give the origin, corresponding to $t=0$.
Thus the image is exactly $V(J)$.
:::

<1>2. The ideal $I(Y)$ is prime and has height $2$.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>1 identifies the quotient with the subring
$$
S/I(Y)\cong k[t^3,t^4,t^5]\subseteq k[t].
$$
This is a domain, so $I(Y)$ is prime.
Its fraction field is $k(t)$: it contains $t=t^4/t^3$ and is contained in $k(t)$.
Its transcendence degree over $k$ is therefore one.
The dimension formula for a prime ideal in a polynomial ring gives
$$
\operatorname{height}I(Y)
=3-\operatorname{trdeg}_k\operatorname{Frac}(S/I(Y))=2
$$
[@Har10a, Chapter I, §1].
:::

<1>3. The ideal $I(Y)S_{\mathfrak m}$ needs exactly three generators in the local ring $S_{\mathfrak m}$.

::: {.proof}
Write $F=y^2-xz$, $G=yz-x^3$ and $H=z^2-x^2y$.
Their classes generate the $k$-vector space $I(Y)/\mathfrak m I(Y)$ by step <1>1.
They are linearly independent in that space.
Indeed, $I(Y)\subseteq\mathfrak m^2$, so $\mathfrak m I(Y)\subseteq\mathfrak m^3$.
If $aF+bG+cH\in\mathfrak m I(Y)$ for $a,b,c\in k$, its homogeneous quadratic part must vanish:
$$
a(y^2-xz)+b yz+c z^2=0.
$$
The coefficients of $y^2$, $yz$ and $z^2$ give $a=b=c=0$.
Consequently $\dim_k I(Y)/\mathfrak m I(Y)=3$.

Localization gives
$$
I(Y)S_{\mathfrak m}/\mathfrak m S_{\mathfrak m}\,I(Y)S_{\mathfrak m}
\cong I(Y)/\mathfrak m I(Y),
$$
because the right-hand module is a $k=S/\mathfrak m$-vector space and every element outside $\mathfrak m$ acts on it by a nonzero scalar.
Any generating family for the localized ideal must span this three-dimensional quotient, so it has at least three elements.
The three displayed polynomials already generate the ideal, proving the assertion.
In particular, a two-element global generating family is impossible, since it would remain a generating family after localization.
:::

<1>4. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>2 proves primality and height two.
Step <1>3 proves that the ideal cannot be generated by two elements even at the origin.
Its codimension there is two, so this also proves failure of the local complete-intersection condition at that point.
:::
