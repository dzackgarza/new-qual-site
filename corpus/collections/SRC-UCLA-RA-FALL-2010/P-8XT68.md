::: {.problem}
Prove the following form of Jensen's inequality: If $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous then $$\int_0^1 e^{f(x)}\,dx\ge\exp\left(\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx\right).$$ Moreover, if equality occurs then $f$ is a constant function.
:::

::: {.solution}
**Part 1: The Inequality.**

<1>1. Let $\mu = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx \in \mathbb{R}$.
Proof: $f$ is continuous on the compact interval $[0,1]$, hence integrable.

<1>2. The exponential function $\phi(t) = e^t$ is strictly convex on $\mathbb{R}$.
Proof: $\phi''(t) = e^t > 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$.

<1>3. For every $t \in \mathbb{R}$, $e^t \ge e^\mu + e^\mu(t - \mu)$, with equality if and only if $t = \mu$.
Proof: the tangent line to the strictly convex graph $y = e^t$ at $t = \mu$ is $y = e^\mu + e^\mu(t - \mu)$; by strict convexity, the graph lies strictly above its tangent line except at the point of tangency.

<1>4. Setting $t = f(x)$ gives $e^{f(x)} \ge e^\mu + e^\mu(f(x) - \mu)$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.
Proof: <1>3 evaluated at $t = f(x)$.

<1>5. Integrating over $[0,1]$ yields $\int_0^1 e^{f(x)}\,dx \ge \exp\left(\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx\right)$.
<2>1. Integrating both sides of <1>4:
\[
\int_0^1 e^{f(x)}\,dx \ge \int_0^1 \bigl(e^\mu + e^\mu(f(x) - \mu)\bigr)\,dx.
\]
Proof: monotonicity of the Riemann / Lebesgue integral.
<2>2. Compute the right-hand integral:
\[
\int_0^1 \bigl(e^\mu + e^\mu(f(x) - \mu)\bigr)\,dx = e^\mu \int_0^1 1\,dx + e^\mu \left(\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx - \mu\right) = e^\mu + e^\mu(\mu - \mu) = e^\mu.
\]
Proof: linearity of the integral and the definition $\mu = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$.
<2>3. Since $e^\mu = \exp\left(\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx\right)$, the inequality holds.
Proof: <2>1 and <2>2.

**Part 2: The Equality Case.**

<1>6. If $\int_0^1 e^{f(x)}\,dx = \exp\left(\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx\right)$, then $f$ is a constant function.
<2>1. Consider the difference function $g(x) = e^{f(x)} - \bigl(e^\mu + e^\mu(f(x) - \mu)\bigr)$ on $[0,1]$.
Proof: definition.
<2>2. $g(x)$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and $g(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.
Proof: $f$ is continuous, $\exp$ and linear maps are continuous, and <1>4 gives nonnegativity.
<2>3. $\int_0^1 g(x)\,dx = \int_0^1 e^{f(x)}\,dx - e^\mu = 0$.
Proof: hypothesis of equality and <2>2 of Part 1. <2>4. A continuous, nonnegative function on $[0,1]$ with zero integral must vanish identically: $g(x) = 0$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.
Proof: standard real analysis: if $g(x_0) > 0$, by continuity $g(x) > g(x_0)/2$ on an interval $(\alpha,\beta) \subseteq [0,1]$, forcing $\int_0^1 g > 0$.
<2>5. By <1>3, $g(x) = 0$ implies $f(x) = \mu$.
Proof: the only solution to $e^t = e^\mu + e^\mu(t - \mu)$ is $t = \mu$.
<2>6. Thus $f(x) = \mu = \int_0^1 f(t)\,dt$ for all $x \in [0,1]$, so $f$ is constant.
Proof: <2>4 and <2>5.

<1>7. Q.E.D. Proof: <1>5 and <1>6.
