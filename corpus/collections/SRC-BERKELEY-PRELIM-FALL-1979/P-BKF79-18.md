    If A=BQ is symmetric, then A^2=AA^T=B^2. Hence A commutes with
    B^2, whose three diagonal entries are distinct, so A is diagonal.
    Its entries differ from those of B only by signs on nonzero
    coordinates. The determinant-one condition gives four matrices;
    if one diagonal entry of B is zero, its unused sign can always be
    chosen to make determinant one.

::: {.problem}
Let
\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}
a&0&0\\
0&b&0\\
0&0&c
\end{pmatrix},
\]
where $a,b,c\in\mathbb R$ and $|a|,|b|,|c|$ are distinct. Show that there are exactly four symmetric matrices of the form $BQ$, where $Q$ is a real orthogonal matrix with determinant $1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let
$$
A=BQ,
$$
where $Q\in SO(3)$, and suppose that $A$ is symmetric.

<1>1. One has
$$
A^2=B^2.
$$

::: {.proof}
Since $A$ is symmetric,
$$
A^2=AA^T.
$$
On the other hand,
$$
AA^T
=
BQ(BQ)^T
=
BQQ^TB
=
B^2,
$$
because $Q$ is orthogonal and $B^T=B$.
:::

<1>2. The matrix $A$ commutes with $B^2$.

::: {.proof}
By step <1>1,
$$
AB^2
=
AA^2
=
A^3
=
A^2A
=
B^2A.
$$
:::

<1>3. The matrix $A$ is diagonal.

::: {.proof}
Write
$$
B^2
=
\operatorname{diag}(a^2,b^2,c^2).
$$
The numbers $a^2,b^2,c^2$ are pairwise distinct because
$\abs a,\abs b,\abs c$ are pairwise distinct. If
$A=(A_{ij})$, the equality
$$
AB^2=B^2A
$$
from step <1>2 gives, for $i\neq j$,
$$
A_{ij}(d_j-d_i)=0,
$$
where $(d_1,d_2,d_3)=(a^2,b^2,c^2)$. Since $d_i\neq d_j$, every
off-diagonal entry $A_{ij}$ is zero.
:::

<1>4. If $a,b,c$ are all nonzero, there are exactly four possible
matrices $A$.

::: {.proof}
By steps <1>1 and <1>3,
$$
A
=
\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1a,\epsilon_2b,\epsilon_3c),
\qquad
\epsilon_i\in\{1,-1\}.
$$
Since $B$ is invertible,
$$
Q=B^{-1}A
=
\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3).
$$
Thus
$$
\det Q=\epsilon_1\epsilon_2\epsilon_3.
$$
The condition $\det Q=1$ is therefore
$$
\epsilon_1\epsilon_2\epsilon_3=1.
$$
Exactly four of the eight sign triples satisfy this condition, and
each gives a distinct symmetric matrix $A=BQ$.
:::

<1>5. If one of $a,b,c$ is zero, there are exactly four possible
matrices $A$.

::: {.proof}
At most one of $a,b,c$ can vanish because their absolute values are
pairwise distinct. After relabeling coordinates, suppose $a=0$.
Steps <1>1 and <1>3 then force
$$
A
=
\operatorname{diag}(0,\epsilon_2b,\epsilon_3c),
\qquad
\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3\in\{1,-1\}.
$$
Hence there are at most four possibilities.

Conversely, for every choice of $\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3$, set
$$
\epsilon_1=\epsilon_2\epsilon_3
$$
and
$$
Q
=
\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3).
$$
Then $Q$ is orthogonal and
$$
\det Q
=
\epsilon_1\epsilon_2\epsilon_3
=
1,
$$
while
$$
BQ
=
\operatorname{diag}(0,\epsilon_2b,\epsilon_3c).
$$
Thus all four possibilities occur.
:::

<1>6. There are exactly four symmetric matrices of the required form.

::: {.proof}
Either none of $a,b,c$ is zero, in which case step <1>4 applies, or
exactly one is zero, in which case step <1>5 applies.
:::

<1>7. Q.E.D.

::: {.proof}
Step <1>6 is the required conclusion.
:::
