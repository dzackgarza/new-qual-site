    {empty,{c},{a,c},{b,c},X} is nonnormal because the disjoint closed sets

    topology is normal. Compactness does descend because every open cover for
    the coarser topology is also an open cover for the finer topology.

::: {.problem}
Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ and $(X,\mathcal T')$ be topological spaces with $\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal T'$.

(a) If $(X,\mathcal T')$ is normal, must $(X,\mathcal T)$ also be normal?

(b) If $(X,\mathcal T')$ is compact, must $(X,\mathcal T)$ also be compact?

Prove your answers in both parts.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. The answer to part (a) is no: normality of the finer topology need not imply normality of the coarser topology.
::: {.proof}
Let
\[
X=\{a,b,c\}
\]
and define
\[
\mathcal T
=
\{\varnothing,\{c\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\},X\}.
\]
This is a topology: unions of its members remain in the displayed list, and finite intersections do as well.

Let $\mathcal T'$ be the discrete topology on $X$.
Then
\[
\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal T'.
\]
The discrete space $(X,\mathcal T')$ is normal, since any two disjoint closed sets are themselves disjoint open neighborhoods of one another.

In $(X,\mathcal T)$, the sets
\[
\{a\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\{b\}
\]
are closed because their complements
\[
\{b,c\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\{a,c\}
\]
belong to $\mathcal T$.
They are disjoint.

Every open neighborhood of $a$ contains $c$: the only such neighborhoods are $\{a,c\}$ and $X$.
Similarly every open neighborhood of $b$ contains $c$.
Thus no open neighborhoods of $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ can be disjoint.
Therefore $(X,\mathcal T)$ is not normal.
:::

<1>2. The answer to part (b) is yes: if $(X,\mathcal T')$ is compact and $\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal T'$, then $(X,\mathcal T)$ is compact.
::: {.proof}
Let $\mathcal U$ be an arbitrary open cover of $X$ by sets belonging to the coarser topology $\mathcal T$.
Because
\[
\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal T',
\]
every member of $\mathcal U$ is also open in the finer topology $\mathcal T'$.
Hence $\mathcal U$ is an open cover of the compact space $(X,\mathcal T')$.
There are therefore finitely many members
\[
U_1,\ldots,U_n\in\mathcal U
\]
with
\[
X=U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_n.
\]
These same sets form a finite subcover in the coarser topology.
Since $\mathcal U$ was arbitrary, $(X,\mathcal T)$ is compact.
:::
