::: {.proposition}
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $I, J\subseteq R$ be [[D-GOFWL|ideals]].

(a) $I + J = \theset{i+j \suchthat i\in I, j\in J}$ is the smallest ideal containing $I$ and $J$.

(b) $IJ \coloneqq \theset{\sum_{k=1}^N x_k y_k \suchthat N\geq 0,\ x_k\in I,\ y_k \in J}$ is an ideal, the ideal generated by the products $xy$ with $x\in I$ and $y\in J$, and $IJ\subseteq I\cap J$.

(c) $I \cap J$ is an ideal.

(d) If $I+J = R$, then $I \cap J = IJ$.
:::

::: {.proof}
(a)--(c) Each set contains $0$ and is closed under subtraction and under multiplication by elements of $R$; any ideal containing $I$ and $J$ contains all sums $i+j$, and any ideal containing all products $xy$ contains their finite sums.
Each product $xy$ lies in $I$ and in $J$, so $IJ\subseteq I\cap J$.

(d) Write $1=i+j$ with $i\in I$ and $j\in J$.
For $x\in I\cap J$, $x=xi+xj$, where $xi\in JI=IJ$ and $xj\in IJ$, so $x\in IJ$.
:::

::: {.example}
A union of ideals need not be an ideal: in $\ZZ$, $2\in 2\ZZ\cup3\ZZ$ and $3\in2\ZZ\cup3\ZZ$, but $2+3=5\notin2\ZZ\cup3\ZZ$.
:::
