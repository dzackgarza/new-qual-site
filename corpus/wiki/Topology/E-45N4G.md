::: exercise
- What is a **nowhere dense** subspace?
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Provide the formal definition, equivalent characterizations, key examples, and topological significance of a **nowhere dense** subset.

<1>1. Definition:
    A subset $A$ of a topological space $X$ is said to be **nowhere dense** (or *rare*) in $X$ if the interior of its topological closure is empty:
    $$\operatorname{Int}(\overline{A}) = \varnothing.$$

<1>2. Equivalent characterizations:
    Let $A \subseteq X$. The following conditions are mutually equivalent:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. $\operatorname{Int}(\overline{A}) = \varnothing$.
    <2>2. The complement of the closure, $X \setminus \overline{A}$, is dense in $X$:
        $$\overline{X \setminus \overline{A}} = X.$$
        *Reason:* For any set $E$, $\overline{X \setminus E} = X \setminus \operatorname{Int}(E)$. Setting $E = \overline{A}$, $\overline{X \setminus \overline{A}} = X \setminus \operatorname{Int}(\overline{A}) = X \setminus \varnothing = X$.
    <2>3. Every non-empty open set $U \subseteq X$ contains a non-empty open subset $V \subseteq U$ that is disjoint from $A$ ($V \cap A = \varnothing$).
        *Reason:* Since $X \setminus \overline{A}$ is dense and open, $U \cap (X \setminus \overline{A})$ is non-empty and open, and $(U \cap (X \setminus \overline{A})) \cap A = \varnothing$.
    <2>4. The closure $\overline{A}$ contains no non-empty open subset of $X$.

<1>3. Examples and non-examples in $\mathbb{R}$:
    - **Nowhere dense:**
      - Finite subsets and isolated sets, such as $\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{R}$ ($\overline{\mathbb{Z}} = \mathbb{Z}$, $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb{Z}) = \varnothing$).
      - Convergent sequence sets $S = \{1/n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}_+\} \subset \mathbb{R}$ ($\overline{S} = S \cup \{0\}$, $\operatorname{Int}(\overline{S}) = \varnothing$).
      - The standard Cantor ternary set $C \subset [0, 1]$ is uncountable, compact, and nowhere dense ($\operatorname{Int}(C) = \varnothing$).
    - **Not nowhere dense:**
      - The set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$ is dense, so $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{R}$ and $\operatorname{Int}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}) = \mathbb{R} \neq \varnothing$ (even though $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb{Q}) = \varnothing$).

<1>4. Significance in Baire Category Theory:
    - Countable unions of nowhere dense sets are called **meager** (or **first category**).
    - The Baire Category Theorem guarantees that no non-empty open subset of a complete metric space or locally compact Hausdorff space is meager. Q.E.D.
:::
