::: problem
- Prove the following implications of separation axioms, and show that they are strict: ![](../../assets/Workshops/Topology/_attachments/Pasted%20image%2020210520150233.png)
:::

::: {.solution}
**Goal.** Prove the implications $T_4 \Rightarrow T_3 \Rightarrow T_2 \Rightarrow T_1 \Rightarrow T_0$ and show each is strict.

<1>1. $T_4 \Rightarrow T_3$.
<2>1. $T_4$ means normal and $T_1$; $T_3$ means regular and $T_1$.
::: {.proof}
definitions.
:::
<2>2. A normal $T_1$ space is regular.
::: {.proof}
given a closed set $C$ and a point $x \notin C$, since the space is $T_1$ the singleton $\theset{x}$ is closed, and normality separates the disjoint closed sets $C$ and $\theset{x}$ by disjoint open sets.
:::

<1>2. $T_3 \Rightarrow T_2$.
<2>1. A regular $T_1$ space is Hausdorff.
::: {.proof}
given distinct $x, y$, $T_1$ makes $\theset{y}$ closed and $x \notin \theset{y}$; regularity separates $x$ and $\theset{y}$ by disjoint open sets.
:::

<1>3. $T_2 \Rightarrow T_1$.
<2>1. A Hausdorff space is $T_1$.
::: {.proof}
given distinct $x, y$, Hausdorff gives disjoint open $U \ni x$, $V \ni y$; then $x \notin V$ and $y \notin U$, so each singleton is closed.
:::

<1>4. $T_1 \Rightarrow T_0$.
<2>1. A $T_1$ space is $T_0$.
::: {.proof}
given distinct $x, y$, $T_1$ gives an open set containing $x$ but not $y$ (namely $X \sm \theset{y}$).
:::

<1>5. Each implication is strict.
<2>1. $T_0$ but not $T_1$: the Sierpiński space $\theset{0,1}$ with open sets $\theset{\emptyset, \theset{1}, \theset{0,1}}$.
::: {.proof}
$1$ has a neighborhood not containing $0$, but $\theset{0}$ is not closed.
:::
<2>2. $T_1$ but not $T_2$: the cofinite topology on an infinite set.
::: {.proof}
singletons are closed, but any two nonempty open sets intersect.
:::
<2>3. $T_2$ but not $T_3$: let $K=\{1/n:n\ge1\}$ and give $\mathbb R$ the $K$-topology generated by the basis
$$
\{(a,b):a<b\}\;\cup\;\{(a,b)\setminus K:a<b\}.
$$
This space is Hausdorff but not regular.
::: {.proof}
The $K$-topology contains the usual topology, so it is Hausdorff. Also $K$ is closed: for every $x\notin K$, some basis set of the form $(a,b)\setminus K$ contains $x$ and misses $K$.

Suppose regularity separated the point $0$ from the closed set $K$ by disjoint open sets $U\ni0$ and $V\supseteq K$. Shrinking $U$ to a basic neighborhood, we may choose $\varepsilon>0$ with
$$
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\setminus K\subseteq U.
$$
Choose $1/n<\varepsilon/2$. Since $1/n\in V$, a basic neighborhood of $1/n$ is contained in $V$. Any basic neighborhood containing $1/n$ must contain an ordinary interval $(c,d)$ about $1/n$, because sets of the form $(a,b)\setminus K$ omit $1/n$. This interval contains a point
$$
y\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\setminus K,
$$
so $y\in U\cap V$, a contradiction. Thus the space is not regular.
:::
<2>4. $T_3$ but not $T_4$: the Sorgenfrey plane $S\times S$, where $S$ is $\mathbb R$ with basis $[a,b)$.
::: {.proof}
First, $S$ has a basis of clopen sets: each $[a,b)$ is open, and its complement
$$
(-\infty,a)\cup[b,\infty)
$$
is open in the Sorgenfrey topology. Hence $S$ is regular and $T_1$. Products of clopen basic sets form a clopen basis for $S\times S$, so the Sorgenfrey plane is also regular and $T_1$, hence $T_3$.

It remains to prove that it is not normal. The countable set $\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q$ is dense, since every basic rectangle $[a,b)\times[c,d)$ contains a rational pair. On the other hand, the anti-diagonal
$$
D=\{(x,-x):x\in\mathbb R\}
$$
is a closed discrete subspace of cardinality $\mathfrak c$. It is discrete because
$$
[x,x+\varepsilon)\times[-x,-x+\varepsilon)
$$
meets $D$ only at $(x,-x)$. It is closed because, if $a+b\ne0$, a sufficiently small basic rectangle about $(a,b)$ keeps the sign of the coordinate sum and therefore misses $D$.

Now use the following Jones lemma. A separable normal $T_1$ space cannot contain a closed discrete subspace of cardinality $\mathfrak c$. Indeed, if $D$ were such a subspace, then every subset $E\subseteq D$ and its complement $D\setminus E$ would be disjoint closed subsets of the ambient space. By Urysohn's lemma there would be a continuous map $f_E$ to $[0,1]$ taking value $0$ on $E$ and $1$ on $D\setminus E$. Distinct subsets give distinct continuous maps, so there would be at least $2^{\mathfrak c}$ continuous maps to $[0,1]$. But on a separable space a continuous map to the Hausdorff space $[0,1]$ is determined by its values on a fixed countable dense subset, so there are at most
$$
|[0,1]|^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c
$$
such maps, contradicting Cantor's theorem $2^{\mathfrak c}>\mathfrak c$.

Thus the separable Sorgenfrey plane is not normal, so it is $T_3$ but not $T_4$.
:::

<1>6. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
<1>1–<1>4 prove the implications; <1>5 shows each is strict.
:::
