::: {.problem}
Let all Galois extensions below be finite.

(a) Show that the splitting field of a separable polynomial is Galois.

(b) Show that every quadratic extension $K/F$ with $\ch F\ne2$ is Galois.

(c) Let $F\subseteq E\subseteq K$ with $K/F$ Galois. Show that $K/E$ is Galois and
$$
\Gal(K/E)\le\Gal(K/F).
$$
Show moreover that
$$
E/F\text{ is Galois}\iff \Gal(K/E)\normal\Gal(K/F),
$$
and in that case
$$
\Gal(E/F)\cong
\Gal(K/F)/\Gal(K/E).
$$

(d) If $E/k$ and $F/k$ are Galois and $E\cap F=k$, show that $EF/k$ is Galois and
$$
\Gal(EF/k)\cong\Gal(E/k)\times\Gal(F/k).
$$
:::

::: {.solution}

::: pf

::: pf-step
(a) The splitting field $K$ over $F$ of a separable polynomial $f\in F[x]$ is Galois over $F$.

::: pf-proof
The splitting field of a polynomial is a finite normal extension.
$K$ is generated over $F$ by the roots of $f$, which are separable over $F$, and an extension generated by separable elements is separable.
A finite normal separable extension is Galois.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
(b) A quadratic extension $K/F$ with $\ch F\ne2$ is Galois.

::: pf-proof
Choose $\alpha\in K\setminus F$; then $K=F(\alpha)$ and the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ is some $f(x)=x^2+bx+c$.
The other root of $f$ is $-b-\alpha\in K$, so $K$ is the splitting field of $f$.
Its roots $\alpha$ and $-b-\alpha$ are distinct, since otherwise $2\alpha=-b$ and $\alpha=-b/2\in F$ because $2$ is invertible in $F$.
So $f$ is separable, and $K/F$ is Galois by (a).
:::

:::

::: pf-step
(c) $K/E$ is Galois, $\Gal(K/E)\le\Gal(K/F)$, $E/F$ is Galois exactly when $\Gal(K/E)\normal\Gal(K/F)$, and then $\Gal(E/F)\cong\Gal(K/F)/\Gal(K/E)$.

::: pf-proof
If $K$ is the splitting field over $F$ of a separable polynomial $f$, then it is also the splitting field of $f$ over $E$, so $K/E$ is Galois by (a).
An $E$-automorphism of $K$ fixes $F\subseteq E$, so $\Gal(K/E)\le\Gal(K/F)$.
The equivalence and the isomorphism are part of the fundamental theorem of Galois theory: with $G=\Gal(K/F)$ and $H=\Gal(K/E)$, $E/F$ is Galois if and only if $H\normal G$, and then restriction $G\to\Gal(E/F)$ is surjective with kernel $H$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #compositum-galois}
(d) $EF/k$ is Galois.

::: pf-proof
If $E$ and $F$ are the splitting fields over $k$ of separable polynomials $f$ and $g$, then $EF$ is the splitting field of the separable part of $fg$, so $EF/k$ is Galois by (a).
:::

:::

::: pf-step
(d) $\Gal(EF/k)\cong\Gal(E/k)\times\Gal(F/k)$.

::: pf-proof
By step [](#compositum-galois){.pf-ref} restriction defines a homomorphism $\rho:\Gal(EF/k)\to\Gal(E/k)\times\Gal(F/k)$.
It is injective, because an automorphism of $EF$ fixing both $E$ and $F$ fixes the field they generate.
Since $E/k$ is Galois and $E\cap F=k$, the natural theorem on compositums gives $\Gal(EF/F)\cong\Gal(E/E\cap F)=\Gal(E/k)$, so $[EF:k]=[EF:F][F:k]=[E:k][F:k]$.
Thus $\rho$ is an injective map between finite groups of the same order, hence an isomorphism.
:::

:::

:::

:::

::: {.remark}
Part (b) also follows by completing the square, which uses $\ch F\ne2$ directly.
If $\alpha$ has minimal polynomial $x^2+bx+c$, then $\beta=\alpha+b/2$ satisfies $K=F(\beta)$ and $\beta^2=d\coloneqq(b^2-4c)/4\in F$.
So $K$ is the splitting field of $x^2-d$, whose roots $\pm\beta$ are distinct because $\beta\ne0$ and $\ch F\ne2$.
:::
