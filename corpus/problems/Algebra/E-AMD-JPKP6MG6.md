::: {.exercise}
Show that every permutation in $S_n$ is a product of disjoint cycles.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Prove that every permutation $\sigma \in S_n$ can be written as a product of disjoint cycles.

<1>1. Orbit decomposition: *Proof:* <2>1. The permutation $\sigma$ acts on $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$.
Define the orbit of $i$ under $\sigma$ as $\mathcal{O}(i) = \{i, \sigma(i), \sigma^2(i), \dots\}$.
<2>2. Because $\{1, \dots, n\}$ is finite, the orbit $\mathcal{O}(i)$ is finite.
Let $k$ be the smallest positive integer such that $\sigma^k(i) = i$.
<2>3. Then $\mathcal{O}(i) = \{i, \sigma(i), \dots, \sigma^{k-1}(i)\}$ has $k$ distinct elements, and $\sigma$ acts on $\mathcal{O}(i)$ as the $k$-cycle $(i \ \sigma(i) \ \sigma^2(i) \ \cdots \ \sigma^{k-1}(i))$.

<1>2. Disjointness: *Proof:* <2>1. The orbits of distinct elements are either equal or disjoint: if $j \in \mathcal{O}(i)$, then $\mathcal{O}(j) = \mathcal{O}(i)$.
<2>2. Thus $\{1, \dots, n\}$ decomposes as a disjoint union of orbits: $$\{1, \dots, n\} = \mathcal{O}(i_1) \sqcup \mathcal{O}(i_2) \sqcup \cdots \sqcup \mathcal{O}(i_m).$$

<1>3. Factoring $\sigma$ as a product of disjoint cycles: *Proof:* <2>1. For each orbit $\mathcal{O}(i_j) = \{i_j, \sigma(i_j), \dots, \sigma^{k_j - 1}(i_j)\}$, define the cycle $c_j = (i_j \ \sigma(i_j) \ \cdots \ \sigma^{k_j - 1}(i_j))$.
<2>2. Each $c_j$ permutes only the elements of $\mathcal{O}(i_j)$ and fixes all other elements.
<2>3. Since the orbits are pairwise disjoint, the cycles $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_m$ are pairwise disjoint.
<2>4. On each element $x \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, exactly one cycle $c_j$ moves $x$ (the one whose orbit contains $x$), and $c_j(x) = \sigma(x)$.
<2>5. Therefore $\sigma = c_1 c_2 \cdots c_m$.

<1>4. Uniqueness: The decomposition is unique up to the order of the factors and omission of $1$-cycles (fixed points).

<1>5. Conclusion: Every permutation in $S_n$ is a product of disjoint cycles.
Q.E.D.
:::
