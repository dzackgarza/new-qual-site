::: problem
Define the radical of an ideal. What is special about the nilradical?
:::

::: solution
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $I\trianglelefteq R$. The radical of $I$ is
\[
\sqrt I
=\{r\in R:r^n\in I\text{ for some }n\ge1\}.
\]
It is an ideal containing $I$.

The nilradical is the radical of the zero ideal:
\[
\operatorname{Nil}(R)=\sqrt{(0)}
=\{r\in R:r^n=0\text{ for some }n\ge1\}.
\]
Thus its elements are exactly the nilpotent elements of $R$.

Equivalently,
\[
\operatorname{Nil}(R)
=\bigcap_{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}R}\mathfrak p.
\]
Hence $R$ is reduced exactly when
\[
\operatorname{Nil}(R)=0.
\]
More generally,
\[
\sqrt I
=\bigcap_{\mathfrak p\supseteq I}\mathfrak p,
\]
where the intersection ranges over prime ideals containing $I$.
:::
