::: {.problem}
Define the radical of an ideal. What is special about the nilradical?
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $I$ an ideal of $R$.

::: pf

::: pf-step
The radical of $I$ is $\sqrt I=\{r\in R:r^n\in I\text{ for some }n\ge1\}$, an ideal containing $I$, and $\sqrt I$ is the intersection of the prime ideals containing $I$.

::: pf-proof
If $r^n\in I$ and $s^m\in I$, every term of the binomial expansion of $(r+s)^{n+m-1}$ is divisible by $r^n$ or by $s^m$, so $r+s\in\sqrt I$; clearly $(ar)^n=a^nr^n\in I$.
The description as an intersection of primes is the standard theorem on radicals, applied in $R/I$.
:::

:::

::: pf-step
The nilradical $\sqrt{(0)}$ is the set of nilpotent elements of $R$, it equals the intersection of all prime ideals of $R$, and $R$ is reduced exactly when $\sqrt{(0)}=0$.

::: pf-proof
Take $I=(0)$ in the previous step; the last statement is the definition of a reduced ring.
:::
