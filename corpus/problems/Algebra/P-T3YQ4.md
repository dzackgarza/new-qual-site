::: problem
(1) Define the **nilradical** $\operatorname{Nil}(R)$ of a commutative ring $R$, and state the characterization theorem relating it to the intersection of all prime ideals of $R$.
(2) Use this characterization to prove that the polynomial ring $F[x]$ over a field $F$ contains **infinitely many prime ideals**.
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** Define the nilradical as the intersection of all prime ideals, and prove that $F[x]$ has infinitely many prime ideals (Euclid's theorem for polynomials).

<1>1. Definition and Characterization of the Nilradical:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. The **nilradical** of a commutative ring $R$, denoted $\operatorname{Nil}(R)$ or $\sqrt{(0)}$, is the set of all nilpotent elements in $R$:
        $$\operatorname{Nil}(R) \coloneqq \{r \in R \mid \exists n \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } r^n = 0\}.$$
    <2>2. **Theorem:** In any commutative ring with identity $R$, the nilradical equals the **intersection of all prime ideals** of $R$:
        $$\operatorname{Nil}(R) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec}(R)} \mathfrak{p}.$$
    <2>3. In particular, for an **integral domain** $R$ (such as $F[x]$), there are no non-zero nilpotent elements, so:
        $$\operatorname{Nil}(R) = \{0\} \implies \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec}(R)} \mathfrak{p} = (0).$$

<1>2. Proof that $F[x]$ has Infinitely Many Prime Ideals:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. In the PID $F[x]$, the non-zero prime ideals are precisely the maximal ideals $(p(x))$ generated by irreducible polynomials $p(x) \in F[x]$.
    <2>2. Suppose, for contradiction, that $F[x]$ has only **finitely many non-zero prime ideals**:
        $$\operatorname{Spec}(F[x]) \setminus \{(0)\} = \{(p_1(x)), (p_2(x)), \dots, (p_k(x))\}$$
        where $p_1(x), \dots, p_k(x) \in F[x]$ are non-constant, pairwise coprime irreducible polynomials.
    <2>3. Consider the non-zero polynomial constructed by multiplying all prime generators:
        $$g(x) \coloneqq p_1(x) p_2(x) \cdots p_k(x) \in F[x] \setminus \{0\}.$$
    <2>4. Now consider the polynomial:
        $$h(x) \coloneqq g(x) + 1 = p_1(x) p_2(x) \cdots p_k(x) + 1 \in F[x].$$
    <2>5. We analyze the properties of $h(x)$:
        - $\deg(h) = \sum_{i=1}^k \deg(p_i) \ge k \ge 1$, so $h(x)$ is a non-constant polynomial.
        - Since $h(x)$ is not a unit in $F[x]$ ($h(x) \notin F^\times$), $h(x)$ must be contained in at least one maximal (prime) ideal of $F[x]$.
        - By our finiteness hypothesis, this prime ideal must be one of $(p_j(x))$ for some $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$.
        - Thus $p_j(x) \mid h(x)$.
    <2>6. But $p_j(x)$ also divides the product $g(x) = p_1(x) \cdots p_k(x)$.
    <2>7. Therefore, $p_j(x)$ divides the difference:
        $$h(x) - g(x) = 1 \implies p_j(x) \mid 1.$$
    <2>8. This implies that $p_j(x) \in F^\times$ is a unit (constant), which contradicts the fact that $p_j(x)$ is an irreducible polynomial of degree $\ge 1$.
    <2>9. **Alternative argument via intersection:**
        If there were only finitely many primes $\mathfrak{p}_i = (p_i)$, then the ideal $I = \bigcap_{i=1}^k \mathfrak{p}_i = (p_1 \cdots p_k) \ne (0)$ would contain elements not in $(0) = \operatorname{Nil}(F[x])$, yet the intersection of non-zero primes cannot vanish on non-constant elements unless infinitely many primes exist.

<1>3. Conclusion:
    $F[x]$ must contain infinitely many distinct prime ideals. Q.E.D.
:::
