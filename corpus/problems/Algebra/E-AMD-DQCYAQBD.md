::: {.exercise}
Let $D_n$ be the dihedral group of order $2n$, with the conventions $D_1\cong C_2$ and $D_2\cong C_2\times C_2$. Show that if $N\trianglelefteq D_n$ is a proper normal subgroup, then $D_n/N$ is again a dihedral group.
:::

::: {.solution}

Write
$$
D_n=\langle r,s\mid r^n=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle,
$$
and let $N\trianglelefteq D_n$ be proper. Put $\bar r=rN$ and $\bar s=sN$.

::: pf

::: pf-step

With this presentation every element of $D_n$ is $r^i$ or $s r^i$ with $0\le i<n$, so $\abs{D_n}=2n$.

::: pf-proof

The relation $srs=r^{-1}$, equivalently $sr=r^{-1}s$, moves every $s$ past every power of $r$, and $s^2=1$ cancels $s$ in pairs, so every word in $r^{\pm1}$ and $s$ can be written as $r^i$ or $sr^i$; the relation $r^n=1$ reduces $i$ modulo $n$. The $2n$ elements so obtained are distinct in the dihedral group of order $2n$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

The classes $\bar r$ and $\bar s$ generate $D_n/N$, and
$$
\bar s^2=1,
\qquad
\bar s\bar r\bar s^{-1}=\bar r^{-1}.
$$

::: pf-proof

The quotient map $\pi\colon D_n\to D_n/N$ is a surjective homomorphism, so the images of generators generate the quotient. It carries each relation of the presentation to the same relation among the images: $\pi(s^2)=\bar s^2=\pi(1)=1$, and $\bar s\bar r\bar s^{-1}=\pi(srs^{-1})=\pi(r^{-1})=\bar r^{-1}$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

Let $d=\abs{\bar r}$; then $d\mid n$.

::: pf-proof

$\bar r^n=\pi(r^n)=\pi(1)=1$, so the order of $\bar r$ divides $n$: if $n=qd+t$ with $0\le t<d$ then $1=\bar r^n=(\bar r^d)^q\bar r^t=\bar r^t$, which forces $t=0$ because $d$ is the least positive exponent killing $\bar r$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If $\bar s\notin\langle\bar r\rangle$, then every element of $D_n/N$ has a unique form $\bar r^i$ or $\bar s\bar r^i$ with $0\le i<d$.

::: pf-proof

By step 2 every element is a word in $\bar r$ and $\bar s$. Since $\bar s\bar r=\bar r^{-1}\bar s$, every word can be brought to the form $\bar r^i$ or $\bar s\bar r^i$, and $\bar r^d=1$ reduces $i$ to $0\le i<d$. For uniqueness, $\bar r^i=\bar r^j$ with $0\le i,j<d$ gives $i=j$ because $d$ is the order of $\bar r$; and $\bar r^i=\bar s\bar r^j$ would give $\bar s=\bar r^{i-j}\in\langle\bar r\rangle$, which the case excludes.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

In that case the quotient has order $2d$ and
$$
D_n/N\cong D_d,
$$
which is $C_2$ for $d=1$ and $C_2\times C_2$ for $d=2$.

::: pf-proof

Step 4 lists exactly $2d$ elements, so $\abs{D_n/N}=2d$. By steps 2 and 4 the quotient is generated by $\bar r$ of order $d$ and $\bar s\notin\langle\bar r\rangle$ of order $2$, subject to $\bar s\bar r\bar s^{-1}=\bar r^{-1}$, which are the defining relations of $D_d=\langle x,y\mid x^d=y^2=1,\ yxy=x^{-1}\rangle$. So $x\mapsto\bar r$, $y\mapsto\bar s$ defines a surjective homomorphism $D_d\to D_n/N$ (von Dyck's theorem), and it is injective because both groups have order $2d$. The conventions of the statement give $D_1\cong C_2$ and $D_2\cong C_2\times C_2$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If $\bar s\in\langle\bar r\rangle$, the quotient is cyclic, and the relation $\bar s\bar r\bar s^{-1}=\bar r^{-1}$ becomes $\bar r=\bar r^{-1}$, hence $\bar r^2=1$. Therefore the quotient has order at most $2$, and it is nontrivial because $N$ is proper, so $D_n/N\cong C_2\cong D_1$.

::: pf-proof

In this case $\bar s$ is a power of $\bar r$, so it commutes with $\bar r$ and the relation of step 2 reads $\bar r=\bar r^{-1}$, that is $\bar r^2=1$; hence $d\le2$. The quotient is generated by $\bar r$ (step 2), so it is cyclic of order $d\le2$. $N$ proper means $D_n/N$ has more than one element, so its order is exactly $2$ and it is the cyclic group $C_2$, which is $D_1$ by the convention of the statement.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

There are no other cases: $D_n/N$ is dihedral, being $D_d$ with $d\mid n$ when $\bar s\notin\langle\bar r\rangle$ and $D_1$ when $\bar s\in\langle\bar r\rangle$.

::: pf-proof

Either $\bar s\in\langle\bar r\rangle$ or not; steps 4 and 5 treat the second case and step 6 the first. In both the quotient is one of the groups $D_d$, so a quotient of $D_n$ by a proper normal subgroup is again dihedral.

:::
