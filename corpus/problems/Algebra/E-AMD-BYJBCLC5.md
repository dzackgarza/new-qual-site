::: {.exercise}
Is $\QQ(2^{1\over 3})/\QQ$ normal?
:::

::: {.solution}
**Goal:** Determine whether the field extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$, where $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$, is a normal extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, and provide a complete proof.

**Answer:** No, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not a normal extension.

<1>1. Definition of a normal extension: An algebraic field extension $K/F$ is normal if and only if for every irreducible polynomial $p(x) \in F[x]$ that has at least one root in $K$, $p(x)$ splits completely into linear factors in $K[x]$.
::: {.proof}
Standard definition/characterization of normal field extensions.
:::

<1>2. The minimal polynomial of $\sqrt[3]{2}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: <2>1. Let $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{2} \in \mathbb{R}$ be the real cube root of 2.
::: {.proof}
Setting notation.
:::
<2>2. $\alpha$ is a root of $f(x) = x^3 - 2 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ since $\alpha^3 - 2 = 2 - 2 = 0$.
::: {.proof}
Definition of $\sqrt[3]{2}$.
:::
<2>3. $f(x) = x^3 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein's Criterion at the prime $p = 2$.
::: {.proof}
$2$ divides the constant term $-2$, $2$ does not divide the leading coefficient $1$, and $2^2 = 4$ does not divide $-2$.
:::
<2>4. Thus $f(x) = x^3 - 2$ is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{2}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
::: {.proof}
A monic irreducible polynomial in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ having $\alpha$ as a root is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$.
:::

<1>3. Determination of all complex roots of $f(x)$: <2>1. In $\mathbb{C}$, the roots of $x^3 - 2$ are: - $\alpha_1 = \sqrt[3]{2} \in \mathbb{R}$, - $\alpha_2 = \sqrt[3]{2} \zeta_3 = \sqrt[3]{2} \left(-\frac{1}{2} + i \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$, - $\alpha_3 = \sqrt[3]{2} \zeta_3^2 = \sqrt[3]{2} \left(-\frac{1}{2} - i \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$, where $\zeta_3 = e^{2\pi i / 3}$ is a primitive cube root of unity.
::: {.proof}
Direct algebraic computation: $(\alpha \zeta_3^k)^3 = \alpha^3 (\zeta_3^3)^k = 2 \cdot 1 = 2$.
:::
<2>2. In particular, $\operatorname{Im}(\alpha_2) = \sqrt[3]{2} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \neq 0$, so $\alpha_2 \notin \mathbb{R}$.
::: {.proof}
$\sqrt[3]{2} > 0$ and $\sqrt{3} > 0$.
:::

<1>4. $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ does not contain all roots of $f(x)$: <2>1. Since $\sqrt[3]{2} \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$, the subfield $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$.
::: {.proof}
Subfield generated by real numbers inside $\mathbb{C}$ consists entirely of real numbers.
:::
<2>2. By <1>3.<2>2, the root $\alpha_2 \notin \mathbb{R}$.
::: {.proof}
$\alpha_2$ has non-zero imaginary part.
:::
<2>3. Therefore, $\alpha_2 \notin K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$.
::: {.proof}
Since $\alpha_2 \notin \mathbb{R}$ and $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.
:::

<1>5. Conclusion: The polynomial $f(x) = x^3 - 2 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, has a root $\alpha_1 \in K$, but does not split completely in $K[x]$ (since $\alpha_2 \notin K$). By <1>1, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal.
::: {.proof}
Follows from <1>1, <1>2, and <1>4.
:::
:::
