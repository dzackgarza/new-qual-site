::: {.exercise}
Give an example showing that normality is not transitive: i.e. $H\trianglelefteq K \trianglelefteq G$ with $H$ *not* normal in $G$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Take $G=D_4=\generators{r,s\mid r^4=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}}$, the dihedral group of order $8$, with $K=\generators{r^2,s}$ and $H=\generators{s}$.

::: pf

::: {.pf-step #group}
$D_4$ has the eight elements $r^j$ and $r^js$ for $0\leq j\leq3$, and its relations give $r^4=s^2=1$, $srs^{-1}=r^{-1}$, hence $sr=r^{-1}s$ and $r^2s=sr^2$.

::: pf-proof
Every element of the group presented is a word in $r^{\pm1}$ and $s$, and $s^2=1$ and $srs^{-1}=r^{-1}$ let each such word be rewritten with all powers of $r$ collected and at most one $s$ at the end; since $r^4=1$ this leaves the eight listed words, and they are distinct because the group of symmetries of the square realizes them as eight distinct permutations of the four vertices. From $srs^{-1}=r^{-1}$ we get $sr=r^{-1}s$, and applying that twice gives $r^2s=sr^{-2}=sr^2$ because $r^{-2}=r^2$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #K}
$K=\generators{r^2,s}=\{1,r^2,s,sr^2\}$ is a subgroup of $G$ of order $4$, so $[G:K]=2$.

::: pf-proof
The set $\{1,r^2,s,sr^2\}$ is closed under multiplication: $(r^2)^2=r^4=1$, $s^2=1$, $r^2s=sr^2$ by step [](#group){.pf-ref}, $s\cdot sr^2=r^2$, and $sr^2\cdot sr^2=s\,r^2s\,r^2=s\,sr^2r^2=r^4=1$. It contains $1$ and is closed under inverses, since each listed element is its own inverse by the same computations. So it is a subgroup, it is exactly the subgroup generated by $r^2$ and $s$, and it has four elements; by Lagrange $\abs{G}=8=2\cdot4=[G:K]\cdot\abs{K}$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #H}
$H=\generators{s}=\{1,s\}$ is a subgroup of $K$ of order $2$, so $[K:H]=2$.

::: pf-proof
$\{1,s\}$ is a subgroup because $s^2=1$, and it lies in $K$ by step [](#K){.pf-ref}; it is the subgroup generated by $s$ and has two elements, so $[K:H]=\abs{K}/\abs{H}=4/2=2$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #index-two}
A subgroup of index $2$ in a finite group is normal.

::: pf-proof
Let $L\leq P$ with $[P:L]=2$. The left cosets of $L$ are $L$ and $P\setminus L$, and so are the right cosets, since each coset of $L$ has $\abs{L}$ elements and there are exactly two of them, one being $L$ itself. For $p\in P$ the coset $pL$ is therefore a coset of $L$ on the right as well, and it contains $p$, so $pL=Lp$; equivalently $pLp^{-1}=L$. Hence $L\trianglelefteq P$.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #normal}
$K\trianglelefteq G$ and $H\trianglelefteq K$.

::: pf-proof
$[G:K]=2$ by step [](#K){.pf-ref} and $[K:H]=2$ by step [](#H){.pf-ref}, so both inclusions are instances of step [](#index-two){.pf-ref}.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #not-normal}
$rHr^{-1}=\{1,sr^2\}\neq H$, so $H\not\trianglelefteq G$.

::: pf-proof
By step [](#group){.pf-ref}, $rs=sr^{-1}=sr^{3}$, so $rsr^{-1}=sr^3r^{-1}=sr^2$. Hence $rHr^{-1}=\{rr^{-1},\,rsr^{-1}\}=\{1,sr^2\}$. That set differs from $H=\{1,s\}$ because $sr^2\neq s$: multiplying by $s^{-1}=s$ on the left would give $r^2=1$, which fails since $r$ has order $4$ by the distinctness of the eight words in step [](#group){.pf-ref}.
:::

:::

::: {.pf-step}
Normality is not transitive: $H\trianglelefteq K\trianglelefteq G$ while $H\not\trianglelefteq G$.

::: pf-proof
The two normalities are step [](#normal){.pf-ref} and the failure is step [](#not-normal){.pf-ref}. Had normality been transitive, $H\trianglelefteq K$ and $K\trianglelefteq G$ would force $H\trianglelefteq G$, which the computation refutes; the three subgroups $H\trianglelefteq K\trianglelefteq G$ therefore form the example required.
:::
