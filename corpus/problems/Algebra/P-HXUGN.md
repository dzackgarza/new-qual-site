::: problem
What is the relation between Euclidean domains and PIDs?
:::

::: solution
**Goal:** State and prove the relationship between Euclidean Domains (EDs) and Principal Ideal Domains (PIDs), and provide the standard counterexample showing that the converse does not hold.

<1>1. Theorem: Every Euclidean Domain is a Principal Ideal Domain:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. Let $R$ be a Euclidean domain with Euclidean function (norm) $d: R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{N}_0$.
    <2>2. Let $I \subseteq R$ be an ideal.
    <2>3. If $I = (0)$, then $I$ is principal (generated by $0$).
    <2>4. If $I \ne (0)$, choose a non-zero element $b \in I \setminus \{0\}$ such that $d(b)$ is minimal in the set $\{d(x) \mid x \in I \setminus \{0\}\}$.
    <2>5. We claim $I = (b)$.
        - We have $(b) \subseteq I$: $(b) = \{rb \mid r \in R\}$, and since $b \in I$ and $I$ is an ideal (closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements), every $rb$ lies in $I$.
        - For the reverse inclusion, let $a \in I$. By the Euclidean property, there exist $q, r \in R$ such that $a = qb + r$, where either $r = 0$ or $d(r) < d(b)$.
        - Rearranging gives $r = a - qb$. Since $a \in I$ and $b \in I$, we have $r \in I$.
        - If $r \ne 0$, then $r \in I \setminus \{0\}$ with $d(r) < d(b)$, contradicting the minimality of $d(b)$.
        - Thus $r = 0$, so $a = qb \in (b)$.
    <2>6. Hence $I = (b)$, so every ideal in $R$ is principal. Since $R$ is an integral domain by definition, $R$ is a PID.

<1>2. The converse is false: PIDs that are not Euclidean Domains:
    *Proof:*
    <2>1. The standard counterexample is the ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-19})$:
        $$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-19})} = \mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1 + \sqrt{-19}}{2}\right].$$
    <2>2. This ring is a PID (its class number is 1, and no non-trivial ideal class exists).
    <2>3. However, it is not a Euclidean domain under *any* Euclidean norm function:
        - In any Euclidean domain $R$, there must exist a "universal side divisor" $u \in R \setminus (R^\times \cup \{0\})$ such that the projection $R^\times \cup \{0\} \to R/(u)$ is surjective.
        - The only units in $R = \mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{-19})/2]$ are $\pm 1$, so $R^\times \cup \{0\} = \{0, 1, -1\}$.
        - Thus $R/(u)$ would have to have order at most 3, so $N(u) \in \{2, 3\}$.
        - But the norm form $N(a + b(1+\sqrt{-19})/2) = a^2 + ab + 5b^2$ has no solutions to $N(x) = 2$ or $N(x) = 3$ in integers.
        - Therefore, no universal side divisor exists, so $R$ is not a Euclidean domain.

<1>3. Hierarchy of Integral Domains:
    $$\text{Fields} \subsetneq \text{Euclidean Domains (ED)} \subsetneq \text{Principal Ideal Domains (PID)} \subsetneq \text{Unique Factorization Domains (UFD)} \subsetneq \text{Integral Domains}.$$

<1>4. Conclusion:
    Every ED is a PID (proved via minimal norm generator), but the converse is false ($\mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{-19})/2]$ is a PID but not an ED). Q.E.D.
:::
