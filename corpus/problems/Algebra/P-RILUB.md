::: problem
Determine the invariant ring for the standard action of $D_4$, the symmetry group of a square, on $\RR[x,y]$.
:::

::: solution
The standard action is generated by
\[
\rho(x,y)=(-y,x),\qquad \tau(x,y)=(x,-y).
\]
Invariance under $\tau$ forces a polynomial to be even in $y$. Since $\rho^2(x,y)=(-x,-y)$, combining invariance under $\tau$ and $\rho^2$ also forces it to be even in $x$. Hence every invariant has the form
\[
f(x,y)=F(x^2,y^2)
\]
for some $F\in\RR[u,v]$.

The quarter-turn $\rho$ interchanges $x^2$ and $y^2$, so $F(u,v)$ must be symmetric in $u,v$. By the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials in two variables,
\[
F(u,v)\in\RR[u+v,uv].
\]
Therefore
\[
\RR[x,y]^{D_4}=\RR[x^2+y^2,x^2y^2].
\]
Conversely, both $x^2+y^2$ and $x^2y^2$ are invariant under every symmetry of the square, so every polynomial in them is $D_4$-invariant.
:::


::: {.solution}
Let \(D_4\) act on \(\mathbb R[x,y]\) through the usual symmetries of the square in the \((x,y)\)-plane. Then
\[
\mathbb R[x,y]^{D_4}=\mathbb R[x^2+y^2,\,x^2y^2].
\]
Equivalently, one may use the generators \(x^2+y^2\) and \(x^4+y^4\).

<1>1. Every \(D_4\)-invariant polynomial lies in \(\mathbb R[x^2,y^2]\).
::: {.proof}
The square-symmetry group contains the reflections
\[
(x,y)\longmapsto(-x,y),
\qquad
(x,y)\longmapsto(x,-y).
\]
If
\[
f(x,y)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}x^iy^j
\]
is fixed by the first reflection, then every coefficient with odd \(i\) is zero. Invariance under the second reflection similarly forces every coefficient with odd \(j\) to vanish. Hence
\[
f(x,y)=F(x^2,y^2)
\]
for some \(F(u,v)\in\mathbb R[u,v]\).
:::

<1>2. Such a polynomial \(F(x^2,y^2)\) is \(D_4\)-invariant if and only if \(F(u,v)\) is symmetric in \(u,v\).
::: {.proof}
The group also contains reflection in the diagonal, which sends \((x,y)\) to \((y,x)\). Thus invariance implies
\[
F(x^2,y^2)=F(y^2,x^2).
\]
Since the substitution homomorphism \(\mathbb R[u,v]\to\mathbb R[x,y]\), \(u\mapsto x^2\), \(v\mapsto y^2\), is injective, this is equivalent to \(F(u,v)=F(v,u)\).

Conversely, the sign changes of the two coordinates and their interchange generate the signed permutation matrices of order \(8\), which are exactly the symmetries of the square. Therefore a polynomial \(F(x^2,y^2)\) with \(F(u,v)=F(v,u)\) is fixed by all of \(D_4\).
:::

<1>3. Every symmetric polynomial in two variables is a polynomial in \(u+v\) and \(uv\).
::: {.proof}
This is the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials in two variables. Explicitly,
\[
\mathbb R[u,v]^{S_2}=\mathbb R[u+v,uv].
\]
:::

<1>4. Therefore
\[
\mathbb R[x,y]^{D_4}=\mathbb R[x^2+y^2,x^2y^2].
\]
::: {.proof}
Apply <1>3 to the symmetric polynomial \(F(u,v)\) from <1>1--<1>2, then substitute \(u=x^2\) and \(v=y^2\). Conversely, both \(x^2+y^2\) and \(x^2y^2\) are fixed by sign changes and by swapping \(x,y\), hence by \(D_4\), so every polynomial in these two generators is invariant.
:::

<1>5. The alternative generating pair \(x^2+y^2\), \(x^4+y^4\) gives the same ring.
::: {.proof}
One has
\[
x^4+y^4=(x^2+y^2)^2-2x^2y^2
\]
and
\[
x^2y^2=\frac{(x^2+y^2)^2-(x^4+y^4)}2.
\]
Since \(2\) is invertible in \(\mathbb R\), each pair generates the other.
:::
