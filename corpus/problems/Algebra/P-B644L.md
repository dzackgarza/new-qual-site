::: problem
- Show that if $H \leq N_G(K)$ then $HK \leq G$, and give a counterexample showing that this condition is not necessary.
:::


::: {.solution}
<1>1. If $H\le N_G(K)$, then $HK$ is a subgroup of $G$.
::: {.proof}
For $h\in H$ and $k\in K$, the normalizer hypothesis gives
\[
hkh^{-1}\in K.
\]
Hence
\[
hk=(hkh^{-1})h\in KH,
\]
so $HK\subseteq KH$. Replacing $h$ by $h^{-1}$ gives the reverse inclusion, hence
\[
HK=KH.
\]
A standard product criterion says that for subgroups $H,K\le G$, the set $HK$ is a subgroup exactly when $HK=KH$. Explicitly, if
\[
h_1k_1,h_2k_2\in HK,
\]
then $k_1h_2\in KH=HK$, so their product lies in $HK$; similarly inverses lie in $HK$. Therefore $HK\le G$.
:::

<1>2. The normalizer condition is not necessary.
::: {.proof}
Take
\[
G=S_3,
\qquad
H=\langle(123)\rangle,
\qquad
K=\langle(12)\rangle.
\]
The subgroups have orders $3$ and $2$ and intersect trivially, so
\[
|HK|=\frac{|H||K|}{|H\cap K|}=6=|S_3|.
\]
Thus
\[
HK=S_3,
\]
which is certainly a subgroup.

However,
\[
(123)(12)(123)^{-1}=(23)\notin K,
\]
so $(123)\notin N_G(K)$ and hence
\[
H\not\le N_G(K).
\]
Thus $H\le N_G(K)$ is sufficient but not necessary for $HK$ to be a subgroup.
:::
:::
