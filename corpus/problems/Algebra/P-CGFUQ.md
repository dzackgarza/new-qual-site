::: {.problem}
Let $R$ be a commutative ring.

1. Say what it means for $R$ to be a unique factorization domain (UFD).

2. Say what it means for $R$ to be a principal ideal domain (PID)

3. Give an example of a UFD that is not a PID. Prove that it is not a PID.
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. Definition of a Unique Factorization Domain (UFD):
<2>1. A commutative ring $R$ (with $1 \neq 0$) is a **Unique Factorization Domain (UFD)** if:
1. $R$ is an integral domain (has no non-zero zero divisors).
2. **Existence of factorizations:** Every non-zero, non-unit $r \in R$ can be written as a finite product of irreducible elements:
\[
r = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k \quad (k \ge 1).
\]
3. **Uniqueness of factorizations:** If $r = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ is another factorization into irreducibles, then $m = k$ and there exists a permutation $\sigma \in S_k$ such that each $q_i$ is an associate of $p_{\sigma(i)}$ (i.e. $q_i = u_i p_{\sigma(i)}$ for some unit $u_i \in R^\times$).
::: {.proof}
standard definition of a UFD.
:::

<1>2. Definition of a Principal Ideal Domain (PID):
<2>1. A commutative ring $R$ (with $1 \neq 0$) is a **Principal Ideal Domain (PID)** if:
1. $R$ is an integral domain.
2. Every ideal $I \subseteq R$ is principal: there exists an element $a \in R$ such that $I = \langle a \rangle = aR$.
::: {.proof}
standard definition of a PID.
:::

<1>3. Example of a UFD that is not a PID:
<2>1. Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]$.
By Gauss's Lemma, since $\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD, the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is also a UFD.
::: {.proof}
Gauss's Lemma for polynomial rings over UFDs.
:::
<2>2. Consider the ideal:
\[
I = \langle 2, x \rangle = \{2f(x) + x g(x) \mid f, g \in \mathbb{Z}[x]\} = \{h(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x] \mid h(0) \in 2\mathbb{Z}\}.
\]
::: {.proof}
evaluating elements of $\langle 2, x \rangle$ at $x = 0$.
:::
<2>3. Suppose $I = \langle d(x) \rangle$ is principal for some $d(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$.
Since $2 \in I$, $d(x) \mid 2$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
Comparing degrees, $\deg(d) \le \deg(2) = 0$, so $d(x) = c \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a constant dividing 2 in $\mathbb{Z}$, hence $c \in \{\pm 1, \pm 2\}$.
::: {.proof}
degree inequality for products of polynomials in an integral domain.
:::
<2>4. - If $c = \pm 1$, then $I = \langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}[x]$, so $1 \in I$.
  Evaluating at $x = 0$ gives $1(0) = 1 \in 2\mathbb{Z}$, a contradiction.
- If $c = \pm 2$, then $I = \langle 2 \rangle = 2\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
  Since $x \in I$, we must have $x = 2 g(x)$ for some $g(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, which is impossible since the coefficient 1 of $x$ is not divisible by 2 in $\mathbb{Z}$.
::: {.proof}
contradiction in both cases.
:::
<2>5. Therefore $I = \langle 2, x \rangle$ is not principal, so $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID.
::: {.proof}
$I$ cannot be generated by a single element.
:::

<1>4. Conclusion:
The definitions of UFD and PID are stated, and $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD that is not a PID because the ideal $\langle 2, x \rangle$ is non-principal. Q.E.D.
::: {.proof}
<1>1 through <1>3.
:::
