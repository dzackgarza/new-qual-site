::: {.exercise}
Let $f$ be entire and for every $a\in \CC$, and write the Laurent expansion about $a$ as $f(z) = \sum_{k\geq 0} c_{k, a} (z-a)^k$.
Suppose that for every $a$, there exists some $k$ such that $c_{k, a} = 0$.
Show that $f$ is a polynomial.
:::

::: {.solution}
\envlist

- Claim: $f^{(n)} = 0$ for some $n$.

- Let $Z_n \definedas \theset{a\in \CC \st c_{n, a} = 0}$ and $Z = \Union_n Z_n$.
  Since $c_{n,a}=f^{(n)}(a)/n!$, we have $f^{(n)}(z) = 0$ on $Z_n$.

- By hypothesis, $Z = \CC$, so not every $Z_n$ can be countable.
  Pick any $Z_n$ which is uncountable.

- Write $Z_n = \Union_{m} Z_{m, n}$ where $Z_{m, n} \definedas \DD_m(0) \intersect Z_n$ as a union of increasing discs, then not every $Z_{m, n}$ can be finite, so pick an infinite one.

- Now the infinite set $Z_{m, n}$ has a limit point in $\CC$, since it lies in the compact closed disk $\overline{\DD_m(0)}$, and $f^{(n)}(z) = 0$ on $Z_{m, n}$, so by the identity principle $f^{(n)}(z) = 0$ on $\CC$.

- Since $f^{(n)}\equiv0$, all Taylor coefficients of $f$ at $0$ of index at least $n$ vanish, so $f$ is a polynomial of degree less than $n$.
:::
