::: {.problem}
(a) Prove: if $f \in C[0,1]$ and $\int_0^1 f(x)e^{-nx}\,dx = 0$ for all $n \in \NN_0$, then $f = 0$.

(b) Let $f : \RR \to \RR$ be an infinitely differentiable function.

    (i) Use Taylor's formula with remainder to show that, given $x$ and $h$, $f'(x) = (f(x+2h) - f(x))/2h - hf''(\xi)$ for some $\xi$.

    (ii) Assume $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$, and that $f''$ is bounded. Show that $f'(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$.

(c) Let $f(x,y)$ on $[-1,1]^2$ be defined by
    $$
    f(x,y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2} & (x,y) \neq (0,0) \\ 0 & (x,y) = (0,0). \end{cases}
    $$
    Determine if $f$ is integrable.

(d) Let $(X,d)$ and $(Y,\rho)$ be metric spaces, $f : X \to Y$, and $x_0 \in X$. Prove that the following statements are equivalent:

    1. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $\rho(f(x), f(x_0)) < \varepsilon$ whenever $d(x, x_0) < \delta$.
    2. The sequence $\theset{f(x_n)}_{n=1}^\infty \to f(x_0)$ for every sequence $\theset{x_n} \to x_0$ in $X$.

(e) Let $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$. Show that $f$ is uniformly continuous on $(1,\infty)$ but not on $(0,\infty)$.

(f) Let
    $$
    f_n(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{n} & x \in \left(\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}, \frac{1}{2^n}\right] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
    $$
    Show that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n$ does not satisfy the Weierstrass M-test but that it nevertheless converges uniformly on $\RR$.

(g) Let $f_n : [0,1) \to \RR$ be the function defined by
    $$
    f_n(x) \coloneqq \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{x^k}{1+x^k}.
    $$

    (i) Prove that $f_n$ converges to a function $f : [0,1) \to \RR$.

    (ii) Prove that for every $0 < a < 1$ the convergence is uniform on $[0,a]$.

    (iii) Prove that $f$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$.

(h) Let $\theset{r_n}_{n=1}^\infty$ be any enumeration of all the rationals in $[0,1]$ and define $f : [0,1] \to \RR$ by setting
    $$
    f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{n} & \text{if } x = r_n \\ 0 & \text{if } x \in [0,1] \setminus \QQ. \end{cases}
    $$
    Prove that $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = 0$ for every $c \in [0,1]$ and conclude that the set of all points at which $f$ is discontinuous is precisely $[0,1] \cap \QQ$.

(i) Let
    $$
    g(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{1+n^2x}.
    $$

    (i) Show that the series defining $g$ does not converge uniformly on $(0,\infty)$, but nonetheless still defines a continuous function on $(0,\infty)$.

    (ii) Is $g$ differentiable on $(0,\infty)$? If so, is the derivative function $g'$ continuous on $(0,\infty)$?

(j) Let $h_n(x) = \dfrac{x}{(1+x)^{n+1}}$.

    (i) Prove that $h_n$ converges uniformly to $0$ on $[0,\infty)$.

    (ii) Verify that
        $$
        \sum_{n=0}^\infty h_n(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0. \end{cases}
        $$
        Does $\sum_{n=0}^\infty h_n$ converge uniformly on $[0,\infty)$?

    (iii) Prove that $\sum_{n=0}^\infty h_n$ converges uniformly on $[a,\infty)$ for any $a > 0$.

(k) Define a function $f$ on $\RR$ by
    $$
    f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2} & \text{if } x > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } x \leq 0. \end{cases}
    $$

    (i) Check whether $f$ is infinitely differentiable at $0$, and, if so, find $f^{(n)}(0)$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$. Show details.

    (ii) Does $f$ have a power series expansion at $0$?

    (iii) Let $g(x) = f(x)f(1-x)$. Show that $g$ is a nontrivial infinitely differentiable function on $\RR$ which vanishes outside $(0,1)$.

(l) A real-valued function $f$ on an interval $I$ for which there exists a constant $C$ such that
    $$
    \abs{f(x) - f(y)} \leq C\abs{x - y}
    $$
    for all $x$ and $y$ in $I$ is called a *Lipschitz function*.

    (i) Show that a Lipschitz function is absolutely continuous.

    (ii) Show that an absolutely continuous function $f$ on an interval is Lipschitz if and only if $f'$ is essentially bounded.

(m) If $f$ is nonnegative and integrable on $[0,1]$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^1 \sqrt[n]{f} = m\theset{x \st f(x) > 0}$.

(n) If $\theset{s_n}$ is a complex sequence, define its arithmetic means $\sigma_n$ by
    $$
    \sigma_n = \frac{s_0 + s_1 + \cdots + s_n}{n+1} \qquad (n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots).
    $$

    (i) If $\lim s_n = s$, prove that $\lim \sigma_n = s$.

    (ii) Construct a sequence $\theset{s_n}$ which does not converge, although $\lim \sigma_n = 0$.

    (iii) Can it happen that $s_n > 0$ for all $n$ and that $\limsup s_n = \infty$, although $\lim \sigma_n = 0$?

    (iv) Put $a_n = s_n - s_{n-1}$ for $n \geq 1$. Show that
        $$
        s_n - \sigma_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^n ka_k.
        $$
        Assume that $\lim(na_n) = 0$ and that $\theset{\sigma_n}$ converges. Prove that $\theset{s_n}$ converges. This gives a converse of (i), under the additional assumption that $na_n \to 0$.

    (v) Derive the last conclusion from a weaker hypothesis: assume $M < \infty$, $\abs{na_n} \leq M$ for all $n$, and $\lim \sigma_n = \sigma$. Prove that $\lim s_n = \sigma$.

(o) Prove that if $f : [0,1] \to \RR$ is continuous then
    $$
    \lim_{k\to\infty} \int_0^1 kx^{k-1}f(x)\,dx = f(1).
    $$

(p) Let $f_n(x) = nx(1-x)^n$, $n \in \NN$.

    (i) Show that $f_n \to 0$ pointwise but not uniformly on $[0,1]$.

    (ii) Show that
        $$
        \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^1 n(1-x)^n \sin x\,dx = 0.
        $$

(q) Show that if $x_n$ is a decreasing sequence of positive real numbers such that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty x_n$ converges, then
    $$
    \lim_{n\to\infty} nx_n = 0.
    $$

::: pf-step

(a) If $f \in C[0,1]$ and $\int_0^1 f(x)e^{-nx}\,dx = 0$ for every $n \geq 0$, then $f \equiv 0$.

::: pf-proof

Substitute $t = e^{-x}$, so $x = -\log t$ and $dx = -dt/t$, and $x \in [0,1]$ corresponds to $t \in [e^{-1}, 1]$. Then
$$
0 = \int_0^1 f(x)e^{-nx}\,dx = \int_{e^{-1}}^1 f(-\log t)\,t^{n-1}\,dt = \int_{e^{-1}}^1 g(t)\,t^n\,dt,
\qquad g(t) \coloneqq \frac{f(-\log t)}{t},
$$
for every $n \geq 0$, and $g$ is continuous on $[e^{-1},1]$. So $\int_{e^{-1}}^1 gp = 0$ for every polynomial $p$. By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, choose polynomials $p_k \to g$ uniformly on $[e^{-1},1]$. Then
$$
\int_{e^{-1}}^1 g(t)^2\,dt = \lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{e^{-1}}^1 g(t)p_k(t)\,dt = 0,
$$
so the continuous function $g$ vanishes on $[e^{-1},1]$. Since $t \mapsto -\log t$ maps $[e^{-1},1]$ onto $[0,1]$, $f \equiv 0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(b) For $h \neq 0$, $f'(x) = \frac{f(x+2h)-f(x)}{2h} - hf''(\xi)$ for some $\xi$ between $x$ and $x+2h$; if $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$ and $\abs{f''} \leq M$, then $f'(x) \to 0$.

::: pf-proof

Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder from $x$ to $x+2h$ gives
$$
f(x+2h)=f(x)+2hf'(x)+2h^2f''(\xi)
$$
for some $\xi$ between $x$ and $x+2h$; solving for $f'(x)$ gives (i). For (ii), fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $h>0$ so that $Mh<\varepsilon/2$. For $x$ large, both $\abs{f(x)}$ and $\abs{f(x+2h)}$ are $<h\varepsilon/2$, so the first term has absolute value $<\varepsilon/2$. Thus $\abs{f'(x)}<\varepsilon$ for all large $x$, and $f'(x)\to0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(c) $f$ is $\boxed{\text{not integrable}}$ on $[-1,1]^2$.

::: pf-proof

Away from the origin $f$ is continuous. In polar coordinates,
$$
\abs{\frac{xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}} = \frac{\abs{\sin\theta\cos\theta}}{r^2}.
$$
On the disk of radius $\delta \leq 1$ about the origin, which lies in $[-1,1]^2$,
$$
\iint \abs{f}\,dx\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi}\abs{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\,d\theta\int_0^\delta \frac{dr}{r} = \infty.
$$
Hence $f\notin L^1([-1,1]^2)$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(d) $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ if and only if $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$ for every sequence $x_n\to x_0$.

::: pf-proof

If $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ and $x_n\to x_0$, then given $\varepsilon > 0$ and its $\delta$, $d(x_n, x_0) < \delta$ for all large $n$, so $\rho(f(x_n), f(x_0)) < \varepsilon$ for all large $n$. Conversely, if continuity fails, there is $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $n$ there is $x_n$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<1/n$ but $\rho(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon_0$. Then $x_n\to x_0$ while $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(e) $1/x$ is uniformly continuous on $(1,\infty)$ but not on $(0,\infty)$.

::: pf-proof

On $(1,\infty)$,
$$
\abs{\frac1x-\frac1y}=\frac{\abs{x-y}}{xy}\le \abs{x-y},
$$
so $1/x$ is Lipschitz and therefore uniformly continuous. On $(0,\infty)$ take $x_n=1/n$ and $y_n=1/(n+1)$. Then $\abs{x_n-y_n}\to0$, but $\abs{1/x_n-1/y_n}=1$, so uniform continuity fails.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(f) $\sum_n\norm{f_n}_\infty=\infty$, but $\sum_n f_n$ converges uniformly on $\RR$.

::: pf-proof

$\norm{f_n}_\infty=1/n$ and $\sum_n1/n=\infty$, so the M-test does not apply. The supports are pairwise disjoint, so at each $x$ at most one term is nonzero. Therefore
$$
\sup_x\abs{\sum_{n>N}f_n(x)}\le\frac1{N+1}\to0,
$$
so the series converges uniformly.

:::

::: pf-step

(g) $\sum_{k\ge1}x^k/(1+x^k)$ converges on $[0,1)$, uniformly on each $[0,a]$ with $a<1$, and its sum is differentiable on $(0,1)$.

::: pf-proof

For fixed $x<1$,
$$
0\le \frac{x^k}{1+x^k}\le x^k,
$$
so the series converges. On every $[0,a]$ with $a<1$, the bound by $a^k$ gives uniform convergence by the M-test. Moreover
$$
\left(\frac{x^k}{1+x^k}\right)'=\frac{kx^{k-1}}{(1+x^k)^2},
$$
and on $[0,a]$ this is bounded by $ka^{k-1}$, whose series converges. So the series of derivatives converges uniformly on each $[0,a]$, termwise differentiation is valid there, and the sum is differentiable on $(0,1)$.

:::

::: pf-step

(h) $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=0$ for every $c\in[0,1]$, and $f$ is discontinuous exactly on $[0,1]\cap\QQ$.

::: pf-proof

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $1/N<\varepsilon$. Only the finitely many points $r_1,\ldots,r_N$ can have value at least $\varepsilon$. For a fixed $c$, choose a punctured neighborhood of $c$ in $[0,1]$ that contains none of those points. Then $\abs{f(x)}<\varepsilon$ throughout that punctured neighborhood, so $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=0$. Hence $f$ is continuous at $c$ exactly when $f(c)=0$: at an irrational $c$, $f(c)=0$, and at $c=r_n$, $f(c)=1/n\neq0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(i) The series for $g$ does not converge uniformly on $(0,\infty)$, $g$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$, and $g$ is differentiable on $(0,\infty)$ with $g'$ continuous.

::: pf-proof

The term $n=0$ is the constant $1$. Uniform convergence on $(0,\infty)$ would force
$$
\sup_{x>0}\frac1{1+n^2x}\to0,
$$
but the supremum is $1$ for every $n$. On every $[a,\infty)$, $a>0$,
$$
\frac1{1+n^2x}\le\frac1{n^2a} \qquad (n \geq 1),
$$
so the series converges uniformly there and $g$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$. The series of derivatives is
$$
-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2},
$$
and on $[a,\infty)$ its terms are bounded by $1/(n^2a^2)$, so it converges uniformly there. Hence $g$ is differentiable on $(0,\infty)$, $g'$ is this series, and $g'$ is continuous there.

:::

::: pf-step

(j) $h_n\to0$ uniformly on $[0,\infty)$; $\sum_{n\ge0}h_n$ equals $1$ for $x > 0$ and $0$ at $x = 0$; it converges uniformly on each $[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$ but $\boxed{\text{not}}$ on $[0,\infty)$.

::: pf-proof

For $n\ge1$, $h_n'(x) = (1-nx)(1+x)^{-n-2}$, so the maximum on $[0,\infty)$ is at $x=1/n$, with
$$
\norm{h_n}_\infty=\frac{n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\le\frac1{n+1}\to0.
$$
For $x>0$,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty h_n(x)
=\frac{x}{1+x}\sum_{n=0}^\infty(1+x)^{-n}=1,
$$
while at $x=0$ every term is $0$. The uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous and this sum is not, so the convergence is not uniform on $[0,\infty)$. For $x > 0$ the tail $\sum_{n \geq N} h_n(x)$ equals $(1+x)^{-N}$, whose supremum on $[a,\infty)$ is $(1+a)^{-N}\to0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(k) $f$ is $C^\infty$ with $f^{(n)}(0)=\boxed{0}$ for every $n$, $f$ has no power series expansion at $0$, and $g(x)=f(x)f(1-x)$ is smooth, positive on $(0,1)$ and zero elsewhere.

::: pf-proof

Induction on $n$ gives $f^{(n)}(x)=P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x>0$ and a polynomial $P_n$. Since $t^m e^{-t^2}\to0$ as $t\to\infty$ for every $m$, the right difference quotient $f^{(n)}(x)/x = (1/x)P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}$ tends to $0$ as $x \to 0^+$, and the left difference quotient is $0$. So by induction $f^{(n+1)}(0)$ exists and equals $0$ for every $n$, and $f\in C^\infty$. A power series expansion at $0$ would be the Taylor series, which is identically zero, but $f(x) > 0$ for $x>0$. Finally $g$ is a product of smooth functions, it is positive on $(0,1)$, and one factor vanishes outside $(0,1)$.

:::

::: pf-step

(l) A Lipschitz function on an interval is absolutely continuous, and an absolutely continuous $f$ is Lipschitz if and only if $f'\in L^\infty$.

::: pf-proof

If $\abs{f(x)-f(y)}\le C\abs{x-y}$, then for pairwise disjoint intervals $(a_j,b_j)$,
$$
\sum_j\abs{f(b_j)-f(a_j)}\le C\sum_j(b_j-a_j),
$$
so $f$ is absolutely continuous. If $f$ is absolutely continuous, then $f(y)-f(x)=\int_x^y f'(t)\,dt$, so $f'\in L^\infty$ implies $\abs{f(y)-f(x)}\le\norm{f'}_\infty\abs{y-x}$. Conversely, at each point where a Lipschitz $f$ is differentiable, $\abs{f'}$ is at most the Lipschitz constant, and $f$ is differentiable a.e.

::: pf-step

(m) For integrable $f\ge0$ on $[0,1]$, $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f^{1/n}=m\theset{f>0}$.

::: pf-proof

Pointwise, $f(x)^{1/n}\to\mathbf1_{\theset{f>0}}(x)$. Also $t^{1/n}\le1+t$ for $t\ge0$, so $f^{1/n}\le1+f\in L^1([0,1])$. The dominated convergence theorem gives
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f(x)^{1/n}\,dx
=m\theset{x \st f(x)>0}.
$$

::: pf-step

(n) The five claims about Cesàro means hold, and the answer to (iii) is $\boxed{\text{yes}}$.

::: pf-proof

::: pf-step

(i) If $s_n\to s$, then $\sigma_n\to s$.

::: pf-proof

Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $K$ with $\abs{s_k - s} < \varepsilon$ for $k > K$. Then $\abs{\sigma_n - s} \leq \frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k \leq K}\abs{s_k - s} + \varepsilon$, and the first term tends to $0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(ii) $s_n=(-1)^n$ does not converge, while $\sigma_n\to0$.

::: pf-proof

$\abs{s_0 + \cdots + s_n} \leq 1$, so $\abs{\sigma_n} \leq 1/(n+1)$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(iii) $s_{2^k}=k$ for $k \geq 1$ and $s_n=1/(n+1)$ for all other $n$ gives $s_n>0$, $\limsup s_n=\infty$ and $\sigma_n\to0$.

::: pf-proof

For $2^m\le n<2^{m+1}$,
$$
0<\sigma_n\le\frac{(1+2+\cdots+m) + \sum_{j=0}^n \frac{1}{j+1}}{n+1}\le\frac{m(m+1)/2 + 1 + \log(n+1)}{2^m}\to0.
$$

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(iv) $s_n-\sigma_n=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^nka_k$, and if $na_n\to0$ and $\sigma_n$ converges, then $s_n$ converges to the same limit.

::: pf-proof

$$
\sum_{k=1}^nka_k
=ns_n-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}s_k
=(n+1)s_n-\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_k
=(n+1)(s_n-\sigma_n).
$$
If $na_n\to0$, then the averages $\frac1{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^nka_k$ tend to $0$ by (i), so $s_n-\sigma_n\to0$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

(v) If $\abs{na_n}\le M$ for all $n$ and $\sigma_n\to\sigma$, then $s_n\to\sigma$.

::: pf-proof

For $0 \leq m<n$, $(n+1)\sigma_n-(m+1)\sigma_m=\sum_{i=m+1}^n s_i$, and therefore
$$
s_n-\sigma_n=\frac{m+1}{n-m}(\sigma_n-\sigma_m)+\frac1{n-m}\sum_{i=m+1}^n(s_n-s_i).
$$
For $m<i\le n$, $\abs{s_n-s_i}\le\sum_{k=i+1}^n\abs{a_k}\le M\frac{n-i}{i+1}\le M\frac{n-m-1}{m+2}$. Fix $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and, for $n$ large, let $m$ be the integer with $m\le\frac{n-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}<m+1$. The first inequality gives $\varepsilon(m+1)\le n-m$, so $\frac{m+1}{n-m}\le\frac1\varepsilon$; the second gives $n-m-1<\varepsilon(m+2)$, so $\frac{n-m-1}{m+2}<\varepsilon$. Hence
$$
\abs{s_n-\sigma_n}\le\frac1\varepsilon\abs{\sigma_n-\sigma_m}+M\varepsilon.
$$
As $n\to\infty$, $m\to\infty$ and $\sigma_n-\sigma_m\to0$, so $\limsup_n\abs{s_n-\sigma}\le M\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $s_n\to\sigma$.

:::

::: pf-step

(o) For $f\in C([0,1])$, $\lim_{k\to\infty}k\int_0^1x^{k-1}f(x)\,dx=f(1)$.

::: pf-proof

Since $\int_0^1kx^{k-1}\,dx=1$,
$$
k\int_0^1x^{k-1}f(x)\,dx-f(1)
=k\int_0^1x^{k-1}(f(x)-f(1))\,dx.
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $a<1$ so that $\abs{f(x)-f(1)}<\varepsilon$ on $[a,1]$. The contribution from $[a,1]$ is at most $\varepsilon$. On $[0,a]$ it is at most $2\norm{f}_\infty a^k$, which tends to $0$.

:::

::: pf-step

(p) $f_n(x)=nx(1-x)^n$ converges to $0$ pointwise but not uniformly on $[0,1]$, and $\int_0^1 n(1-x)^n\sin x\,dx\to0$.

::: pf-proof

$f_n(0)=0$, and for fixed $x\in(0,1]$, $n(1-x)^n \to 0$ because $0 \leq 1-x < 1$, so $f_n\to0$ pointwise. Since $f_n'(x) = n(1-x)^{n-1}(1-(n+1)x)$, the maximum is at $x=1/(n+1)$ and equals
$$
\frac{n}{n+1}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n\to e^{-1},
$$
so convergence is not uniform. Since $0\le\sin x\le x$ on $[0,1]$,
$$
0\le \int_0^1 n(1-x)^n\sin x\,dx
\le n\int_0^1x(1-x)^n\,dx
=\frac{n}{(n+1)(n+2)}\to0.
$$

:::

::: pf-step

(q) A decreasing summable sequence of positive numbers satisfies $nx_n\to0$.

::: pf-proof

Since $(x_n)$ is decreasing,
$$
\sum_{k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}^{n}x_k
\ge \bigl(n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor\bigr)x_n
\ge \frac n2x_n.
$$
The left side is a block of the tail of a convergent series of positive terms and therefore tends to $0$. Hence $nx_n\to0$.
