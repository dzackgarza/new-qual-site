::: pf-step

If $f\in C([0,1])$ and $\int_0^1 f(x)x^n\,dx=0$ for every $n\ge0$, then $f\equiv0$.

::: pf-proof

Assume $f\in C([0,1])$ and $\int_0^1 f(x)x^n\,dx=0$ for every $n\ge0$. Then $\int_0^1 f p=0$ for every polynomial $p$. By Weierstrass, choose polynomials $p_k\to f$ uniformly. Since $f\in L^1$,
$$
\int_0^1 f(x)^2\,dx
=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_0^1 f(x)p_k(x)\,dx=0.
$$
Hence $f=0$ almost everywhere, and continuity gives $f\equiv0$.

::: pf-step

If $f$ is twice differentiable, $f(x)\to0$ as $x\to\infty$, and $|f''|\le M$, then $f'(x)\to0$.

::: pf-proof

Taylor's theorem applied from $x$ to $x+2h$ gives
$$
f(x+2h)=f(x)+2hf'(x)+2h^2f''(\xi)
$$
for some $\xi$ between $x$ and $x+2h$, hence
$$
f'(x)=\frac{f(x+2h)-f(x)}{2h}-h f''(\xi).
$$
If $f(x)\to0$ as $x\to\infty$ and $|f''|\le M$, fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $h>0$ so that $Mh<\varepsilon/2$. For $x$ large, both $|f(x)|$ and $|f(x+2h)|$ are $<h\varepsilon/2$, so the first term has absolute value $<\varepsilon/2$. Thus $|f'(x)|<\varepsilon$ eventually, proving $f'(x)\to0$.

::: pf-step

The function $xy/(x^2+y^2)^2$ is not integrable on $[-1,1]^2$.

::: pf-proof

Away from the origin the function is continuous. In polar coordinates,
$$
\left|\frac{xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\right|
=\frac{|\sin\theta\cos\theta|}{r^2}.
$$
On any small disk around the origin,
$$
\iint |f|\,dx\,dy
\ge c\int_0^\delta \frac{dr}{r}=\infty
$$
for a positive angular constant $c$. Hence $f\notin L^1([-1,1]^2)$; in particular it is not Lebesgue integrable.

::: pf-step

A map $f\colon(X,d)\to(Y,\rho)$ of metric spaces is continuous at $x_0$ if and only if $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$ for every sequence $x_n\to x_0$.

::: pf-proof

If $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ and $x_n\to x_0$, the epsilon-delta definition immediately gives $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$. Conversely, if continuity fails, there is $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $n$ one can choose $x_n$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<1/n$ but $\rho(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon_0$. Then $x_n\to x_0$ while $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$, contradicting the sequential condition.

:::

::: pf-step

$1/x$ is uniformly continuous on $(1,\infty)$ but not on $(0,\infty)$.

::: pf-proof

On $(1,\infty)$,
$$
\left|\frac1x-\frac1y\right|=\frac{|x-y|}{xy}\le |x-y|,
$$
so $1/x$ is Lipschitz and therefore uniformly continuous. On $(0,\infty)$ take $x_n=1/n$ and $y_n=1/(n+1)$. Then $|x_n-y_n|\to0$, but
$$
\left|\frac1{x_n}-\frac1{y_n}\right|=1,
$$
so uniform continuity fails.

:::

::: pf-step

For $f_n=\frac1n\mathbf1_{(2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n}]}$, the series $\sum_n f_n$ converges uniformly, but $\sum_n\|f_n\|_\infty=\infty$.

::: pf-proof

The supports are pairwise disjoint, so at each $x$ at most one term is nonzero. Therefore the tail satisfies
$$
\sup_x\left|\sum_{n>N}f_n(x)\right|\le\frac1{N+1}\to0,
$$
so the series converges uniformly. But $\|f_n\|_\infty=1/n$ and $\sum_n1/n=\infty$, so the M-test does not apply.

:::

::: pf-step

$\sum_{k\ge1}x^k/(1+x^k)$ converges on $[0,1)$, uniformly on each $[0,a]$ with $a<1$, and its sum is differentiable on $(0,1)$.

::: pf-proof

For fixed $x<1$,
$$
0\le \frac{x^k}{1+x^k}\le x^k,
$$
so the series converges. On every $[0,a]$ with $a<1$, the bound by $a^k$ gives uniform convergence by the M-test. Moreover
$$
\left(\frac{x^k}{1+x^k}\right)'=\frac{kx^{k-1}}{(1+x^k)^2},
$$
and on $[0,a]$ this is bounded by $ka^{k-1}$, whose series converges. Hence termwise differentiation is valid locally uniformly, and the sum is differentiable on $(0,1)$.

:::

::: pf-step

Let $(r_n)$ enumerate $\QQ$, and let $f(r_n)=1/n$ and $f(x)=0$ for irrational $x$. Then $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=0$ for every $c\in\RR$.

::: pf-proof

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $1/N<\varepsilon$. Only the finitely many points $r_1,\dots,r_N$ can have value at least $\varepsilon$. For a fixed $c$, choose a punctured neighborhood of $c$ avoiding those finitely many points other than possibly $c$ itself. Then $|f(x)|<\varepsilon$ throughout that punctured neighborhood, so
$$
\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=0.
$$
Thus $f$ is continuous exactly at the irrational points and discontinuous exactly at the rational points.

::: pf-step

$g(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}(1+n^2x)^{-1}$ does not converge uniformly on $(0,\infty)$, converges uniformly on each $[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$, and is $C^1$ on $(0,\infty)$.

::: pf-proof

Uniform convergence on $(0,\infty)$ fails because a necessary condition would be
$$
\sup_{x>0}\frac1{1+n^2x}\to0,
$$
but the supremum is $1$ for every $n$. On every $[a,\infty)$, $a>0$,
$$
\frac1{1+n^2x}\le\frac1{n^2a},
$$
so the series converges uniformly there and $g$ is continuous. Also
$$
g'(x)=-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n^2}{(1+n^2x)^2}.
$$
On $[a,\infty)$ the derivative terms are bounded by $1/(n^2a^2)$, so the derivative series converges uniformly. Hence $g$ is differentiable on $(0,\infty)$ and $g'$ is continuous there.

::: pf-step

$h_n(x)=x/(1+x)^{n+1}$ tends to $0$ uniformly on $[0,\infty)$, and $\sum_{n\ge0}h_n$ converges uniformly on each $[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$ but not on $[0,\infty)$.

::: pf-proof

For $n\ge1$, differentiating shows the maximum on $[0,\infty)$ occurs at $x=1/n$, with
$$
\|h_n\|_\infty=\frac{n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\le\frac1{n+1}\to0.
$$
Thus $h_n\to0$ uniformly. For $x>0$,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty h_n(x)
=\frac{x}{1+x}\sum_{n=0}^\infty(1+x)^{-n}=1,
$$
while at $x=0$ the sum is $0$. Hence the series cannot converge uniformly on $[0,\infty)$ because its pointwise sum is discontinuous. On $[a,\infty)$ with $a>0$, the tail beginning at $N$ equals $(1+x)^{-N}$, so its supremum is $(1+a)^{-N}\to0$; therefore convergence is uniform there.

::: pf-step

The function $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x>0$, $f(x)=0$ for $x\le0$, is $C^\infty$ with $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n$, and $f(x)f(1-x)$ is a smooth function that is positive on $(0,1)$ and zero elsewhere.

::: pf-proof

Repeated differentiation on $x>0$ gives $f^{(n)}(x)=P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}$ for a polynomial $P_n$. Since $t^m e^{-t^2}\to0$ as $t\to\infty$ for every $m$, all right derivatives tend to $0$ at the origin, matching the left derivatives. Hence $f\in C^\infty$ and $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n$. Its Taylor series at $0$ is therefore identically zero, so it does not equal $f$ for $x>0$. Finally $g(x)=f(x)f(1-x)$ is smooth, nonzero on $(0,1)$, and vanishes outside $(0,1)$.

::: pf-step

A Lipschitz function on an interval is absolutely continuous, and an absolutely continuous $f$ is Lipschitz if and only if $f'\in L^\infty$.

::: pf-proof

If $|f(x)-f(y)|\le C|x-y|$, then for pairwise disjoint intervals $(a_j,b_j)$,
$$
\sum_j|f(b_j)-f(a_j)|\le C\sum_j(b_j-a_j),
$$
so $f$ is absolutely continuous. If $f$ is absolutely continuous, then
$$
f(y)-f(x)=\int_x^y f'(t)\,dt.
$$
Thus $f'\in L^\infty$ implies
$$
|f(y)-f(x)|\le\|f'\|_\infty|y-x|,
$$
so $f$ is Lipschitz. Conversely, a Lipschitz function is differentiable a.e. and $|f'|$ is bounded a.e. by its Lipschitz constant.

::: pf-step

For integrable $f\ge0$ on $[0,1]$, $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f^{1/n}=m(\{f>0\})$.

::: pf-proof

For nonnegative integrable $f$,
$$
f(x)^{1/n}\to\mathbf1_{\{f>0\}}(x)
$$
pointwise. Also $t^{1/n}\le1+t$ for $t\ge0$, so $f^{1/n}\le1+f\in L^1([0,1])$. Dominated convergence yields
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f(x)^{1/n}\,dx
=m(\{x:f(x)>0\}).
$$

:::

::: pf-step

Let $\sigma_n=(s_0+\cdots+s_n)/(n+1)$ be the Cesàro means of $(s_n)$. Convergence of $s_n$ implies convergence of $\sigma_n$ to the same limit; the converse fails, even for $s_n\ge0$; and it holds if $n(s_n-s_{n-1})\to0$.

::: pf-proof

(a) If $s_n\to s$, then $\sigma_n\to s$ by the Cesaro theorem, proved directly by splitting the average into a fixed initial block and a tail where $|s_k-s|$ is small.

(b) Take $s_n=(-1)^n$. Then $s_n$ does not converge, while $\sigma_n\to0$.

(c) Let $s_{2^k}=k$ and $s_n=0$ otherwise. Then $s_n\ge0$ and $\limsup s_n=\infty$, but for $2^m\le n<2^{m+1}$,
$$
0\le\sigma_n\le\frac{1+2+\cdots+m}{2^m}\to0.
$$

(d) With $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}$,
$$
\sum_{k=1}^nka_k
=ns_n-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}s_k
=(n+1)(s_n-\sigma_n),
$$
so
$$
s_n-\sigma_n=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^nka_k.
$$
If $na_n\to0$ and $\sigma_n$ converges, the right side tends to $0$ by Cesaro averaging, hence $s_n$ converges to the same limit as $\sigma_n$.

:::

::: pf-step

For $f\in C([0,1])$, $\lim_{k\to\infty}k\int_0^1x^{k-1}f(x)\,dx=f(1)$.

::: pf-proof

Let $f\in C([0,1])$. Since $\int_0^1kx^{k-1}\,dx=1$,
$$
k\int_0^1x^{k-1}f(x)\,dx-f(1)
=k\int_0^1x^{k-1}(f(x)-f(1))\,dx.
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $a<1$ so that $|f(x)-f(1)|<\varepsilon$ on $[a,1]$. The contribution from $[a,1]$ is at most $\varepsilon$. On $[0,a]$ it is at most $2\|f\|_\infty a^k$. Hence the expression tends to $0$, proving the limit is $f(1)$.

:::

::: pf-step

$f_n(x)=nx(1-x)^n$ converges to $0$ pointwise but not uniformly on $[0,1]$, and $\int_0^1 n(1-x)^n\sin x\,dx\to0$.

::: pf-proof

For each fixed $x\in(0,1]$, exponential decay of $(1-x)^n$ dominates the factor $n$, while $f_n(0)=0$, so $f_n\to0$ pointwise. The maximum occurs at $x=1/(n+1)$ and equals
$$
\frac{n}{n+1}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n\to e^{-1},
$$
so convergence is not uniform. Finally, since $0\le\sin x\le x$ on $[0,1]$,
$$
0\le \int_0^1 n(1-x)^n\sin x\,dx
\le n\int_0^1x(1-x)^n\,dx
=\frac{n}{(n+1)(n+2)}\to0.
$$

:::

::: pf-step

A decreasing summable positive sequence satisfies $nx_n\to0$.

::: pf-proof

Let $(x_n)$ be decreasing, positive, and suppose $\sum_nx_n<\infty$. Then
$$
\sum_{k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}^{n}x_k
\ge \bigl(n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor\bigr)x_n
\ge \frac n2x_n.
$$
The left side is a tail block of a convergent positive series and therefore tends to $0$. Hence $nx_n\to0$.
