::: {.exercise}
(a) What does it mean for a metric space to be **complete**?

(b) Give two or more equivalent definitions for **compactness** in a complete metric space.

(c) What is an interior point?
An isolated point?
A limit point?

(d) What does it mean for a set to be open?
Closed?

(e) What is the **closure** of a subspace $E\subseteq X$?

(f) What does it mean for $E\subseteq X$ to be a **dense** subspace?

(g) What does it mean for a family of sets to form a **basis** for a topology?
What is a basis for the standard topology on $\RR^d$?

(h) Let $X$ be a subset of $\RR^d$.
Prove the Heine-Borel theorem:

1. Show that $X$ compact $\implies X$ is closed.
2. Show that $X$ compact $\implies X$ is bounded.
3. Show that a closed subset of a compact set must be compact.
4. Show that if $X$ closed and bounded $\implies X$ is compact.

(i) Find an example of a metric space with a closed and bounded subspace that is not compact.
How can this be modified to obtain a necessary and sufficient condition?

(j) Determine if the following subsets of $\RR$ are open, closed, both, or neither:

1. $\QQ$
2. $\ZZ$
3. $\theset{1}$
4. $\theset{p \in \ZZ^{\geq 0} \st p\text{ is prime}}$
5. $\theset{ {1\over n} \st n\in \ZZ^{\geq 1}}$
6. $\theset{ {1\over n} \st n\in \ZZ^{\geq 1}} \union \theset{0}$
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space and $E \subseteq X$.

- $(X,d)$ is [[D-G5N6I|complete]] if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point of $X$.

- For a metric space $K$ the following are equivalent: every open cover of $K$ has a finite subcover (the definition of compactness); every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging in $K$; $K$ is complete and totally bounded, that is, for every $\eps > 0$ finitely many $\eps$-balls cover $K$.

- $x$ is an [[D-5VCMJ|interior point]] of $E$ if $B(x,r) \subseteq E$ for some $r > 0$; an [[D-D7AFV|isolated point]] of $E$ if $x \in E$ and $B(x,r) \cap E = \theset{x}$ for some $r > 0$; a [[D-Y6JAS|limit point]] of $E$ if $(B(x,r)\setminus\theset{x}) \cap E \neq \emptyset$ for every $r > 0$.

- $E$ is open if every point of $E$ is an interior point, and [[D-UI7ZL|closed]] if $X \setminus E$ is open, equivalently if $E$ contains all its limit points.

- The [[D-ASXW6|closure]] $\overline E$ is the smallest closed set containing $E$, equivalently $E$ together with its limit points.

- $E$ is [[D-KJBAK|dense]] if $\overline E = X$, equivalently every nonempty open subset of $X$ meets $E$.

- A family $\mathcal B$ of subsets of a set $X$ is a [[D-WKURJ|basis]] for a topology if every point lies in some $B \in \mathcal B$, and for $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal B$ and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ there is $B_3 \in \mathcal B$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$; the topology it generates consists of the unions of members of $\mathcal B$. The open balls $B(x,r)$, $x \in \RR^d$, $r > 0$, form a basis for the standard topology on $\RR^d$, as do the open boxes.

::: pf

::: pf-step

If $K \subseteq \RR^d$ is compact, then $K$ is closed.

::: pf-proof

::: {.pf-step #s1-1}

Fix $x \in \RR^d \setminus K$. For $y \in K$ the balls $U_y = B(y, \abs{x-y}/3)$ and $V_y = B(x, \abs{x-y}/3)$ are disjoint.

::: pf-proof

$\abs{x-y} > 0$, and a common point $z$ would give $\abs{x-y} \le \abs{x-z} + \abs{z-y} < 2\abs{x-y}/3$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #s1-2}

There are $y_1, \ldots, y_k \in K$ with $K \subseteq U_{y_1} \cup \cdots \cup U_{y_k}$.

::: pf-proof

The balls $U_y$, $y \in K$, form an open cover of the compact set $K$.

:::

:::

::: pf-qed

$V = \bigcap_{i=1}^k V_{y_i}$ is an open ball about $x$. A point $z \in V \cap K$ would lie in some $U_{y_i}$ by step [](#s1-2){.pf-ref} and in $V_{y_i}$, contradicting step [](#s1-1){.pf-ref}. So every point of $\RR^d \setminus K$ has a neighborhood disjoint from $K$, and $\RR^d \setminus K$ is open.

:::

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If $K \subseteq \RR^d$ is compact, then $K$ is bounded.

::: pf-proof

The balls $B(0,N)$, $N \geq 1$, cover $K$, so finitely many do, and $K \subseteq B(0,N)$ for the largest such $N$.

:::

:::

::: {.pf-step #closed-in-compact}

A closed subset $C$ of a compact set $K$ is compact.

::: pf-proof

Given an open cover $\mathcal U$ of $C$, the family $\mathcal U \cup \theset{\RR^d \setminus C}$ is an open cover of $K$. A finite subcover of it, with $\RR^d \setminus C$ removed, covers $C$.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

If $X \subseteq \RR^d$ is closed and bounded, then $X$ is compact.

::: pf-proof

$X$ lies in a closed box $Q = [-N,N]^d$. Suppose an open cover $\mathcal U$ of $Q$ has no finite subcover. Bisecting each side gives $2^d$ closed boxes, one of which has no finite subcover; iterating gives nested closed boxes $Q = Q_0 \supseteq Q_1 \supseteq \cdots$ with $\operatorname{diam} Q_k = 2^{-k}\operatorname{diam} Q$, none finitely covered. Their intersection contains a point $x$, by completeness of $\RR$ applied coordinatewise. Some $U \in \mathcal U$ contains $x$ and a ball $B(x,r)$, and $Q_k \subseteq B(x,r)$ once $\operatorname{diam} Q_k < r$, a contradiction. So $Q$ is compact, and $X$ is compact by step [](#closed-in-compact){.pf-ref}.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

$\ZZ$ with the discrete metric $d(m,n) = 1$ for $m \neq n$ is closed and bounded in itself but not compact; a subset of a metric space is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded.

::: pf-proof

$\ZZ$ is closed in itself and has diameter $1$. The balls $B(n, 1/2) = \theset{n}$ form an open cover with no finite subcover. The characterization is the third equivalent form of compactness recalled before the proof.

:::

:::

::: pf-step

$\QQ$ is $\boxed{\text{neither}}$; $\ZZ$, $\theset{1}$ and the primes are $\boxed{\text{closed}}$ and not open; $\theset{1/n \st n \geq 1}$ is $\boxed{\text{neither}}$; $\theset{1/n \st n \geq 1} \cup \theset{0}$ is $\boxed{\text{closed}}$ and not open.

::: pf-proof

A nonempty open subset of $\RR$ contains an interval, and none of these sets does: each is countable, and every interval is uncountable. So none is open. $\QQ$ is not closed because its closure is $\RR$. $\ZZ$ and the primes have complements that are unions of open intervals, and $\theset{1}$ is closed because points are closed. $0$ is a limit point of $\theset{1/n}$ not in the set, so that set is not closed. Adding $0$ gives a closed set: its complement is $(-\infty, 0) \cup (1,\infty) \cup \bigcup_{n\geq1}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}, \frac1n\right)$.

:::
