::: exercise
- What does it mean for a metric space to be **complete**?

- Give two or more equivalently definitions for **compactness** in a complete metric space.

- What is an interior point?
  An isolated point?
  A limit point?

- What does it mean for a set to be open?
  Closed?

- What is the **closure** of a subspace $E\subseteq X$?

- What does it mean for $E\subseteq X$ to be a **dense** subspace?

- What does it mean for a family of sets to form a **basis** for a topology?

  - What is a basis for the standard topology on $\RR^d$?

- Let $X$ be a subset of $\RR^d$.
  Prove the Heine-Borel theorem:

  - Show that $X$ compact $\implies X$ is closed

  - Show that $X$ compact $\implies X$ is bounded

  - Show that a closed subset of a compact set must be bounded.

  - Show that if $X$ closed and bounded $\implies X$ is compact.

- Find an example of a metric space with a closed and bounded subspace that is not compact.

  - How can this be modified to obtain a necessary and sufficient condition?

- Determine if the following subsets of $\RR$ are opened, closed, both, or neither:

  - $\QQ$

  - $\ZZ$

  - $\ts{1}$

  - $\ts{p \in \ZZ^{\geq 0} \st p\text{ is prime}}$

  - $\ts{ {1\over n} \st n\in \ZZ^{\geq 0}}$

  - $\ts{ {1\over n} \st n\in \ZZ^{\geq 0}} \union \ts{0}$
:::

::: {.solution}
<1>1. A metric space $(X,d)$ is complete if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point of $X$.
::: {.proof}
definition.
:::

<1>2. Equivalently definitions for compactness in a complete metric space (indeed in any metric space): <2>1. Every open cover has a finite subcover (the defining property).
::: {.proof}
definition of compactness.
:::
<2>2. Every sequence has a convergent subsequence (sequential compactness); in a metric space this is equivalent to compactness.
::: {.proof}
standard equivalence in metric spaces.
:::
<2>3. $X$ is complete and totally bounded (every $\eps > 0$ admits a finite $\eps$-net); this is the metric-space characterization.
::: {.proof}
compact $\implies$ complete and totally bounded; conversely, in a metric space, complete + totally bounded $\implies$ compact (via sequential compactness and a diagonal argument).
:::

<1>3. Interior, isolated, and limit points of a set $E$ in a metric space: <2>1. $x$ is an interior point of $E$ if some ball $B(x,r) \subseteq E$.
::: {.proof}
definition.
:::
<2>2. $x$ is an isolated point of $E$ if $x \in E$ and some ball $B(x,r)$ contains no point of $E$ other than $x$.
::: {.proof}
definition.
:::
<2>3. $x$ is a limit (accumulation) point of $E$ if every punctured ball $B(x,r)\setminus\{x\}$ meets $E$.
::: {.proof}
definition.
:::

<1>4. Open and closed sets: $E$ is open if every point of $E$ is an interior point; $E$ is closed if its complement is open, equivalently if $E$ contains all its limit points.
::: {.proof}
definitions (the two characterizations of closed are equivalent).
:::

<1>5. The closure $\overline E$ of $E \subseteq X$ is the smallest closed set containing $E$, equivalently $E$ together with all its limit points.
::: {.proof}
definition/equivalence.
:::

<1>6. $E \subseteq X$ is dense if $\overline E = X$, equivalently every nonempty open set meets $E$.
::: {.proof}
definition.
:::

<1>7. A family $\mathcal B$ of subsets of $X$ is a basis for a topology if (i) every point lies in some $B \in \mathcal B$, and (ii) for $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal B$ and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ there is $B_3 \in \mathcal B$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$; the topology is then the collection of unions of basis elements.
<2>1. A basis for the standard topology on $\RR^d$ is the family of open balls $B(x,r)$, $x \in \RR^d$, $r > 0$ (equivalently, open boxes).
::: {.proof}
the standard topology is defined by the Euclidean metric, whose open sets are unions of balls.
:::

<1>8. Heine–Borel in $\RR^d$: if $X \subseteq \RR^d$ is compact, then $X$ is closed.
<2>1. Let $x \notin X$; for each $y \in X$ choose disjoint open balls $U_y \ni y$, $V_y \ni x$.
::: {.proof}
$\RR^d$ is Hausdorff.
:::
<2>2. The cover $\{U_y : y \in X\}$ of the compact set $X$ has a finite subcover $U_{y_1}, \ldots, U_{y_k}$.
::: {.proof}
compactness.
:::
<2>3. Then $V = \bigcap_{i=1}^k V_{y_i}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ disjoint from $X$.
::: {.proof}
$V$ is a finite intersection of open sets, contains $x$, and if $z \in V \cap X$ then $z \in U_{y_i}$ for some $i$, but $U_{y_i} \cap V_{y_i} = \emptyset$ while $z \in V \subseteq V_{y_i}$, contradiction.
:::
<2>4. Hence $X$ is closed.
::: {.proof}
every $x \notin X$ has an open neighborhood avoiding $X$, so $\RR^d \setminus X$ is open.
:::
:::
