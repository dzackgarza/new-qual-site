::: {.exercise}
- Show that if $f\in L^1(\RR)$ and $\hat f = 0$, then $f = 0$ almost everywhere.

- Show that Fourier inversion formula $f(x) = \int_\RR \hat f(\xi) e^{2\pi i x \xi}\, d\xi$ does not necessarily hold pointwise for arbitrary $f \in L^1(\RR)$ (since $\hat f$ need not be in $L^1(\RR)$).
:::

::: {.solution}
**(1) Injectivity.** Let $f \in L^1(\RR)$ with $\hat f(\xi) = \int_\RR f(x) e^{-2\pi i \xi x}\, dx = 0$ for all $\xi \in \RR$.
For $\varepsilon > 0$ let $\Phi_\varepsilon$ be the Gaussian approximate identity
$$
\Phi_\varepsilon(x) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} e^{-\pi (x/\varepsilon)^2}, \qquad \text{with } \widehat{\Phi_\varepsilon}(\xi) = e^{-\pi \varepsilon^2 \xi^2}.
$$
Fourier inversion holds for the Gaussian: $\Phi_\varepsilon(x-y) = \int_\RR \widehat{\Phi_\varepsilon}(\xi) e^{2\pi i (x-y)\xi}\, d\xi$.
Substituting this into the convolution and applying Fubini's theorem, which is justified because $\abs{f(y)}\widehat{\Phi_\varepsilon}(\xi)$ is integrable on $\RR^2$, gives
$$
(f * \Phi_\varepsilon)(x) = \int_\RR f(y) \Phi_\varepsilon(x - y)\, dy = \int_\RR \hat f(\xi) e^{2\pi i x \xi} \widehat{\Phi_\varepsilon}(\xi)\, d\xi.
$$
Since $\hat f(\xi) = 0$ everywhere, the right-hand side is identically $0$, so:
$$
(f * \Phi_\varepsilon)(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in \RR \text{ and all } \varepsilon > 0.
$$
Since $\Phi_\varepsilon$ is an approximate identity on $L^1(\RR)$, as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$:
$$
\|f * \Phi_\varepsilon - f\|_{L^1} \to 0.
$$
Because $f * \Phi_\varepsilon = 0$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$, we have:
$$
\|0 - f\|_{L^1} = \|f\|_{L^1} = 0.
$$
Thus $f(x) = 0$ almost everywhere on $\RR$.

**(2) Failure of pointwise inversion.** The inversion integral $\int_\RR \hat f(\xi) e^{2\pi i x \xi}\, d\xi$ is a Lebesgue integral only when $\hat f \in L^1(\RR)$. For $f \in L^1(\RR)$, $\hat f$ is continuous and vanishes at infinity by the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma, but $\hat f$ need not be in $L^1(\RR)$.

Let $f = \mathbf{1}_{[-1, 1]} \in L^1(\RR)$.
Its Fourier transform is
$$
\hat f(\xi) = \int_{-1}^1 e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx = \frac{e^{2\pi i \xi} - e^{-2\pi i \xi}}{2\pi i \xi} = \frac{\sin(2\pi \xi)}{\pi \xi}.
$$
For $k \geq 1$ and $\xi \in [k/2 + 1/8, k/2 + 3/8]$, $\abs{\sin(2\pi\xi)} \geq 1/\sqrt 2$ and $\abs{\hat f(\xi)} \geq \frac{1}{\sqrt 2\, \pi (k/2+1)}$; each such interval has length $1/4$, so comparison with the harmonic series gives
$$
\int_\RR |\hat f(\xi)|\, d\xi = \int_\RR \left| \frac{\sin(2\pi \xi)}{\pi \xi} \right| d\xi = \infty.
$$
Hence the classical inversion integral $\int_\RR \hat f(\xi) e^{2\pi i x \xi}\, d\xi$ does not exist in the Lebesgue sense for arbitrary $L^1$ functions.
Furthermore, at points of discontinuity (such as $x = \pm 1$), the principal value limit equals $1/2 \neq f(\pm 1) = 1$.
:::
