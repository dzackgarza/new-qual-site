::: problem
Let $f(x) = \frac 1 x$.
Show that $f$ is uniformly continuous on $(1, \infty)$ but not on $(0,\infty)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
> **AI-Generated Solution** (Gemini 3.7 Flash)

**Part 1: $f$ is uniformly continuous on $(1, \infty)$.**

Let $\varepsilon > 0$.
We want to find $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x, y \in (1, \infty)$, if $|x - y| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon$.
For any $x, y \in (1, \infty)$:
$$
|f(x) - f(y)| = \left| \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{y} \right| = \frac{|x - y|}{xy}.
$$
Since $x > 1$ and $y > 1$, we have $xy > 1$, which implies $\frac{1}{xy} < 1$.
Therefore:
$$
|f(x) - f(y)| < |x - y|.
$$
Choosing $\delta = \varepsilon$, whenever $|x - y| < \delta$ we have $|f(x) - f(y)| < |x - y| < \varepsilon$.
Thus $f$ is (Lipschitz continuous and hence) uniformly continuous on $(1, \infty)$.

**Part 2: $f$ is not uniformly continuous on $(0, \infty)$.**

To disprove uniform continuity on $(0, \infty)$, it suffices to produce $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ and two sequences $(x_n), (y_n)$ in $(0, \infty)$ such that $\lim_{n \to \infty} |x_n - y_n| = 0$, but $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \geq \varepsilon_0$ for all $n \geq 1$.

Define:
$$
x_n = \frac{1}{n}, \qquad y_n = \frac{1}{n+1}.
$$
Then $x_n, y_n \in (0, \infty)$ for all $n \geq 1$, and:
$$
|x_n - y_n| = \left| \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} \right| = \frac{1}{n(n+1)} \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.
$$
However:
$$
|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| = |n - (n+1)| = 1.
$$
Taking $\varepsilon_0 = 1$, we see that $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \geq 1$ for all $n \geq 1$.
Therefore, $f$ is not uniformly continuous on $(0, \infty)$.
