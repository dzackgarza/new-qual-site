::: exercise
Show that $[0, 1]$ is compact without using the Heine-Borel theorem.
:::

::: {.solution}
> **AI-Generated Solution** (Gemini 3.7 Flash)

We give a self-contained proof of the compactness of $I = [0, 1]$ using the method of bisection (nested intervals / Bolzano-Weierstrass property) without citing the Heine-Borel theorem.

Let $\mathcal{U} = \{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be an open cover of $[0, 1]$.
Suppose towards a contradiction that $[0, 1]$ cannot be covered by any finite subcollection of $\mathcal{U}$.
We say such an interval is *uncoverable*.

1. **Bisection procedure:**

   - Set $I_0 = [a_0, b_0] = [0, 1]$, which is uncoverable.

   - Bisect $I_0$ into two subintervals of equal length $1/2$: $[0, 1/2]$ and $[1/2, 1]$.
     If both subintervals could be covered by finite subcollections $\mathcal{U}_1$ and $\mathcal{U}_2$ of $\mathcal{U}$, then $\mathcal{U}_1 \cup \mathcal{U}_2$ would be a finite cover of $[0, 1]$, a contradiction.
     Thus, at least one half is uncoverable.
     Let $I_1 = [a_1, b_1]$ be an uncoverable half.

   - Inductively, having constructed an uncoverable interval $I_k = [a_k, b_k]$ of length $2^{-k}$, bisect it into $[a_k, \frac{a_k+b_k}{2}]$ and $[\frac{a_k+b_k}{2}, b_k]$.
     At least one of these two halves cannot be covered by any finite subcollection of $\mathcal{U}$.
     Choose one such half and call it $I_{k+1} = [a_{k+1}, b_{k+1}]$.

2. **Intersection of nested intervals:** This produces a sequence of nested closed intervals:
   $$
   [0, 1] = I_0 \supset I_1 \supset I_2 \supset \cdots \supset I_k \supset \cdots
   $$
   with length $\operatorname{len}(I_k) = 2^{-k} \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$.
   By the Cantor Intersection Theorem (or least-upper-bound property of $\RR$), the intersection of these nested non-empty closed bounded intervals contains a unique point:
   $$
   \bigcap_{k=0}^\infty I_k = \{x_0\} \quad \text{for some } x_0 \in [0, 1].
   $$

3. **Reaching a contradiction:** Since $\mathcal{U}$ covers $[0, 1]$, there is some open set $U_{\alpha_0} \in \mathcal{U}$ containing $x_0$.
   Since $U_{\alpha_0}$ is open, there exists an open ball (interval) $B(x_0, \varepsilon) = (x_0 - \varepsilon, x_0 + \varepsilon)$ such that:
   $$
   B(x_0, \varepsilon) \cap [0, 1] \subseteq U_{\alpha_0}.
   $$
   Choose $k \in \mathbb{N}$ large enough so that $2^{-k} < \varepsilon$.
   Since $x_0 \in I_k$ and $\operatorname{len}(I_k) = 2^{-k} < \varepsilon$, for any point $y \in I_k$:
   $$
   |y - x_0| \leq \operatorname{len}(I_k) = 2^{-k} < \varepsilon.
   $$
   Thus $I_k \subseteq B(x_0, \varepsilon) \cap [0, 1] \subseteq U_{\alpha_0}$.

   This means that $I_k$ is covered by a **single** set $U_{\alpha_0} \in \mathcal{U}$.
   This directly contradicts the fact that $I_k$ is uncoverable by any finite subcollection of $\mathcal{U}$.

Therefore, $[0, 1]$ must admit a finite subcover from $\mathcal{U}$, proving that $[0, 1]$ is compact.
