**Goal:** Prove that the alternating group $A_n$ is simple for all $n \ge 5$.

<1>1. Key Structural Lemmas: <2>1. $A_n$ is generated by the set of all $3$-cycles for all $n \ge 3$.
Proof: Every permutation in $A_n$ is a product of an even number of transpositions.
Any product of two transpositions $(a\,b)(c\,d)$ factors as a product of one or two $3$-cycles: $(a\,b)(b\,c) = (a\,b\,c)$ when they share a point, and $(a\,b)(c\,d) = (a\,b\,c)(b\,c\,d)$ when disjoint.
<2>2. For $n \ge 5$, all $3$-cycles are conjugate in $A_n$.
Proof: In $S_n$, any two $3$-cycles $(a\,b\,c)$ and $(u\,v\,w)$ are conjugated by some $\sigma \in S_n$.
Since $n \ge 5$, there exist two distinct points $x, y \notin \{a, b, c\}$.
If $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = -1$, replacing $\sigma$ with $\sigma \circ (x\,y) \in A_n$ conjugates $(a\,b\,c)$ to $(u\,v\,w)$.
<2>3. If a normal subgroup $N \trianglelefteq A_n$ ($n \ge 5$) contains a $3$-cycle, then $N = A_n$.
Proof: Normality implies $N$ contains all conjugates of that $3$-cycle in $A_n$.
By <1>1.<2>2, $N$ contains all $3$-cycles, and by <1>1.<2>1, these generate $A_n$, so $N = A_n$.

<1>2. Base Case: $A_5$ is simple.
<2>1. $|A_5| = 60$.
The conjugacy classes of $A_5$ have sizes $1$ (identity), $15$ (products of two disjoint transpositions), $20$ ($3$-cycles), and two classes of $5$-cycles each of size $12$.
Proof: In $S_5$, the class sizes are $1, 15, 20, 24$.
Since centralizers of $5$-cycles have order $5$ (odd), the $5$-cycles split into two $A_5$-classes of size $60/5 = 12$.
The other classes do not split.
<2>2. Any normal subgroup $N \trianglelefteq A_5$ is a union of conjugacy classes containing the identity, so $|N| = 1 + \sum k_i$ where $k_i \in \{12, 12, 15, 20\}$, and $|N|$ must divide $60$.
Proof: Lagrange's Theorem and the definition of normal subgroups as unions of conjugacy classes.
<2>3. No proper non-empty subset sum of $\{12, 12, 15, 20\}$ plus $1$ divides $60$.
Proof: Exhaustive check of sums: $1+12=13$, $1+15=16$, $1+20=21$, $1+24=25$, $1+27=28$, $1+32=33$, $1+35=36$, $1+39=40$, $1+44=45$, $1+47=48$, none of which divides $60$.
<2>4. Thus the only normal subgroups of $A_5$ are $\{e\}$ and $A_5$, so $A_5$ is simple.
Proof: Follows from <2>2 and <2>3.

<1>3. Induction Step: If $A_{n-1}$ is simple for $n \ge 6$, then $A_n$ is simple.
<2>1. Assume $n \ge 6$ and $A_{n-1}$ is simple.
Let $N \trianglelefteq A_n$ with $N \neq \{e\}$.
Proof: Inductive hypothesis and setting.
<2>2. For each $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, let $H_i = \{\sigma \in A_n \mid \sigma(i) = i\} \cong A_{n-1}$ be the point stabilizer.
Proof: Stabilizer of a single point in $A_n$ is naturally isomorphic to $A_{n-1}$.
<2>3. $N \cap H_i \trianglelefteq H_i$ for each $i$.
Proof: Intersection of a normal subgroup with any subgroup is normal in that subgroup.
<2>4. Since $H_i \cong A_{n-1}$ is simple, either $N \cap H_i = \{e\}$ or $N \cap H_i = H_i$.
Proof: Simple groups have no non-trivial proper normal subgroups.
<2>5. If $N \cap H_i = H_i$, then $H_i \subseteq N$, so $N$ contains a $3$-cycle (since $n-1 \ge 5 \ge 3$), which implies $N = A_n$ by <1>1.<2>3. Proof: Point stabilizers contain $3$-cycles.
<2>6. It is impossible that $N \cap H_i = \{e\}$ for all $i$.
<3>1. Choose $\sigma \in N$ with $\sigma \neq e$.
Proof: $N$ is non-trivial.
<3>2. If $\sigma$ fixes any element $j$, then $\sigma \in N \cap H_j$, so $N \cap H_j \neq \{e\}$.
Proof: Direct definition of point stabilizer.
<3>3. If $\sigma$ moves every element, consider its cycle decomposition.
If $\sigma$ contains a cycle of length $\ge 3$, say $\sigma = (a_1\,a_2\,a_3\dots)\dots$, let $\tau = (a_3\,a_4\,a_5) \in A_n$ (valid since $n \ge 6$). Then $\rho = \sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma\tau^{-1} \in N$.
Direct evaluation gives $\rho(a_1) = a_1$ and $\rho(a_3) \neq a_3$, so $\rho \in N \cap H_{a_1}$ with $\rho \neq e$.
If $\sigma$ is a product of disjoint $2$-cycles, say $\sigma = (a_1\,a_2)(a_3\,a_4)\dots$, let $\tau = (a_1\,a_2)(a_3\,a_5) \in A_n$.
Then $\rho = \sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma\tau^{-1} \in N$ satisfies $\rho(a_1) = a_1$ and $\rho(a_3) = a_4 \neq a_3$, so $\rho \in N \cap H_{a_1}$ with $\rho \neq e$.
Proof: Explicit computation of commutators in $N$.
<3>4. Q.E.D. Proof: In every case, there exists $i$ such that $N \cap H_i \neq \{e\}$.
<2>7. Therefore, $N = A_n$.
Proof: By <2>4, <2>5, and <2>6.

<1>4. Conclusion: $A_n$ is simple for all $n \ge 5$.
Proof: By induction on $n \ge 5$, combining <1>2 and <1>3.
